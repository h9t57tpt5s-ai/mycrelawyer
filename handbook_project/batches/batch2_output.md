### Georgia — Landlord-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** Georgia imposes no default statutory pre-suit notice requirement for a commercial dispossessory action; notice obligations exist only to the extent the lease itself requires them. A dispossessory affidavit/demand for possession under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-50 effectively doubles as the landlord's demand and the trigger for the summary proceeding — the landlord may demand possession and, if the tenant refuses to vacate or pay, immediately file. Where the lease is silent, no separate advance notice to vacate or to cure is statutorily mandated before filing.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** Georgia law does not categorically prohibit self-help for commercial tenancies, but it is not available as a default remedy either — a landlord may lawfully change locks or otherwise exclude a defaulting commercial tenant only where the lease expressly authorizes self-help and the exclusion can be accomplished without a breach of the peace. Georgia courts have not clearly defined "breach of the peace" in this context, which creates real exposure even where the lease authorizes the remedy, and most commercial landlords and their counsel favor the judicial dispossessory process specifically because a completed dispossessory judgment forecloses later wrongful-eviction and trespass claims.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** The landlord may combine a claim for possession with a claim for rent and other damages in the same dispossessory proceeding, filed in the magistrate, state, or civil court (as applicable) of the county where the property is located. O.C.G.A. §§ 44-7-49, 44-7-55(a).

**Timing.** After the dispossessory affidavit is filed and served, the tenant has seven days to answer (orally or in writing), with the deadline extended to the next non-weekend/non-holiday day if the seventh day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday. O.C.G.A. § 44-7-51. If the tenant fails to answer, the court will issue a writ of possession without a hearing; if the tenant answers, the matter proceeds to trial. Georgia law also affords a tenant a statutory defense — full payment of rent owed plus dispossessory costs within the seven-day answer period bars the eviction once in any twelve-month period — but the lease may expressly override this defense, and commercial leases commonly do. O.C.G.A. § 44-7-52. Once judgment issues, the writ of possession becomes executable after seven days, and the landlord must apply for execution within thirty days, directing the sheriff to remove the tenant and its property. O.C.G.A. §§ 44-7-53, 44-7-55.

**Damages.** Rent acceleration clauses are enforceable in Georgia commercial leases if expressly provided for, though a successful dispossessory judgment ordinarily terminates the lease and the tenant's obligation for future rent unless the lease preserves that obligation. Georgia imposes no general statutory duty on a commercial landlord to mitigate damages following a tenant default.

**Special Considerations.** Georgia enacted the "Safe at Home Act" (H.B. 404) in 2024, adding a pre-filing cure notice and other tenant protections to O.C.G.A. Chapter 44-7 — but those provisions apply only to residential lease agreements entered into or renewed on or after July 1, 2024, and do not extend the cure-notice requirement to commercial tenancies. Commercial landlords and counsel should not assume the residential cure-notice reform changes the commercial dispossessory timeline described above.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express, unambiguous self-help/re-entry provision if the parties intend to preserve that remedy, paired with a "no breach of the peace" limitation acknowledgment.
- Express waiver of the twelve-month statutory cure defense under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-52.
- Express contractual notice-and-cure periods, since none apply by default.
- Rent acceleration clause stated expressly, with survival-of-damages language after repossession.
- Express disclaimer of any duty to mitigate, to avoid ambiguity given the absence of a statutory mitigation duty.
- Attorney's fees provision (Georgia does not bar commercial fee-shifting clauses the way it restricts them in residential URLTA-style statutes elsewhere).
- Reservation of the right to seek both possession and damages in the same dispossessory action.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: O.C.G.A. §§ 44-7-49, 44-7-50, 44-7-51, 44-7-52, 44-7-53, 44-7-55(a) (dispossessory proceedings, Article 3); O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14 (tort liability of landlord, reviewed and found inapplicable to self-help authority); H.B. 404 ("Safe at Home Act"), effective July 1, 2024, reviewed and confirmed residential-only in application; 2024 Ga. Laws 484, amending O.C.G.A. § 44-7-55, reviewed and confirmed as addressing writ-of-possession/personal-property mechanics rather than the commercial notice or timing framework described above.

### Hawaii — Landlord-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** A landlord seeking to recover possession following termination or forfeiture of a commercial lease must give the tenant a written notice to quit of at least ten days before restoring possession. Haw. Rev. Stat. § 666-1. No 2023–2026 amendment to this notice period was identified.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** Self-help is not available by default, but Hawaii permits a landlord to exercise self-help to regain possession where the lease expressly grants that right. Outside an express lease grant, a landlord must use the summary possession process, and a wrongful, non-court-ordered exclusion overnight can expose the landlord to a statutory penalty of two months' rent (or two months' free occupancy) plus costs and attorney's fees.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** An action for summary possession must be brought in the district court of the circuit where the premises are located, and a landlord may join claims for rent and damages with the possession claim. Haw. Rev. Stat. §§ 666-6, 666-7.

**Timing.** A commercial summary possession proceeding can resolve in as little as five to ten days after service if uncontested, but can extend to four to six weeks or longer where a trial is required.

**Damages.** A rent acceleration clause is enforceable only if expressly stated in the lease. Hawaii imposes a duty on the landlord to mitigate damages, but the burden of proving a failure to mitigate rests on the tenant. Marco Kona Warehouse v. Sharmilo, Inc., 7 Haw. App. 383, 768 P.2d 247 (1989). Rent a tenant continues to pay during the pendency of a successful eviction proceeding is treated as damages for wrongful withholding of the premises rather than as ordinary rent. Haw. Rev. Stat. § 666-5.

**Special Considerations.** Where a commercial tenant creates a statutory "nuisance," the landlord may serve a written twenty-four-hour notice to abate; if the tenant fails to comply, the landlord may then terminate the tenancy on five days' written notice following that second notice. Haw. Rev. Stat. § 666-3.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express self-help/re-entry authorization if the parties intend that remedy to be available.
- Express rent acceleration clause, since none is implied.
- Clear mitigation and reletting language, given the tenant-favorable burden-of-proof rule on mitigation.
- Provisions addressing treatment of rent accepted during a pending eviction action.
- Nuisance-cure procedures mirrored or modified from the twenty-four-hour/five-day statutory sequence if the parties want a different process.
- Attorney's fees and cost-shifting provisions, since these are not supplied by statute for commercial leases.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: Haw. Rev. Stat. §§ 666-1, 666-3, 666-5, 666-6, 666-7 (Title 36, Chapter 666, Landlord and Tenant / Summary Possession); Marco Kona Warehouse v. Sharmilo, Inc., 7 Haw. App. 383, 768 P.2d 247 (1989). No material 2023–2026 amendment to Chapter 666's commercial summary-possession provisions was located; 2026 legislative activity affecting Chapter 666 (e.g., H.B. 463's eviction-record disassociation provisions) is expressly limited to residential tenants and is not addressed here.

### Idaho — Neutral

**Statutory Notice.** After a commercial tenant defaults, the landlord must give three days' written notice before proceeding with an unlawful detainer action. For nonpayment, the notice must state the amount due and demand payment or possession; for breach of other lease conditions, it must demand performance or possession. The notice must also inform a commercial tenant that, if the court enters judgment for the landlord, the tenant has seven days (or longer if the court grants an extension) to remove its belongings before the landlord may remove and dispose of them — longer than the seventy-two hours given to residential tenants. Idaho Code § 6-303.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** ⚠ Sources diverge on this point. The 2022 baseline characterized Idaho as having no statute expressly forbidding self-help. Current secondary authority, including the Idaho Legal Services Authority and practitioner commentary, describes self-help eviction — changing locks, shutting off utilities, or removing a tenant's property outside the unlawful detainer process — as unlawful in Idaho for landlords generally, and Idaho's forcible entry statute independently penalizes retaking possession by force after a tenant is in possession. Commercial landlords should not rely on lease language purporting to authorize self-help without confirming current guidance with Idaho counsel, and should expect that only the judicial unlawful detainer process reliably avoids liability.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** The landlord may combine an action for possession with a claim for damages, and the district court of the county where the property is located has jurisdiction. Idaho Code §§ 6-305, 6-311E.

**Timing.** After the complaint is filed, the court must schedule trial within twelve days of filing; the summons, complaint, and notice of trial setting must be served on the defendant at least five days before trial. Idaho Code § 6-310. Following judgment, the commercial tenant has seven days (or a court-extended period) to remove belongings.

**Damages.** Idaho's treatment of accelerated-rent clauses remains unsettled: such clauses may be collected but are not treated as enforceable liquidated-damages provisions as such, and Idaho courts examine whether the future damages sought are disproportionate to actual damages. The landlord has a duty to mitigate damages.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Do not rely on a lease self-help clause without current Idaho counsel confirmation given the unsettled/adverse secondary authority on self-help legality.
- Draft accelerated-rent provisions as a genuine pre-estimate of loss, anticipating a disproportionality challenge.
- Express mitigation/reletting procedures, since a duty to mitigate applies.
- Contractual notice periods longer than the three-day statutory minimum if the parties prefer more cure time.
- Personal-property removal and storage procedures consistent with the seven-day statutory removal window.
- Attorney's fees and cost provisions, since none are supplied by statute.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: Idaho Code §§ 6-303, 6-305, 6-310, 6-311E (Title 6, Chapter 3, Forcible Entry and Unlawful Detainer). No 2023–2026 legislative amendment to these commercial provisions was identified in this research.

### Illinois — Tenant-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** Before filing, an Illinois landlord must make a formal demand for payment of past-due rent (or cure of the default at issue) under the state's Eviction Article (formerly styled "Forcible Entry and Detainer," renamed by P.A. 100-173). 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-209. The landlord must give at least five days' written notice before acting on nonpayment of rent, and at least ten days' notice for all other grounds. The nonpayment notice must include statutorily prescribed language that only full payment of the amount demanded waives the landlord's termination right, unless the landlord agrees in writing to accept partial payment and continue the lease. 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-209.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** Self-help is not available in Illinois; the Eviction Article, 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-101 et seq., provides the exclusive judicial procedure for regaining possession, and it applies to commercial as well as residential tenancies.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** The eviction action must be filed in the circuit court of the county where the property is located, and the landlord may join a claim for rent with the claim for possession. 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-209.

**Timing.** Timing varies materially by circuit/county docket; resolution typically takes roughly three to four months.

**Damages.** Illinois imposes a statutory duty on the landlord to take reasonable measures to mitigate damages recoverable against a defaulting tenant. 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-213.1.

**Special Considerations.** A landlord should be cautious about accepting partial rent payment, since doing so (outside the statutory safe harbor described above) can be construed as an unintentional waiver of the default and may preclude eviction on that notice. Similarly, accepting keys back from a tenant before a court has entered an eviction order can be construed as a voluntary surrender of the lease and may jeopardize the landlord's ability to collect future rent.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express non-waiver language addressing partial-payment acceptance, tracking but reinforcing the statutory safe harbor.
- Express procedures preventing return of keys/possession from being construed as surrender before judgment.
- Mitigation and reletting provisions consistent with the statutory duty to mitigate.
- Realistic timeline expectations (three to four months) built into any default remedies or guaranty triggers.
- Attorney's fees and cost-shifting provisions.
- Acceleration-of-rent language, since Illinois eviction law does not supply one by default.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: 735 Ill. Comp. Stat. 5/9-209, 5/9-211, 5/9-213.1 (Code of Civil Procedure, Article IX, "Eviction," renamed from "Forcible Entry and Detainer" by P.A. 100-173, effective January 1, 2018). No material 2023–2026 amendment specific to the commercial notice, self-help, or mitigation provisions discussed above was identified; a 2025 amendment barring minors from being named as eviction defendants was reviewed and is not commercially significant.

### Indiana — Tenant-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** The parties may contractually waive statutory notice. Absent a waiver, a landlord terminating a tenancy and bringing an action for ejectment must give the tenant no less than ten days' notice. Ind. Code § 32-31-1-6. Notice is not required in several defined circumstances, including a fixed-term tenancy that has run its course, a tenancy at will where waste is committed, a tenancy at sufferance, or a lease requiring advance rent payment that the tenant fails to make. Ind. Code § 32-31-1-8.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** Indiana does not recognize a general or absolute right of landlord self-help; the more consistently supported basis for self-help is where the tenant has abandoned the premises, and commercial parties sometimes attempt to reserve a contractual self-help right, but Indiana courts and regulators generally treat non-judicial lockouts, utility shutoffs, and similar exclusionary conduct as improper outside abandonment, exposing a landlord who acts unilaterally to liability.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** Indiana commercial possession actions are generally brought as ejectment/possession actions under Ind. Code art. 32-30, and in most counties proceed as a two-step process: an initial hearing (typically set two to three weeks after filing) determines only who is entitled to possession, with a separate, later hearing (often roughly two months out) to determine damages owed. After judgment, the landlord may pursue proceedings supplemental to collect.

**Timing.** Possession is generally determined in under a month; damages determinations often take three to four months, varying by county docket.

**Damages.** A rent acceleration clause is enforceable if expressly stated in the lease. As a general matter, eviction terminates the lease and future rent liability unless the lease preserves it. Where a tenant abandons the premises, the landlord has a duty to make reasonable efforts to relet and mitigate damages.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express notice-waiver language if the parties intend to bypass the ten-day statutory notice.
- Express, narrowly tailored self-help provision limited to abandonment scenarios, given the uncertainty around self-help outside that context.
- Express acceleration-of-rent and survival-of-damages provisions.
- Contractual timeline/damages-hearing coordination language, given the bifurcated possession/damages process.
- Mitigation and reletting cooperation provisions.
- Proceedings-supplemental/judgment-collection considerations built into guaranty or security provisions.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: Ind. Code §§ 32-31-1-6, 32-31-1-8 (notice); Ind. Code art. 32-30, ch. 3 (ejectment and recovery of possession of real estate). No 2023–2026 legislative amendment materially affecting these commercial provisions was identified.

### Iowa — Landlord-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** Before a forcible entry and detainer action may be brought, the landlord must give the tenant three days' written notice to quit. Iowa Code § 648.3.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** ⚠ No Iowa statute expressly authorizes or forbids commercial self-help, but current secondary guidance (including Iowa Legal Aid) treats non-judicial lockouts, utility shutoffs, or exclusion of a tenant without notice and a court process as improper, and describes such self-help as exposing a landlord to suit for damages and repossession. Commercial landlords should not assume a lease self-help clause is safely enforceable without confirming current treatment with Iowa counsel.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** A forcible entry and detainer action must be brought in the county where the property is located. Iowa Code § 648.5. Forcible entry and detainer claims may not be joined with other claims except a claim for rent, and where a damages claim is joined it proceeds under a separate case number and timeline from the possession claim. Iowa Code § 648.19.

**Timing.** Once the petition is filed, the court must set the hearing no later than eight days after filing, or no later than fifteen days after filing with the plaintiff's consent. Iowa Code § 648.5.

**Damages.** There is no expedited statutory remedy to recover delinquent rent apart from the possession action. A rent acceleration clause is enforceable only if expressly stated in the lease. The landlord's duty to mitigate applies only where the tenant has abandoned the premises.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Do not rely on an unqualified self-help clause without confirming current Iowa treatment; consider limiting any self-help right to post-abandonment scenarios.
- Express acceleration-of-rent clause, since none is implied.
- Coordinate any damages claim strategy with the separate case-number/timeline requirement for joined rent claims.
- Express mitigation obligations if the parties want mitigation to apply beyond the abandonment context.
- Contractual notice periods longer than three days if additional cure time is desired.
- Attorney's fees provisions, since none are supplied by statute.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: Iowa Code §§ 648.3, 648.5, 648.19 (Forcible Entry and Detainer, Title XV, Chapter 648). No 2023–2026 amendment materially affecting these commercial provisions was identified; a 2024 amendment to Iowa Code § 648.22 (H.F. 548, effective July 1, 2024) addressing court-record aggregation and reporting for forcible entry and detainer actions was reviewed and found to be procedural/record-keeping in nature rather than substantively affecting commercial notice, self-help, jurisdiction, timing, or damages.

### Kansas — Neutral

**Statutory Notice.** A Kansas landlord must give the tenant three days' notice to leave the premises for which possession is sought before filing an eviction lawsuit. Kan. Stat. Ann. § 61-3803. The three-day period is computed as three consecutive 24-hour periods beginning when the notice is delivered, posted, or mailed; if mailed, an additional two days is added; and Saturdays, Sundays, legal holidays, and days the clerk's office is closed do not count toward the period. Id.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** There is no right to use self-help in Kansas. Kan. Stat. Ann. § 61-3804.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** A landlord may bring an action for rent due together with the possession action, or may pursue a separate rent action for the amount due. A judgment in the eviction action does not bar a subsequent lawsuit by either party for claims not included in that judgment. Kan. Stat. Ann. §§ 61-3802, 61-3804.

**Timing.** The defendant must appear on the date in the summons, which must be no less than three and no more than fourteen days after the summons issues. If trial is necessary, it must occur within fourteen days after the appearance date in the summons. Kan. Stat. Ann. §§ 61-3805, 61-3806, 61-3807.

**Damages.** Acceleration-of-rent clauses are likely enforceable in Kansas, though some case law cuts against enforceability. A landlord may seek rent due together with the possession claim. Kan. Stat. Ann. § 61-3804.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express rent acceleration clause, given the mixed case law on enforceability absent express lease language.
- No reliance on self-help; build default remedies around the judicial eviction timeline.
- Careful computation of the three-day notice period (24-hour blocks, mail add-on, and excluded days) built into any contractual notice mechanics.
- Consider whether to pursue rent and possession jointly or reserve the rent claim separately, given the non-bar rule for unincluded claims.
- Attorney's fees provisions, since none are supplied by statute.
- Contractual notice periods longer than three days if desired.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: Kan. Stat. Ann. §§ 61-3802, 61-3803, 61-3804, 61-3805, 61-3806, 61-3807 (Chapter 61, Article 38, Evictions). No 2023–2026 amendment materially affecting these commercial provisions was identified.

### Kentucky — Landlord-Friendly

**Statutory Notice.** ⚠ Correction to the 2022 baseline: the fixed seven-day (nonpayment) and fourteen-day (other breach) notice periods found in KRS § 383.660 are part of Kentucky's Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, KRS §§ 383.500–383.715, which by its terms governs only residential dwelling units and applies only in the specific counties and cities that have formally adopted it (e.g., Louisville-Jefferson County, Lexington-Fayette County, and a handful of others) — it does not govern commercial tenancies anywhere in the Commonwealth. For a commercial tenancy, notice to terminate for default or holdover is governed by the lease and Kentucky common law rather than a fixed statutory period; the forcible detainer action itself, applicable to commercial and residential tenancies alike, is defined and governed by KRS § 383.200 et seq.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** ⚠ Correction to the 2022 baseline: the self-help prohibition of KRS § 383.655 is also a URLTA (residential-only) provision and does not, by its terms, bar self-help in a commercial tenancy. Secondary authority identifies Kentucky as among the states with no statute or reported decision squarely prohibiting commercial self-help, meaning a common-law self-help remedy may remain available where the lease reserves it — but Kentucky courts, like courts generally, tend to disfavor a landlord's use of self-help because it forces a tenant out before the tenant can litigate its right to remain in possession, so counsel should weigh this risk carefully even where no statute forecloses the remedy.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** Forcible detainer actions are heard in Kentucky district court, with appeal to circuit court. KRS § 383.200 et seq.

**Timing.** After judgment, the tenant is ordered to vacate within seven days, and either party may appeal to circuit court within seven days of the judgment's entry. KRS §§ 383.240, 383.255.

**Damages.** ⚠ Because the treble-holdover-damages remedy (KRS § 383.695), the attorney's-fee-clause prohibition (KRS § 383.570), and the related damages provisions cited in the 2022 baseline are also URLTA (residential-only) provisions, they do not apply to a commercial tenancy. Commercial rent acceleration, mitigation, and attorney's-fee-shifting are instead governed by the lease and Kentucky common contract-law principles; this research could not independently verify Kentucky-specific case law resolving the enforceability of a commercial acceleration clause against a penalty/liquidated-damages challenge or confirming the scope of any mitigation duty. Confirm current commercial-damages case law with Kentucky counsel before relying on a specific acceleration or mitigation position.

**Special Considerations.** Because the residential URLTA framework does not apply to commercial tenancies, a commercial lease is the primary — and often only — source of the parties' notice, cure, self-help, and damages rights in Kentucky; unlike many other states, there is little statutory backstop protecting a commercial tenant absent express lease terms.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express notice-and-cure provisions, since no fixed statutory notice period applies to commercial leases.
- Express, carefully limited self-help/re-entry clause if the parties want to preserve that remedy, paired with breach-of-the-peace and risk-allocation language.
- Express rent acceleration clause with a genuine pre-estimate-of-loss rationale, anticipating a possible penalty challenge.
- Express duty-to-mitigate (or disclaimer of mitigation) language, since the statutory mitigation duty does not extend to commercial tenancies.
- Express attorney's-fee-shifting clause, since the URLTA fee-clause prohibition does not apply to commercial leases.
- Contractual timeline coordination given the compressed seven-day post-judgment vacate and seven-day appeal windows.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: KRS §§ 383.200, 383.210, 383.240, 383.255 (Chapter 383, forcible entry and detainer, applicable to all tenancies); KRS §§ 383.500, 383.570, 383.655, 383.660, 383.695, 383.705 (Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, confirmed via multiple secondary sources — including Kentucky Legal Services Authority and University of Kentucky College of Law commentary — to apply only to residential dwelling units in adopting localities). ⚠ Commercial-specific case law on acceleration-clause enforceability and mitigation could not be independently verified in this research; confirm with local counsel.

### Louisiana — Neutral

**Statutory Notice.** When a lessee's right of occupancy has ceased — whether by expiration of the lease term, action of the lessor, nonpayment of rent, or otherwise — and the lessor wishes to obtain possession, the lessor must deliver written notice to vacate, giving the lessee not less than five days from delivery to vacate. La. Code Civ. Proc. art. 4701. A lessee may waive this notice by written waiver in the lease, in which case the lessor may institute eviction proceedings immediately upon termination of the right of occupancy. Id.

**Self-Help / Commercial Lockout.** No self-help remedy is available in Louisiana. Commercial landlords must repossess leased premises only through a court order authorizing eviction, executed by a government official such as a representative of the local sheriff's office; the landlord may not carry out the dispossession itself.

**Jurisdiction and Venue.** The landlord must file separate actions for possession and for damages. The possession action must be filed in the parish court, city court, or justice of the peace court (as applicable) where the property is located. Effective August 1, 2024, La. Code Civ. Proc. art. 4844, as amended by Acts 2024, No. 129, gives parish and city courts jurisdiction concurrent with the district courts over eviction suits — including suits to evict occupants or tenants of leased commercial premises — without regard to the dollar amount of rent or the value of the right of occupancy involved, removing the jurisdictional dollar thresholds that previously constrained parish/city court jurisdiction in higher-rent commercial matters.

**Timing.** An eviction proceeding is typically completed within thirty to sixty days. The eviction hearing may not be held earlier than three days after service of the eviction suit, though the actual date depends on the court's docket.

**Damages.** A rent acceleration clause must be expressly provided for in the lease to be enforceable. A landlord may not continue collecting rent after the lease has been terminated.

**Special Considerations.** There is no right to a jury trial on eviction matters in Louisiana.

**2026 Update Note.** Effective August 1, 2024, Acts 2024, No. 129 amended and re-enacted La. Code Civ. Proc. art. 4844 to give parish and city courts jurisdiction concurrent with district courts over eviction proceedings — including commercial-lease and farmland evictions — regardless of the daily, monthly, or yearly rental amount or the annual value of the right of occupancy, eliminating the prior dollar-based jurisdictional caps for those courts in commercial matters.

**Key Commercial Lease Drafting Considerations.**
- Express written waiver of the five-day notice-to-vacate requirement if immediate suit upon default is desired.
- Express rent acceleration clause, since none is implied by law.
- Post-termination rent-collection limitations built into any holdover or damages provisions, given the bar on continuing to collect rent after termination.
- Venue selection clause anticipating concurrent parish/city and district court jurisdiction post-2024 amendment.
- Provisions addressing the mandatory separate-action structure for possession versus damages claims.
- Jury-trial-waiver acknowledgment, consistent with the statutory bar on jury trials in eviction matters.

**Source Notes.** Statutory authorities reviewed: La. Code Civ. Proc. arts. 4701, 4702, 4732 (notice to vacate, timing), and 4844 (jurisdiction); Acts 2024, No. 129, amending La. Code Civ. Proc. art. 4844, effective August 1, 2024.
