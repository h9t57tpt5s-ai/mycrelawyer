# NJ Bankruptcy Judge Dismisses Developer's Chapter 11, Citing Harm to Preferred-Equity Investor

### A single-asset Chapter 11 filed six months after a Delaware Chancery Court loss draws a "for cause" dismissal — a reminder that bankruptcy is not a reliable escape hatch from a lost fight over LLC control.

A New Jersey bankruptcy judge on September 28, 2026 dismissed the Chapter 11 case of Brass Works Urban Renewal Company, LLC, the sponsor behind a warehouse-to-residential loft conversion on Paterson Plank Road in Jersey City, finding that the creditor who moved to dismiss stood to be harmed more by the case continuing than the debtor would be by having it tossed, according to Law360. The ruling closes out, at least for now, a Chapter 11 case that Brass Works filed on May 22, 2026 in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of New Jersey (Case No. 2:26-bk-15809), listing assets of $50 million to $100 million against liabilities of $10 million to $50 million.

The dismissal is notable less for its dollar figures than for its timing and posture. Brass Works did not arrive in bankruptcy court as a company blindsided by a single creditor's collection suit. It arrived roughly six months after losing a bruising fight in the Delaware Court of Chancery over control of the very entity that owns the Jersey City property. In a post-trial opinion issued November 21, 2025 in ASVRF Paterson Plank RD J-C, LLC v. Brass Works Urban Renewal Company, LLC, the Chancery Court found that ASVRF Paterson Plank RD J-C, LLC — a preferred-equity investor that had put $18 million into the LLC formed to develop the property, in exchange for a preferred monthly distribution — had validly exercised a contractual remedy to remove and replace Brass Works as manager after Brass Works defaulted on its payment obligations. The court held that Brass Works' refusal to cede control breached the operating agreement.

Law360's report on the September 28 dismissal does not identify by name the creditor whose objection persuaded the bankruptcy judge to dismiss "for cause." But the timeline is hard to read any other way: a real estate sponsor that has just been ordered out of the manager's seat by a Chancery Court, after defaulting on a preferred-equity investor's distributions, filing a single-asset Chapter 11 case less than half a year later is a fact pattern bankruptcy judges see often enough to have developed a healthy skepticism toward it. Courts applying 11 U.S.C. § 1112(b) routinely dismiss "for cause" where a Chapter 11 case appears aimed less at reorganizing a viable business than at using the automatic stay to relitigate, or simply delay enforcement of, a state-law ownership or control dispute the debtor has already lost. The balancing test the report describes — weighing harm to the objecting creditor against harm to the debtor — is the standard vehicle for that skepticism.

For commercial real estate sponsors, guarantors and their lenders and equity partners, the case is a useful data point on where that line tends to fall. A Chapter 11 filing automatically stays state-court and Chancery Court enforcement efforts the moment it is filed, which makes it an attractive tool for a sponsor facing imminent loss of control over a single-asset property vehicle. But that same single-asset character — one property, a thin creditor body, and a control dispute that has often already been fully litigated elsewhere — is precisely what makes these cases vulnerable to a quick "for cause" dismissal motion. Preferred-equity and mezzanine investors who have already won a removal or foreclosure remedy in a separate forum should not assume a debtor's bankruptcy filing meaningfully changes their position; it may only add a procedural detour before the underlying remedy is enforced.

The case also underscores the layered capital structures common in New Jersey urban renewal entity (URE) deals, where redevelopment agreements, PILOT tax arrangements and preferred-equity joint ventures combine to create multiple, often disconnected, forums in which a single project's disputes can play out — Delaware for entity-control questions under the LLC agreement, New Jersey for the bankruptcy case, and potentially the local redevelopment agency for compliance with the underlying urban renewal agreement. Sponsors and investors negotiating these structures should build clear, coordinated default and remedy provisions across all three layers rather than relying on any one forum to resolve a dispute completely.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A Chapter 11 filing that follows closely on the heels of a lost control fight in another forum invites a "for cause" dismissal motion under Section 1112(b); debtors should not treat bankruptcy as a fresh substantive review of an already-litigated ownership dispute.
- Preferred-equity and mezzanine investors who win a contractual removal or enforcement remedy elsewhere should be prepared for a follow-on bankruptcy filing and should preserve evidence of harm from further delay to support a swift dismissal motion.
- Multi-layered New Jersey urban renewal/PILOT structures create several independent forums for the same underlying project dispute; deal documents should anticipate how a default cascades across all of them, not just the one where it first surfaces.
