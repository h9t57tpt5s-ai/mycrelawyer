# CMBS Trustee Moves to Foreclose on Atlantic City's Showboat Over $48.4M Loan Default

### Computershare Trust Co. alleges Bart Blatstein's ownership entity has gone more than a year without a payment on the mortgage backing the historic Boardwalk property

Atlantic City's Showboat, one of the Boardwalk's most recognizable hotel properties, is now the subject of a commercial foreclosure action after its mortgage trustee accused the ownership entity controlled by developer Bart Blatstein of defaulting on a $48.4 million commercial mortgage-backed securities loan. Computershare Trust Co., acting on behalf of investors holding the CMBS trust that owns the loan, filed the foreclosure complaint on June 30, 2026 in the Superior Court of New Jersey, Chancery Division, Atlantic County, naming Showboat Properties LLC and related Blatstein entities as defendants. The trustee alleges the borrower has made no payments toward the debt in over a year and is seeking a judgment permitting a sheriff's sale of the mortgaged portion of the property.

The complaint targets a specific slice of the sprawling Showboat complex: at least 475 guest rooms, the Lucky Snake Arcade, and the property's parking garage. Blatstein, a longtime Philadelphia-area developer, purchased the shuttered former casino in 2016 and has since repositioned it as a non-gaming hotel and entertainment destination, adding attractions including an indoor waterpark. The CMBS loan at issue financed that repositioning, and its default follows a pattern that has become familiar across the hospitality sector over the past several years: properties that took on debt during a period of low rates and aggressive underwriting are now confronting a combination of higher refinancing costs, softer post-pandemic hospitality demand in some secondary and tertiary gaming markets, and lenders and servicers with far less patience for extend-and-modify workouts than they showed a few years ago.

What distinguishes this matter from a routine payment default, however, is the trail of related litigation the foreclosure complaint sits alongside. Court records show at least two separate contractor disputes tied to construction and renovation work at the Showboat's waterpark attraction. In one, a judge ordered Blatstein and his construction company to pay an electrical subcontractor, Lee-Way Electrical of Buena, more than $532,000 plus 12.5% interest after finding the work had gone unpaid. In another, an interior landscaping contractor sued Blatstein in 2024 over roughly $1.7 million in unpaid work at the waterpark; that dispute was later resolved and the case closed. Taken together with the CMBS default, the pattern painted by these filings is one of a property owner facing simultaneous pressure from its lender and from contractors who performed work on the site — precisely the kind of liquidity strain that servicers watch for as an early warning sign before a formal default.

For CMBS special servicers and trustees, the Showboat matter is also a reminder that a loan default on a large hospitality asset rarely arrives as an isolated credit event. Deferred maintenance, unpaid contractor claims, and mechanic's lien exposure frequently surface alongside a payment default, and each of those threads can complicate a foreclosure sale by clouding title or creating competing claims against sale proceeds. A receiver, if appointed, will typically need to account for and resolve outstanding contractor claims before or during the disposition process, which can extend timelines and reduce net recovery for certificate holders relative to a straightforward payment default with no ancillary litigation.

The case also illustrates the particular foreclosure mechanics that apply to CMBS-financed hospitality assets more broadly. Unlike a single-lender workout, a CMBS special servicer acts on behalf of a pool of bondholders with varying risk tolerances and often limited flexibility to grant the kind of extended forbearance a balance-sheet lender might offer a longtime borrower relationship. That structural rigidity is part of why CMBS-backed hospitality and retail assets have accounted for a disproportionate share of the distress that has worked its way through special servicing over the past two years, and why owners of CMBS-financed properties facing cash flow pressure should engage servicers early, before a monetary default forecloses more flexible resolution paths.

Practical takeaways for commercial property owners, lenders, and hospitality operators:

- Owners of CMBS-financed hospitality or entertainment assets facing cash flow strain should engage their servicer well before a payment default, since CMBS trust structures generally offer less workout flexibility than balance-sheet lenders once a monetary default has occurred.
- Unresolved contractor and mechanic's lien claims tied to renovation or expansion work can surface as red flags to lenders and servicers, and can complicate a subsequent foreclosure sale by clouding title and creating competing claims on proceeds.
- A foreclosure targeting only a defined portion of a larger mixed-use property (here, specific guest rooms, an arcade, and a garage) underscores the importance of precisely understanding which collateral secures which loan tranche in complex, multi-asset ownership structures.
- Lenders and receivers stepping into distressed hospitality assets should anticipate the need to resolve ancillary contractor litigation as part of, not separate from, the disposition process.
