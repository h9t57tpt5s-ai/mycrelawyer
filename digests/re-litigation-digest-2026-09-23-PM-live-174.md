# Cogent Bank Sues to Foreclose on Shuttered Rochester Wyndham After Code-Violation Closure

### Lender alleges more than $6.3 million owed on SBA and conventional loans after a second forbearance agreement also went into default

A downtown Rochester, New York hotel that the city forced to close over fire and building code violations is now facing a separate foreclosure lawsuit from its primary lender. Cogent Bank filed a foreclosure complaint in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, Monroe County, against hotel owner Nayan K. Patel and his affiliated entities, Horizon Edge Hospitality LLC and Horizon Edge Capital LP, alleging that more than $6.3 million is owed on loans secured by the Wyndham Rochester Downtown property at 32-70 State Street. The complaint reflects a debt of $6,347,940.30 as calculated on July 7, 2026, with interest and other charges continuing to accrue.

According to the complaint, Cogent extended Horizon Edge Hospitality two loans in April 2024: a $5 million loan guaranteed by the U.S. Small Business Administration and an $850,000 conventional loan. The bank alleges the borrowers defaulted on both by failing to make required payments and by failing to maintain the business itself, a default category that in SBA-backed hospitality lending typically covers matters such as lapsed insurance, unpaid property taxes, deferred maintenance, or a failure to keep the property operating as a going concern. The parties subsequently entered into a second forbearance agreement in May 2026, under which Cogent apparently agreed to hold off on immediate enforcement in exchange for a revised payment plan, but the bank alleges the borrowers defaulted on that agreement as well, which precipitated the foreclosure filing.

The lawsuit lands on top of an already precarious operational situation at the property. In June 2026, the City of Rochester ordered the Wyndham closed after inspectors documented 13 open fire code violations and 10 open building code violations, a decision the mayor's office followed by publicly calling on ownership to address the issues "immediately." Court filings reviewed by local reporters connect Horizon Edge Hospitality, despite the entity's presence in the New York State corporate registry, to a Georgia-based dentist, Dr. Ashish Kakadia, illustrating how SBA-guaranteed hospitality lending has increasingly drawn borrowers and investors from outside the traditional hotel-operating industry into hands-on ownership of distressed or challenging assets.

Cogent's complaint seeks both a judgment permitting foreclosure on the mortgages and the appointment of a receiver to take possession of and protect the property while the litigation proceeds — a standard and important request in a case like this one, since a shuttered hotel with open code violations is exactly the kind of asset that can deteriorate further, or become a liability magnet, if left unsecured and unmanaged during a multi-month or multi-year foreclosure timeline. A receiver's ability to step in, secure the premises, and begin addressing code violations can meaningfully affect the property's ultimate resale value and reduce the lender's loss severity relative to leaving a shuttered, code-deficient building to sit vacant.

The case is also a useful illustration of how SBA-guaranteed commercial lending interacts with local code enforcement in ways that can compound a borrower's distress. An SBA guarantee reduces a lender's credit risk on the underlying loan, but it does not insulate a borrower from a municipality's independent authority to shut down a noncompliant property, and a forced closure can itself trigger or accelerate a payment default by eliminating the operating revenue a borrower needs to service its debt. For lenders underwriting hospitality assets with SBA participation, the case is a reminder that code compliance and deferred maintenance risk deserve ongoing monitoring even after closing, not just at origination, particularly where a borrower is a first-time or out-of-industry hotel operator.

Practical takeaways for lenders, borrowers, and hospitality asset owners:

- A municipal closure order over code violations can independently trigger or accelerate a loan default by cutting off a property's operating revenue, even where the loan itself was otherwise performing.
- SBA guarantees reduce a lender's ultimate credit exposure but do not substitute for ongoing post-closing monitoring of a borrower's maintenance practices, code compliance, and operational health.
- A second forbearance agreement that also lapses is a meaningful signal to courts and to the market that a workout path has been exhausted, and lenders should be prepared to move promptly to receivership relief to preserve asset value once that point is reached.
- Out-of-industry or first-time hotel owners, including those using SBA-guaranteed acquisition financing, face the same code-compliance and capital-reserve obligations as experienced operators, and lenders should factor operator experience into underwriting and servicing intensity.
