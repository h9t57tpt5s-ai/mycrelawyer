# Ohio Supreme Court Clears the Way for Developers to Sue Cities for Damages After Winning a Site-Plan Appeal

### In a unanimous ruling, the state's high court holds that a developer need not plead damages during an administrative zoning appeal to preserve a later claim for money — a procedural win with implications for any commercial project stalled by a municipality's improper rejection of an approved site plan.

Commercial developers who win an administrative appeal reversing a municipality's improper rejection of a site plan have long faced an uncomfortable question: does that victory, by itself, preserve a right to sue for the resulting financial harm, or must damages be pleaded during the administrative appeal itself, on pain of forfeiting them? On September 15, 2026, the Supreme Court of Ohio answered that question in *Sheetz, Inc. v. City of Centerville*, holding unanimously that Ohio law does not permit — and therefore does not require — a party to seek money damages or declaratory relief during an R.C. Chapter 2506 administrative appeal, and that a developer who prevails on such an appeal may still bring a separate civil action for damages afterward.

The case traces to 2023, when the Centerville City Council, responding to public opposition from groups including Epiphany Lutheran Church and Bethany Village Senior Living, voted to overturn a site plan for a new Sheetz travel-center and convenience store at 6318 Far Hills Avenue near the Interstate 675 interchange — a plan the city's own planning commission had already approved unanimously as compliant with every applicable development standard. Sheetz appealed that rejection administratively, and in January 2025 a Montgomery County Common Pleas Court judge agreed that the council had exceeded its authority, ordering the city to approve the site plan it never should have rejected. That victory settled the zoning question, but it left open whether Sheetz could recover for the sixteen-plus months of construction delay, lost revenue, and carrying costs the improper rejection caused — and whether Sheetz had, in fact, already lost that right by not asking for damages during the administrative appeal itself.

Centerville argued it had. The city's position was that Ohio's administrative-appeal statute, combined with ordinary preclusion principles, required Sheetz to raise any damages claim as part of the same proceeding that challenged the council's decision, and that failing to do so barred a later, separate lawsuit seeking money. A federal district court handling a related claim certified the underlying state-law question to the Ohio Supreme Court for guidance, given its significance beyond the immediate dispute. Writing for a unanimous court that included Chief Justice Sharon L. Kennedy and Justices Patrick F. Fischer, Jennifer Brunner, Daniel R. Hawkins, and Megan E. Shanahan, Justice Joseph T. Deters rejected Centerville's position outright: an R.C. Chapter 2506 administrative appeal is, by its nature, a limited proceeding confined to reviewing whether the agency's decision was supported by the evidence and consistent with law, not a vehicle for litigating damages or declaratory claims. Because Sheetz could not have sought money in the administrative appeal, it was under no obligation to try, and pursuing a later civil action for damages arising from the same wrongful rejection is not a prohibited second bite at the same claim.

The ruling matters well beyond one Dayton-area convenience store. Ohio, like most states, relies heavily on administrative appeals as the primary mechanism for challenging local zoning and site-plan decisions, and municipalities frequently argue — sometimes successfully in the absence of controlling authority — that a developer's failure to raise every conceivable claim during that appeal forfeits it. The Ohio Supreme Court's decision forecloses that argument for damages claims specifically, giving developers, and the REITs and institutional owners who fund them, a clear procedural roadmap: win the zoning fight first through the administrative process, then pursue a separate action for the resulting financial harm without fear that the first victory quietly extinguished the second claim. For municipalities, the decision raises the stakes of an improper site-plan denial considerably, since a reversed rejection can now be followed by real exposure to damages litigation rather than ending, from the city's perspective, with a merely symbolic administrative loss.

The decision also underscores a broader lesson about how procedural architecture shapes substantive risk in land-use disputes. Because Ohio's Chapter 2506 review is deliberately narrow — a court sitting in that posture reviews the existing administrative record rather than hearing new evidence or entertaining claims for relief the underlying agency itself has no power to grant — grafting a damages requirement onto it would have forced developers into an impossible position, seeking a remedy from a forum structurally incapable of awarding it. The Ohio Supreme Court's unwillingness to read such a requirement into the statute reflects a broader principle that state courts nationally have grappled with in various forms: that preclusion doctrines exist to prevent piecemeal litigation of the same claim, not to punish parties for failing to bring claims a prior proceeding could never have entertained in the first place.

For now, Sheetz's underlying damages claim heads back to the trial court for further proceedings, with liability for the sixteen months of delay caused by Centerville's rejected site plan still to be determined. Centerville, for its part, still faces the prospect of defending a damages case on the merits, having lost the procedural argument that might have ended the dispute before it reached that stage.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Developers and REITs whose approved or entitled projects are wrongfully rejected or delayed by a municipality should not assume that winning an administrative zoning appeal exhausts their remedies — a separate civil action for the resulting damages generally remains available, at least in Ohio, and developers in other states should confirm their own jurisdiction's preclusion rules rather than assume the same result.
- Because administrative appeals under statutes like Ohio's R.C. Chapter 2506 are typically confined to the existing record and cannot award money damages, attempting to shoehorn a damages claim into that proceeding is neither required nor likely to succeed — a separate, properly pleaded civil action is the correct vehicle.
- Owners and developers facing a protracted, improperly motivated site-plan rejection should document delay-related costs contemporaneously (carrying costs, lost revenue, financing costs, and construction-cost escalation), since a later damages action will turn on proof of these figures once liability is established.
- Municipalities and their counsel should recognize that reversing course on a wrongful site-plan denial no longer functions as a clean exit from the dispute — a damages exposure can follow even after the zoning question itself is resolved in the developer's favor.
