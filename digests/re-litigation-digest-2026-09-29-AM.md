# CMBS Lenders Accuse Upstate NY Town of Running a 'Debt Evasion Scheme' to Save a Defaulted Mall Owner

### A $210 million foreclosure fight over Eastview Mall has collided with a small town's eminent domain plan, raising a pointed question for CRE lenders everywhere: can a municipality use its condemnation power to bail out a private borrower?

A foreclosure action filed earlier this month against the owners of Eastview Mall, one of upstate New York's largest shopping centers, has escalated into a sharp fight over whether the Town of Victor, New York is using its eminent domain authority to help the mall's owner dodge a $210 million debt. In objections filed September 25 ahead of a September 28 public hearing on the town's condemnation plan, counsel for lenders U.S. Bank National Association and Wells Fargo Bank — acting through special servicer Rialto Capital Advisors — called the plan a "debt evasion scheme" designed to serve the private interests of mall owner Wilmorite rather than any legitimate public purpose. The dispute is now playing out on two fronts at once: a foreclosure case pending in New York State Supreme Court, New York County, and a municipal eminent domain proceeding in Victor, New York, a small Ontario County town best known as the mall's home.

The underlying debt dates to 2012, when Eastview Mall's owner refinanced the property with a $210 million CMBS loan. That loan matured in September 2022, and Wilmorite negotiated three separate extensions with its lenders over the following three years, pushing the maturity date out to September 6, 2026. When that final deadline passed without repayment or refinancing, U.S. Bank and Wells Fargo — as trustee and noteholder on the securitized loan — filed a foreclosure action on September 8 in New York County Supreme Court, seeking not only to foreclose but also to have Spinoso Real Estate Group appointed receiver to operate the mall while the case proceeds.

What makes the case unusual is the parallel track that opened almost simultaneously in Victor's town government. According to lender filings and local reporting, Wilmorite and Victor Town Supervisor Jack Marren worked between July and early September on a plan under which the town would use its eminent domain power to acquire the mortgaged portions of the mall — five specific parcels — paying compensation pegged to assessed value, a figure lenders say would fall well short of what is owed on the loan. The town would then convey the acquired property to Victor 2040 LLC, an entity affiliated with Wilmorite, effectively returning control of the mall to its existing owner while stripping the lenders' collateral out from under the pending foreclosure action.

Lenders' counsel argues the arrangement fails eminent domain's most basic constitutional requirement: that a taking serve a genuine public use, not a private one. Their September 25 objection contends the town's proposal does not adequately identify which specific property rights it intends to acquire, was not preceded by the environmental review New York law requires for a condemnation of this scale, and — most pointedly — was conceived and timed specifically to interrupt the foreclosure case rather than to advance any independent municipal purpose. At the September 28 hearing, Victor officials heard extended public comment and lender objections but left the matter open rather than voting, according to local coverage of the session, meaning both the eminent domain question and the foreclosure case remain live.

For commercial real estate lenders and special servicers, the Eastview Mall dispute is a warning shot about a creative — and largely untested — defensive maneuver against foreclosure. Borrowers facing loan maturity default have limited options once extension negotiations are exhausted: negotiate a deed-in-lieu, contest the foreclosure on the merits, or seek bankruptcy protection. What Wilmorite and Victor allegedly attempted instead is a fourth path that borrows the machinery of local government — condemnation, ordinarily reserved for roads, schools, and public infrastructure — to extinguish a private lender's collateral rights on favorable terms. If courts or the town ultimately bless that approach, it would hand distressed borrowers with sympathetic local relationships a powerful new tool: enlist a friendly municipality to condemn the collateral before the lender can foreclose on it, then buy it back from the town at a price far below the outstanding debt.

The case also illustrates how public-private entanglement in economically important local landmarks — Eastview Mall is reportedly one of the larger private employers and sales-tax generators in the Rochester suburb — can create pressure on municipal officials to intervene on a struggling owner's behalf, even where that intervention sits uneasily with eminent domain doctrine's public-use requirement. Practitioners advising either lenders or distressed CRE borrowers should watch how the New York County Supreme Court and the Town of Victor resolve the overlapping proceedings, since the outcome will speak directly to whether "debt evasion by condemnation" is a viable strategy or a one-off controversy unlikely to survive judicial scrutiny.

**Practical takeaways:**
- CMBS special servicers negotiating loan extensions with borrowers who have close relationships with local government should watch for parallel municipal action — zoning changes, tax assessments, or eminent domain proceedings — that could be timed to interfere with eventual foreclosure remedies.
- A eminent domain action aimed at transferring condemned property to a private buyer, rather than retaining it for public use, remains vulnerable to a public-use challenge; lenders facing this tactic should move quickly to build a record on intent and timing, as the Eastview lenders did here.
- Receivership requests filed alongside a foreclosure complaint can help lenders preserve property operations and value during exactly this kind of prolonged, multi-front dispute.
- Borrowers and municipalities contemplating similar arrangements should expect that maturity-default timing, negotiation history, and the identity of the ultimate transferee will all be scrutinized closely by a court asked to evaluate whether a taking truly serves the public.
