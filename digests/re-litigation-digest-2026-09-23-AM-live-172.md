# California Appeals Court Extends Privette Doctrine Shield to Landlords in Tenant-Contractor Injury Cases

### A commercial landlord that leased roof space to a tenant for equipment installation, without itself hiring the contractor whose worker was injured, is nonetheless protected from liability under California's Privette doctrine, the First Appellate District has ruled — widening a defense long thought to require a direct hiring relationship.

Commercial landlords in California have generally understood that when their tenants bring in independent contractors to perform work on leased premises, and one of that contractor's workers gets hurt, the landlord's own liability exposure turns heavily on whether it was the entity that actually hired the contractor. On August 26, 2026, the California Court of Appeal, First Appellate District, Division Two, complicated that understanding in *Schurman Family Company TIC v. Superior Court*, granting a warehouse owner writ relief and directing the trial court to enter summary judgment in the landlord's favor — even though the landlord itself never hired the contractor whose employee was injured.

The underlying facts are a common commercial real estate fact pattern. Schurman Family Company, as tenants-in-common owner of a warehouse, had leased roof space and associated access rights to Verizon Wireless specifically so Verizon could install and maintain communications equipment there. Verizon, exercising rights the lease expressly contemplated, hired a general contractor to service that equipment, and the contractor in turn sent an employee to the roof to perform the work. That employee fell through a skylight and was seriously injured, and he sued the warehouse owner directly, rather than — or in addition to — pursuing workers' compensation remedies against his own employer.

California's Privette doctrine, developed over three decades of appellate decisions beginning with the California Supreme Court's 1993 decision in *Privette v. Superior Court*, generally shields a party that hires an independent contractor from liability for injuries the contractor's own employees sustain while performing the contracted work, on the theory that the contractor is better positioned to control workplace safety and that its workers' compensation coverage is the intended remedy. The doctrine's core protection has always run most naturally to the actual hiring party. The trial court in this case applied that logic straightforwardly: because Schurman was the tenant's landlord, not the entity that directly hired the contractor Verizon brought in, the trial court reasoned Privette simply did not apply, and it denied the landlord's motion for summary judgment.

The Court of Appeal reversed, holding that Privette's protection is not confined to parties in a direct hiring relationship with the contractor but extends to anyone within what the court described as the "chain of delegation" leading to the contractor's retention. Because Schurman's lease with Verizon expressly contemplated that Verizon would bring in independent contractors to install and maintain the very equipment the injured worker was servicing, and Verizon in fact hired the contractor to perform exactly that contemplated work, the appellate panel held that Schurman fell within that chain of delegation and was entitled to Privette's protection just as though it had hired the contractor directly, absent some recognized exception to the doctrine.

The decision matters considerably for how commercial landlords structure and think about lease provisions authorizing tenant-arranged contractor work — a routine feature of leases covering rooftop telecommunications equipment, HVAC systems, tenant improvements, and similar installations where the tenant, not the landlord, engages and directs the outside contractor. Before this ruling, a cautious landlord's counsel might have advised that Privette protection was uncertain, or unavailable outright, whenever the landlord itself was not the hiring party — counseling separate insurance, indemnification, or risk-transfer provisions specifically to cover that gap. The First District's chain-of-delegation reasoning substantially narrows that gap for California landlords, at least where the lease itself contemplates and authorizes the tenant's use of independent contractors for the work in question, and where no recognized exception to Privette (such as a landlord's concealment of a known hazard, or retained control over the manner of the contractor's work) applies.

That last qualification matters. The Privette doctrine has several well-established exceptions — for instance, where the hiring party retains control over safety conditions and negligently exercises that control, or where a landowner fails to warn about a concealed hazardous condition. The First District's opinion extends the doctrine's core protection to a broader category of parties in the delegation chain, but it does not eliminate those exceptions, meaning landlords still need to be attentive to whether they retained any control over the work or possessed undisclosed knowledge of hazardous conditions like a compromised skylight or roof structure. Landlords whose leases are silent on contractor use, or that do not clearly authorize the type of work the contractor's employee was performing when injured, may find themselves outside the chain-of-delegation reasoning this decision relies on.

**Practical takeaways:**

- California commercial landlords whose leases authorize tenants to bring in independent contractors for installation, maintenance, or similar work now have a stronger argument that Privette doctrine protection extends to them even without a direct hiring relationship, provided the lease clearly contemplates the type of contracted work at issue.
- The decision does not eliminate Privette's traditional exceptions — landlords who retain control over how contracted work is performed, or who fail to disclose known hazardous conditions such as structural roof or skylight defects, remain exposed regardless of the chain-of-delegation analysis.
- Landlords negotiating or renewing leases that authorize tenant-arranged contractor access, particularly for rooftop, mechanical, or similar specialized installations, should ensure the lease language clearly and specifically contemplates that category of work, since the strength of the chain-of-delegation argument in future disputes will likely turn on how explicitly the lease anticipated it.
- Because this is a Court of Appeal decision from a single district, landlords and their counsel should watch for whether other California appellate districts, or ultimately the California Supreme Court, adopt the same chain-of-delegation reasoning before relying on it as settled statewide law.
