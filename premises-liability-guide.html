Premises Liability & Negligence: A 51-Jurisdiction Handbook

What a claimant must prove, what defenses a property owner can raise, and the standard to recover punitive damages — for every state plus D.C.

Free reference guide — CREdocket Litigation Tools

Scope & Disclaimer

This guide covers ordinary premises-liability/negligence claims arising from a hazardous or dangerous condition on commercial or residential real property — slip-and-fall, inadequate/negligent security, negligent maintenance or structural failure, and failure to warn. It does not cover product liability, medical malpractice, workers'-compensation-exclusive workplace injuries, or landlord-tenant habitability disputes (see the Commercial Eviction Handbook for the latter).

This is general legal information for orientation purposes only — not legal advice for any specific matter, and not a substitute for a licensed attorney in the relevant jurisdiction. Comparative-fault rules, punitive-damages standards, and statutory caps change through legislation and case law; always confirm current, binding authority before relying on anything below.

The General Framework

What has to be proven, and how it can be defeated

Elements to Prove

A premises-liability negligence claim generally requires: (1) Duty — owed based on the visitor's status (invitee/licensee/trespasser at common law, or a unified reasonable-care duty in "Rowland rule" states such as California); (2) Breach — the owner knew or, through reasonable inspection, should have known of the hazard and failed to fix, remedy, or warn of it; (3) Notice — actual or constructive notice that the specific condition existed long enough to have been discovered, unless the owner's own conduct created the hazard (some states relax this via a "mode of operation" rule for self-service business risks); (4) Causation — the breach actually and proximately caused the injury; and (5) Damages — provable medical expenses, lost income, and pain and suffering.

Defenses Available

The most consequential defense in nearly every case is the plaintiff's own comparative or contributory fault — see the state-by-state rule below, since it varies from an absolute bar (any plaintiff fault) to no bar at all. Other recurring defenses: the open-and-obvious doctrine (no duty to warn of a hazard a reasonable visitor would have seen, though a growing number of courts preserve a duty to remedy it regardless); assumption of risk (the plaintiff knowingly and voluntarily encountered a known danger); a reduced duty to trespassers (limited to refraining from willful/wanton injury, subject to the attractive-nuisance exception for child trespassers); lack of notice; and, where a tenant or independent contractor — not the owner — controlled or caused the condition, a dispute over which party owed the duty at all.

Punitive Damages

Ordinary negligence — even negligence that causes a severe injury — never supports punitive damages. A claimant must additionally prove the owner's conduct was willful, wanton, or in reckless/conscious disregard of a known danger — typically shown through evidence the owner already knew of the exact hazard (e.g., prior similar incidents) and did nothing. Most states require this be shown by clear and convincing evidence, a higher bar than the preponderance standard used for the underlying negligence claim; a handful of states set a different bar entirely (see below), and several prohibit punitive damages in an ordinary tort claim altogether absent a specific enabling statute.
