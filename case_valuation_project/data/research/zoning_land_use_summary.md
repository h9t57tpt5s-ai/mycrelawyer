# Zoning & Land Use Litigation — Research Summary

18 cases collected (target was 15-20). 9 jurisdictions represented: California (3), New York (3), Pennsylvania (3), Michigan (3), North Carolina (2), Washington (1), Arizona (1), Minnesota (1), Puerto Rico/1st Circuit (1). Claim types: section_1983_zoning_claim (8), variance_permit_denial_appeal (4), spot_zoning_challenge (3), development_agreement_breach (3). Commercial/industrial development is the dominant fact pattern (industrial buildings, billboards, self-storage, cannabis retail, warehouse/distribution, hotel/condo, mixed-use), with two development-agreement cases (Cle Elum, Mammoth Lakes) arising from primarily residential projects included because they are the best-documented, highest-dollar examples of the development-agreement-breach mechanics that apply equally to commercial deals.

## Patterns observed

**Plaintiffs lose routine variance/permit-denial appeals more often than they win, and lose Section 1983 zoning claims even more often.** Of the four variance_permit_denial_appeal cases here, two reversed the denial (Sam Commercial Properties, The Smoking Tree) and one affirmed it (Franklin Sq. Realty) — roughly consistent with the general rule that state courts apply a deferential, rational-basis-style standard to zoning board determinations and only reverse for a clear legal or procedural error (misreading an ordinance, ignoring a mandatory standard). Of the eight section_1983_zoning_claim cases, only two produced a disclosed plaintiff recovery (Del Monte Dunes, Orangetown v. Magee); the rest either failed outright (PFZ Properties, Green Genie, Rubicon, and ultimately Sherman v. Town of Chester on remand) or won on liability/ripeness without a confirmed final damages figure in the public record (Avenue 6E, Nasierowski). This lopsided win rate reflects the doctrinal reality that federal courts require far more than an erroneous or even bad-faith zoning denial to find a constitutional violation — ordinary administrative error is relegated to state-court review (Article 78, certiorari, etc.), not a federal damages remedy.

**What separates a winning Section 1983 zoning claim from a routine denied appeal is usually one of: (1) a vested property right coupled with government bad faith (Orangetown v. Magee — permit already issued, over $4M spent, then the zoning code was amended specifically to kill the project); (2) a jury-tried regulatory-taking theory after a long pattern of shifting, increasingly severe demands across repeated applications (Del Monte Dunes — five rejections over years); (3) a complete absence of any notice/hearing before a targeted rezoning (Nasierowski — pure procedural due process, which is more forgiving than substantive due process or equal protection); or (4) direct evidence of discriminatory intent or disparate treatment tied to a protected class, which is what got Avenue 6E Investments past summary judgment where dozens of other cannabis- and permit-delay cases (Green Genie, Rubicon, PFZ Properties) failed. Simple frustration, delay, or an arguably wrong administrative decision — even one that collapses a project and costs a developer millions, as in Rubicon Real Estate Holdings v. City of Pontiac (2026) — is not enough on its own.**

**When a Section 1983 zoning claim or development-agreement breach does succeed with a disclosed dollar figure, the awards are large relative to routine unpaid-rent or lease disputes, because the injury is the lost value of an entire development project, not a stream of rent:** Del Monte Dunes ($1.45M), Orangetown v. Magee ($5.14M), Lockaway Storage ($1.7M combined damages/fees for a permit-delay taking), Mammoth Lakes ($30M + ~$2.3M fees), and City Heights Holdings v. Cle Elum ($22M arbitration award, large enough to push the town toward Chapter 9 bankruptcy). These are the tail of the distribution, not the median — most zoning disputes that reach litigation produce no dollar figure at all because the relief sought and granted is an order compelling or invalidating a permit/rezoning (see below), and even the successful Section 1983 plaintiffs represent a small fraction of filed claims.

**Fee-shifting under 42 U.S.C. Section 1988 matters but is only reached after liability is established, which is the hard part.** Every successful Section 1983 case in this sample that disclosed a dollar figure (Orangetown v. Magee, Lockaway Storage — a state regulatory-taking case but analytically parallel) included an attorneys'-fee award on top of the merits recovery, and Mammoth Lakes' state-law development-agreement judgment likewise carried a separate ~$2.3M fee award. But fee-shifting is irrelevant in the large majority of cases in this sample where the municipality prevailed (PFZ Properties, Sherman on remand, Green Genie, Rubicon) — there, the municipality's own litigation costs are typically not recoverable from the plaintiff absent a frivolous-suit finding, so the practical exposure asymmetry favors well-resourced municipal defendants able to litigate to summary judgment.

**Spot-zoning challenges are almost always brought by neighboring objectors against a developer's favorable rezoning, not by developers seeking relief, and the remedy is categorically injunctive/declaratory — invalidating the ordinance — never damages.** All three spot-zoning cases here (Allen Distribution, Lathan, Chaffier) follow this pattern: a landowner secures a rezoning benefiting a single parcel, and neighbors successfully challenge it as lacking a comprehensive-plan rationale. A developer relying on a spot-zoning theory offensively (to attack a denial or a competitor's rezoning) is a much rarer posture in the reported case law found here.

**Cannabis retail and cultivation permitting has become a major, current source of zoning-adjacent litigation across all four claim types**, appearing in three of the eighteen cases here (Green Genie v. Detroit, Rubicon v. Pontiac, The Smoking Tree v. Albert Lea) — all decided or arising within the last three years. Outcomes are mixed and fact-specific: The Smoking Tree won on a narrow "the city broke its own ordinance" theory (an administrative-law/variance-appeal argument), while Green Genie and Rubicon lost on constitutional theories requiring proof of discriminatory intent or a protected property interest that is difficult to establish. Data-center development is an emerging, closely related 2024-2026 fact pattern (multiple lawsuits noted but not yet resolved publicly, so not included as scored cases here — see Data limitations).

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **The great majority of zoning and land-use disputes never generate a public dollar figure, or any litigation at all.** Most variance/permit denials are resolved (or simply accepted) at the municipal zoning board or planning commission level with no court filing whatsoever. Of the disputes that do escalate to court, the standard remedy sought is an order compelling issuance of a permit, invalidating a rezoning, or remanding for reconsideration — not money damages — so this sample is structurally skewed toward the minority of cases that (a) escalated all the way to litigation and (b) either sought damages under Section 1983/development-agreement theories or happened to generate public reporting of a settlement figure. A valuation tool relying on this dataset should treat the no-dollar-figure outcomes as the statistically typical case, not the exception.
2. **Twelve of eighteen entries carry no dollar figure** (Allen Distribution, Lathan, Chaffier, Sam Commercial Properties, Franklin Sq. Realty, The Smoking Tree, Avenue 6E, Nasierowski, PFZ Properties, Sherman, Green Genie, Rubicon). These were kept because they establish the load-bearing doctrine (what separates a winning claim from a losing one, what the actual injunctive/declaratory relief looks like) even without a monetary anchor, consistent with the assignment's framing that injunctive/declaratory outcomes are a legitimate and common real-world result in this category.
3. **Two of the three development_agreement_breach entries (Mammoth Lakes, Cle Elum) involve primarily residential projects**, even though development-agreement law and damages mechanics apply identically to commercial deals. A search for a comparably well-documented, large-dollar, commercial-only development-agreement breach judgment did not surface a case as clean as these two in the time available; a production pass with paid docket/verdict-database access should prioritize finding a directly commercial substitute or supplement.
4. **Several "final outcome" figures were not independently confirmed on remand.** Avenue 6E Investments v. City of Yuma and Nasierowski Bros. v. City of Sterling Heights both established liability or survived summary judgment at the circuit level, but this research did not locate a publicly reported settlement or damages-trial figure from the subsequent remand proceedings — both are recorded with dollarAmount: null and flagged for follow-up rather than guessed at.
5. **The Cle Elum figure is an arbitration award, not a court judgment**, though it is exceptionally well-documented (it precipitated the city's move toward municipal bankruptcy) and analytically identical to a court-ordered damages award for breach of the development agreement.
6. **No verdict/settlement database access.** This research used public web search and case-summary/news sources only, without Westlaw/Lexis/VerdictSearch access. Zoning fights over specific developments that settled quietly (a very common outcome, especially where a municipality agrees to approve a modified project in exchange for dropping a threatened Section 1983 suit) are systematically underrepresented here, because those settlements are rarely covered by legal press unless the dollar figure or public controversy is unusually large.
7. **Currently pending, unresolved litigation was deliberately excluded from the scored dataset**, even where well-publicized and clearly zoning-related — for example, several 2024-2026 data-center zoning fights (Hexa Builders LLC's reported $300M claim against Monroe Township, NJ; TenKey LandCo v. Simpson County, KY; National Land Developers LLC v. Andover Township, NJ) were identified during research but have no disclosed resolution yet and were left out rather than included with a speculative outcome. A future refresh of this dataset should revisit these, since data-center zoning litigation is a fast-growing category likely to produce several of the next disclosed large-dollar outcomes in this space.
8. **Jurisdictional coverage is broad but not exhaustive and skews toward states with strong online case-law/news indexing** (California, New York, Pennsylvania, Michigan, North Carolina). Large commercial real estate markets underrepresented in this batch include Texas, Florida, Illinois, New Jersey, and Georgia — all of which have active zoning/land-use litigation (e.g., New Jersey's data-center zoning fights, noted above) that a follow-up pass should target specifically.
