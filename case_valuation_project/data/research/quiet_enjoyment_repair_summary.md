# Research Summary: Breach of Quiet Enjoyment / Constructive Eviction / Failure to Repair (Commercial Leases)

17 real, sourced cases collected in `quiet_enjoyment_repair.json`, spanning NJ, NY, MA, IL, CA, NC, TX, FL, PA, CO, and MI. 13 are published court opinions with real reporter citations; 2 are law-firm case-result announcements with real dollar figures but no independently verifiable citation; 2 (Sentry Water Systems, Kernochan) are older/standard-setting opinions included mainly for their legal-principle value.

## Patterns observed

1. **Dollar figures are the exception, not the rule, in published opinions.** Most appellate constructive-eviction/quiet-enjoyment decisions affirm or reverse *liability* and articulate the legal standard, but either don't state a specific damages figure in the opinion, remand damages for further proceedings, or (as in Wesson) award a modest, specific sum that is far smaller than what the tenant claimed. Where I could not personally verify a number from a primary source, `dollarAmount` is `null` rather than an estimate.

2. **Failure-to-repair fact patterns cluster around a small number of recurring triggers**: roof leaks/water intrusion (Reste Realty, K&S Enterprises, Katz Deli, the Miami-Dade penthouse case, the Delray Beach mold case), failure to provide essential building services — heat, electricity, elevator, HVAC (Charles E. Burt, Automobile Supply Co., Barash), and active landlord interference via construction or renovation (Radinsky v. Weaver, Frittelli). Very few cases involve a landlord literally blocking access short of a full lockout; that fact pattern is discussed heavily in practitioner blog posts but rarely litigated to a reported opinion — it usually resolves via injunction or self-help-eviction statutes rather than a quiet-enjoyment damages verdict.

3. **Tenant remaining in possession is a recurring defense-winning fact.** Barash (NY), Automobile Supply Co. (IL), and the older Pennsylvania rule in Chelten Ave. Bldg. Corp. v. Mayer (not included as a full entry, but referenced during research) all turned on the tenant staying in occupancy or delaying abandonment too long, which forfeits a constructive-eviction claim. Any valuation tool should flag "did the tenant vacate, and how quickly" as a threshold, near-binary variable — much like the reasonable-time analysis the Texas court did in Daftary (12-18 months was reasonable for a dental practice to relocate).

4. **"Cumulative interference" is a growing doctrine.** Sears v. 69th Street Retail Mall's "death by a thousand cuts" theory (PA, 2015) lets a tenant aggregate years of individually-insufficient neglect into a single constructive-eviction claim. This is a relatively recent and significant doctrinal development worth flagging to users — it lowers the bar for otherwise-marginal failure-to-repair complaints when there's a long documented history.

5. **Modern commercial leases often waive or narrow quiet enjoyment.** Frittelli (CA, 2011) shows that standard remodeling/exculpatory clauses in commercial leases can defeat a quiet-enjoyment claim as a matter of law even where the landlord's renovation genuinely damaged the tenant's business. Any valuation model should ask users whether their lease contains a remodeling, alteration, or "no liability for landlord's acts" clause, since it can be case-dispositive.

## Honest limitations of this dataset

- **Confirmed dollar figures are thin.** Of 17 cases, only 4 carry a specific number I could verify (Wesson: $1,063; Radinsky: $2,212; the Miami-Dade case: $570,000; the Delray Beach settlement: $435,000). The two largest, most "usable" dollar figures are **not from published court opinions** — they are law-firm case-result/marketing pages. I could not locate the underlying case name, docket number, or citation for either despite multiple targeted searches, so they are flagged `confidence: "medium"` with an explicit warning in `notes` not to present them as citable published opinions. If citation integrity is paramount, consider dropping these two or clearly labeling them in the product UI as "reported outcomes, not verified court records."
- **Several older/foundational cases (Kernochan 1917, Burt 1959, Automobile Supply Co. 1930) are frequently cited for their legal standard but their opinions/summaries available to me did not state exact damages**, even though a trial-level number likely exists in the original record. I did not fabricate figures to fill these gaps.
- **PDF opinions were often unreachable.** The primary opinion PDFs for Sears v. 69th Street Retail Mall (PA Superior Court) and a few Justia case pages returned HTTP 403 or unparseable binary content to my fetch tool, so some facts and any damages figures for that case rely on a secondary law-firm summary rather than the opinion itself.
- **Jurisdictional coverage is uneven.** I found solid published opinions for NJ, NY, MA, IL, CA, NC, TX, FL, PA, CO, and MI, but was not able to locate a clearly on-point *commercial* (not residential) reported case for several other populous states (e.g., Washington, Virginia, Ohio's commercial-specific case law was thinner than its residential case law, Minnesota's leading case was residential). I did not force-fit residential cases into this commercial-only dataset — e.g., I found and deliberately excluded George v. Hercules Real Estate Services (GA), East Haven Associates v. Gurian (NY), Blackett v. Olanoff (MA), and Colonial Court Apartments v. Kern (MN) because their fact patterns were residential apartment leases, not commercial.
- **Some included cases are procedural/negative outcomes rather than damages awards** (Barash, Automobile Supply Co., Frittelli, Westwood Motorcars). These are valuable for the valuation tool because they show *when a claim fails* and why, which is arguably as important for exposure modeling as a positive damages figure — but they should not be mistaken for evidence of typical recovery amounts.
- **No claim of exhaustiveness.** This is a sample assembled from targeted searches, not a systematic survey of all reported US commercial quiet-enjoyment/constructive-eviction case law. A production-grade valuation model should supplement this with a Westlaw/Lexis pull and, ideally, verdict/settlement databases (e.g., VerdictSearch, Jury Verdict Research) that specialize in reporting dollar outcomes with docket-level verification — general web search is a poor tool for finding reliable damages figures in older or unpublished commercial cases.
