# Construction Defect Litigation — Research Summary

17 cases collected (target was 15-20). 9 jurisdictions represented: FL (5), NJ (3), NY, NV, WI, TX, SC, HI, CA, MA (1st Cir., MA law), IA (7th Cir., Iowa law), DC. Two entries (Aston Martin Residences, 443 Greenwich St.) were pulled from CREdocket's own already-tracked "construction-defect" cases in `js/data.js` per the research brief. Property types span luxury condo/condo-conversion towers, townhome communities, a casino parking garage, a hotel tower, a university research building, and a mixed-use retail/office development. All four claim types are represented: contractor_breach_negligence (11), insurance_coverage_defect_dispute (3), design_professional_malpractice (2), indemnification_contribution_claim (1).

Of the 17 cases, 13 have a disclosed dollar figure representing an actual settlement or jury verdict (not just an amount sought); the remaining 4 (Mycon v. six CGL insurers, Admiral v. Tocci, Cornice & Rose v. Acuity, Aston Martin, 443 Greenwich) are either still pending or are pleading-stage demands, and are flagged accordingly in `dollarAmountBasis`.

## Patterns observed

**Disclosed dollar figures cluster by defect severity and failure mode, not just building size:**
- **Catastrophic/life-safety failures during construction** (structural collapse or a defect so severe the structure can't safely be completed) produce the largest outcomes in this sample by a wide margin: Champlain Towers South ($997M, including a $400M contribution from an adjacent project's developer/contractor/engineer), Harmon Hotel ($195M, ending in demolition of the unfinished tower), Tropicana parking garage collapse ($101M), and the Milwaukee parking garage panel collapse ($39M). These are outliers that should anchor the *top* of a valuation range, not the median.
- **Post-occupancy latent defects** (water intrusion, facade/envelope failure, HVAC/MEP defects) in condo and townhome projects cluster much lower, roughly $10M-$56M in this sample regardless of unit count: Regalia on the Ocean ($17.5M, 39 units), Grandview at Riverwalk ($10M combined), Palm Springs 88-unit condo ($9.75M), Waiea tower (~$116.5M, 177 units — an outlier likely reflecting the scale of the curtain-wall replacement), Park Hill Winter Park ($26.5M, only 10 units — about $2.65M/unit, the highest per-unit figure in the sample), and East Bridge Town Lofts ($55M across ~200 units, driven mostly by a $33M punitive-damages award).
- **A rough per-unit or per-project heuristic does not hold well across property types.** Small, severely-defective luxury projects (Park Hill: 10 units, $26.5M) can produce a higher per-unit settlement than much larger towers with more contained defects (Regalia: 39 units, $17.5M total but only $9.6M was cash repair cost). Defect *pervasiveness across every unit* (present in Park Hill, ACTS Law Palm Springs case, and East Bridge Town Lofts) is a stronger driver of settlement size than raw unit count or building height.

**Defect type drives both the theory of liability and who ends up paying:**
- **Building-envelope/waterproofing defects** (window/door seals, stucco, stone veneer, roofing, deck waterproofing) are the most common defect type in this sample (Palm Springs, Park Hill, Regalia, 443 Greenwich, Aston Martin) and are almost always pursued as ordinary negligence/breach-of-contract against the developer and GC, with architects and subcontractors added as co-defendants but bearing a smaller share of the eventual recovery (see Grandview: $9M developer vs. $1M architect on the same facts).
- **Structural defects** (rebar, foundation, facade panel attachment) trigger the largest exposure and are more likely to involve engineers directly and to escalate into demolition/collapse scenarios (Harmon Hotel, Champlain Towers, Milwaukee garage, Tropicana garage).
- **MEP/HVAC defects** appear as a contributing factor rather than the primary claim driver in this sample (Park Hill's undersized HVAC causing mold; Waiea's inconsistent hot water) — they tend to compound an envelope or structural claim rather than support a standalone large recovery.

**Indemnification/contribution allocation among GC, subcontractors, and design professionals:**
- Where a defect traces to a specific subcontractor's workmanship (Milwaukee garage panel attachment), fault allocation strongly favors placing most of the liability on that subcontractor (88% in Milwaukee) with the GC bearing a smaller share tied to inspection/oversight failures (10%) and the owner an even smaller residual share (2%). This 3-way GC/sub/owner split is the clearest allocation data point in the sample.
- Where a design professional (architect/engineer) is a co-defendant alongside the developer/GC, the design professional's share of the total recovery is consistently smaller than the developer's/GC's on the same facts — roughly 10% of the total in Grandview ($1M of $10M). This is consistent with architects/engineers generally carrying lower per-project liability exposure than the parties who physically built the defective work, largely because E&O/professional-liability coverage limits are typically much smaller than a GC's CGL policy and because architects are one step removed from the actual construction.
- Direct GC-vs-architect suits over pure design errors (Clark Construction v. Perkins Eastman, Princeton v. TWBTA/Jacobs) tend to resolve confidentially or remain unresolved in public records even when the initial demand is disclosed ($5M and $10.7M respectively) — final recoveries in these two entries are not confirmed, only the amounts sought.

**Statute-of-repose defenses were harder to find with disclosed CRE dollar outcomes than expected.** Nearly every state (30+) has a construction statute of repose (commonly 6-15 years from substantial completion, e.g., 12 years in Pennsylvania under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5536, 10 years in Texas under CPRC § 16.009, 10 years in Florida) that operates as an absolute bar regardless of when the defect was discovered, and public reporting confirms courts do dismiss even severe-defect claims once the repose period runs (e.g., the Pennsylvania Superior Court's 2023 dismissal in *Johnson v. Toll Brothers*, 302 A.3d 1231 — a residential rather than commercial matter, so it was not included as a full dataset entry, but the doctrine it applies is identical for commercial statutes of repose). For the valuation tool, the practical effect is that statute-of-repose defenses do not produce a "settlement amount" data point at all — a successful repose defense simply zeroes out the claim regardless of the underlying defect's severity, which means repose exposure should be modeled as a binary time-bar risk factor (years since substantial completion vs. the applicable state's repose period) rather than as a dollar-scaling variable.

**Insurance coverage disputes rarely disclose the underlying claim's dollar value in public reporting.** Of the three insurance_coverage_defect_dispute entries in this dataset, only one (Mycon v. six CGL insurers) discloses even a damages floor ($1M+) for the underlying facade-failure claim, and none discloses a final coverage settlement amount — coverage litigation tends to resolve the *legal* question (duty to defend/indemnify, exclusion scope) in a published opinion while the dollar consequences flow through confidential settlements downstream. The two 2024 circuit-level rulings in this sample (Admiral v. Tocci, 1st Cir., and Cornice & Rose v. Acuity, 7th Cir.) reached opposite practical outcomes on similar "faulty work" exclusion and duty-to-defend questions, underscoring that coverage outcomes are highly state-law- and policy-language-dependent even on similar facts.

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **Disclosed-dollar bias toward large, newsworthy, or law-firm-marketed cases.** Consistent with the brief's instruction to prioritize real dollar figures over doctrine, this dataset leans on cases picked up by trade press (ENR, Construction Dive, Insurance Business), plaintiffs'-firm press releases (ACTS Law, Yarborough Applegate, Pursiano/Wedbush), and a few landmark catastrophic-failure cases. The large majority of construction-defect claims — particularly small commercial tenant improvement disputes, single-building retail/office envelope claims, and most subcontractor-level indemnification fights — resolve through confidential mediation or arbitration and never generate any public dollar figure at all. The sample is therefore skewed toward the larger, more newsworthy end of the real-world distribution, even though it avoids the "appellate doctrine" skew flagged in the prior lease/eviction research pass.
2. **Several entries reflect an amount sought or a pleading-stage figure, not a confirmed final settlement/judgment** — Mycon v. six CGL insurers ($1M+ sought, pending), Princeton v. TWBTA/Jacobs ($10.7M sought, outcome undisclosed), Clark Construction v. Perkins Eastman ($5M sought, settled confidentially), Aston Martin Residences ($750K pleading minimum, pending), and 443 Greenwich St. ($376M demand vs. ~$100M outside estimate, still litigated). These five are marked in `dollarAmountBasis` and should be treated as directional signals of claim scale, not benchmarks for actual recovery.
3. **Two coverage-doctrine cases (Admiral v. Tocci, Cornice & Rose v. Acuity) carry no dollar figure at all** — they were kept because duty-to-defend/exclusion-scope rulings materially determine whether a contractor or design professional's own insurer will fund defense and indemnity for a defect claim, which is a load-bearing input for the insurance_coverage_defect_dispute claim type even without a settlement number attached.
4. **Some figures could not be tied to a single, precisely verified source.** The Waiea tower figure is reported as either $114.5M or $116.5M across different contemporaneous articles (used the higher, more commonly cited figure); the 88-unit Palm Springs condo case (ACTS Law) does not disclose the property name or defendant names in the available source. Champlain Towers South's $997M is reported as potentially rising to ~$1.1B with property-sale proceeds — the entry uses the $997M court-approved base figure.
5. **Confidence ratings:** 9 of 17 entries are marked "high" confidence (facts and figures cross-checked against a primary source — a court opinion, SEC filing, or detailed law-firm/trade-press writeup with named parties and a verified figure). 8 are marked "medium" — either because the underlying figure is a pleading demand rather than a confirmed outcome, because the source is a single press release without independent corroboration, or because a specific detail (docket number, exact figure) could not be confirmed across multiple sources.
6. **No verdict/settlement database access.** As with the prior lease/eviction research pass, this research used public web search, trade press, and law-firm case-result pages only — it did not have access to Westlaw/Lexis VerdictSearch or Jury Verdict Research, which would surface many more mid-sized, unpublicized construction-defect settlements with disclosed dollar figures (particularly single-tenant retail/office envelope and MEP defect claims, which are underrepresented here relative to condo/residential-conversion defect claims). A production version of this valuation tool would benefit substantially from that kind of paid database access, especially to round out the design_professional_malpractice and indemnification_contribution_claim buckets, which have the fewest entries in this sample.
7. **Jurisdictional coverage is concentrated in FL, NJ, and a handful of other states with active plaintiffs'-firm construction-defect practices and receptive trade press coverage (Florida condo litigation in particular).** Large commercial real estate markets underrepresented or absent from this batch include California (only one, minimally-detailed entry), Illinois, Georgia, and most of the Midwest/Mountain West outside Wisconsin — a gap the valuation tool should account for when weighting jurisdiction-specific benchmarks.
