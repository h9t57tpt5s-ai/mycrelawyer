# Research Summary: Holdover Damages, Attorney's Fees, and Security Deposit Disputes in Commercial Leases

Source data: `holdover_fees_deposit.json` (18 cases: 8 holdover, 5 attorney's-fees, 5 security-deposit)

All cases below were located and verified through public web sources (court opinion PDFs, Justia/FindLaw/Leagle/CourtListener case summaries, and law-firm case analyses that quote the underlying opinions). Every entry includes a source URL. Where a specific dollar figure could not be independently confirmed, `dollarAmount` is `null` rather than estimated.

## A. Holdover Damages (8 cases)

**Pattern:** Commercial leases routinely include a holdover clause fixing rent at 150%–300% (most commonly 200% "double rent" or 300% "treble rent") of the prior rent for any period the tenant overstays the lease term. Courts in the cases found (NY, TX, PA) generally **enforce these clauses as liquidated damages, not penalties** — *ESRT 501 Seventh Ave. v. Regine* (200% rent) and *Khan v. Meknojiya* (Texas, doubled rent) both rejected "unenforceable penalty" arguments. *Victoria's Secret v. Herald Sq. Owner* shows this can scale to enormous dollar figures in high-value retail/office leases (tens of millions of dollars in a Manhattan flagship-store case).

**But recovery is not automatic**, and three of the eight cases found are landlord losses or limitations on the remedy:
- **Consent defeats the penalty.** In *Baca v. Kuang* (CA, 2025) and *Clairton Corp. v. Geo-Con* (PA, 1993), a landlord who accepted rent or acquiesced in continued occupancy after lease expiration was found to have consented to a new month-to-month tenancy — defeating the elevated holdover rent entirely. This is a recurring landlord trap: accepting even one rent payment after a termination notice can forfeit the multiplied-rent remedy.
- **Willfulness/good faith matters.** In *Spatz v. 2263 North Lincoln Corp.* (IL, 2013), Illinois's holdover statute requires the tenant's overstay be "willful" — a tenant with a good-faith, colorable claim to a right of occupancy (there, a disputed purchase option) defeated the double-rent claim.
- **Demand timing matters.** In *Lincoln Oldsmobile v. Branch* (FL, 1990), Florida's double-rent penalty is not self-executing; the landlord must affirmatively demand it, and cannot recover it retroactively for the period before the demand.
- **Washington is the outlier in strictness.** *Queen v. McClung* (WA, 1974) held the state's unlawful-detainer statute (RCW 59.12.170) makes doubling **mandatory**, not discretionary, for all unpaid rent in a commercial unlawful-detainer judgment — a materially more landlord-favorable default rule than the "penalty unless clearly liquidated damages" framework used elsewhere.

**Takeaway for the valuation tool:** A holdover-damages exposure estimate should weight heavily on (1) whether the lease has an express multiplier clause (most jurisdictions require one, or a specific holdover statute, to get more than fair-market holdover rent), (2) whether the landlord accepted any post-termination rent (fatal in several jurisdictions), and (3) whether the tenant has a colorable good-faith basis for remaining (can defeat willfulness-based multipliers).

## B. Attorney's Fees Awards in Commercial Lease Litigation (5 cases)

**Pattern:** Contractual fee-shifting clauses in commercial leases are broadly enforceable (*Oak Park Investment Co. v. Lundy's*, KS, 1981), and some jurisdictions are expanding what they cover — Colorado's *1046 Munras Properties v. Kabod Coffee* (2025) is the state's first published decision allowing "fees on fees" (fees incurred to litigate the fee award itself).

However, the sample here skews toward **cases where fees were reduced, denied, or capped despite a valid contractual clause** — which is likely to be the more decision-relevant fact pattern for a valuation tool, since it shows how far actual recoveries can diverge from what a lease's fee clause nominally promises:
- **Evidentiary proof requirements can wipe out a fee award entirely.** *Rohrmoos Venture v. UTSW DVA Healthcare* (TX, 2019) — the Texas Supreme Court's landmark lodestar case — reversed an $800,000–$1,000,000 fee award because the prevailing party's proof (general testimony about a fee range, without contemporaneous billing records) did not satisfy the lodestar method. This is now the controlling standard for proving fees in Texas contract litigation generally, not just leases.
- **Statutory caps can override actual-fees lease language.** *RadioShack Corp. v. Cascade Crossing II* (GA, 2007) — the Georgia Supreme Court held a statute originally written for promissory notes (O.C.G.A. § 13-1-11) also caps commercial-lease attorney's fees at a small statutory percentage formula, cutting the landlord's actual ~$280,000 in incurred fees down to roughly $17,000.
- **"Prevailing party" is contestable even after a clear damages verdict.** In the companion Georgia appeals *Wells & McElwee, P.C. v. Tiffany & Tomato, Inc.* (2021), the tenant argued it was the "prevailing party" for fee purposes because the landlord lost a separate bad-faith fee claim, even though the landlord won a $45,667 jury verdict on the main breach claim — the court rejected this and denied the tenant's own fee motion.

**Takeaway for the valuation tool:** Do not model "fee award = X% of damages" as a simple ratio. The evidence strongly suggests fee recovery is contingent on (1) rigorous lodestar-style proof (especially in TX and similarly aligned jurisdictions), (2) any applicable statutory caps that can override the lease's fee language (as in Georgia), and (3) a genuine "prevailing party" determination that looks at overall litigation success, not just the isolated claim a party cares about.

## C. Commercial Security Deposit Disputes (5 cases)

**Pattern:** Unlike residential tenancies, **most states have no statute at all governing commercial security deposits** — no cap on amount, no mandated timeline for return, no interest requirement, and (in most states) no prohibition on commingling with the landlord's own funds. This was confirmed directly from a 50-state comparison chart (Akerman LLP / Practical Law, 2020): the overwhelming majority of states impose zero statutory requirements on commercial deposits, leaving the matter entirely to the lease's own terms. *Shwachman v. Khoroshansky* (MA, 1983) is a direct judicial confirmation of this gap — Massachusetts's residential deposit protections (segregation, interest) simply do not extend to commercial tenants.

Where a minority of states (California, New York) *do* regulate commercial deposits by statute, litigation clusters around a few recurring fact patterns:
- **Deposit vs. future/speculative damages.** California's Civil Code § 1950.7 limits a security deposit to covering damages accrued as of the statutory return date — a landlord cannot apply it against anticipated future rent losses (*250 L.L.C. v. PhotoPoint Corp.*, 2005).
- **Deposit vs. repairs without express lease authorization.** Also under § 1950.7(c), a California commercial landlord can only apply the deposit to repairs if the lease specifically says so (*Aljabban v. Fontana Indoor Swap Meet*, 2020 — unpublished, included only as an illustrative fact pattern, not binding precedent).
- **Technical statutory violations without actual damages yield no remedy.** New York's GOL § 7-103 requires deposits be held at a New York bank branch; in *Urban Soccer Inc. v. Royal Wine Corp.* (2016), the landlord violated this but the tenant recovered nothing because there was no commingling and no actual harm — the court called it a "technical" violation.
- **A deposit-return win can be a Pyrrhic victory.** In *Oak Forest Properties LLC v. RER Financial* (IL, 2018 — unpublished Rule 23 order, included as an illustrative, verifiable fact pattern), the tenant recovered its full $3,403.60 deposit but was denied "prevailing party" fee-shifting status because the deposit claim was minor relative to the much larger contract claims litigated in the same two-week trial.

**Takeaway for the valuation tool:** In most jurisdictions, a commercial-tenant security-deposit claim's value depends almost entirely on the **lease's own language**, not a protective statute — the opposite baseline assumption from residential tools. Even in the minority of regulated states, courts read the statutes narrowly (limited to accrued-as-of-date damages, or express authorization for specific deductions) and require proof of actual harm before awarding a remedy for a technical violation.

## Data Limitations (read before using this data in the valuation product)

1. **Sample size fell short of the 8–10 target in two categories.** Attorney's fees (5) and security deposit (5) categories have fewer verified cases than requested. Additional research time (a fresh web-search budget) would be needed to round these out with more jurisdictional diversity; I prioritized verifiability over hitting the numeric target, per instructions.
2. **Two entries are formally unpublished/nonprecedential opinions** (*Aljabban v. Fontana Indoor Swap Meet*, CA; *Oak Forest Properties LLC v. RER Financial*, IL Rule 23 order). Both are real, verifiable cases with real facts and dollar figures, but under their states' court rules they may not be cited as binding precedent. They are flagged with `"confidence": "medium"` and an explicit note; the valuation tool should either exclude them from any "citable authority" list or clearly label them as illustrative fact patterns only.
3. **Several dollar figures represent amounts *sought* or *initially awarded before reversal/reduction*, not final collected amounts** (e.g., Rohrmoos's $800K fee figure was reversed; RadioShack's $280K sought was capped to ~$17K by statute). The `dollarAmountBasis` field on each entry specifies exactly what the number represents — do not treat `dollarAmount` alone as "money actually collected" without reading the basis field.
4. **The Victoria's Secret / Herald Square litigation is procedurally complex**, with multiple partial payments, a separate "Office Judgment Amount," and ongoing related litigation over the same property (a 2025 suit by TIAA/Norges Bank against SL Green over an unrelated $24M rent default at the same address). The figure used here ($19.9M combined) is the trial court's initial holdover-damages calculation under the lease's treble-rent clause — verify current docket status before quoting it as a final, collected number.
5. **No case in this set involved a jury-trial commercial security-deposit verdict with a large, clean dollar figure** — the deposit disputes located were resolved on summary judgment, bench trial, or statutory-interpretation grounds with either no stated total or a modest one (under $5,000 in two of the five cases). This likely reflects the underlying reality that commercial security deposits are typically smaller relative to the overall lease value than the primary damages claims that accompany them, so they rarely drive standalone, high-value litigation — a useful data point in itself for the valuation model (deposit disputes tend to ride alongside larger breach claims rather than being litigated as standalone high-dollar cases).
6. **Jurisdictional coverage:** Holdover — NY, CA, FL, IL, WA, TX, PA (7 states). Fees — TX, KS, IL, GA (x2), CO (5 states, GA appears twice with different cases). Deposit — CA (x2), IL, NY, MA (4 states). No case from every state was sought or is claimed; this is a sample for pattern illustration, not an exhaustive 50-state survey.
7. **One promising lead was discarded as unverifiable/likely fabricated.** A search result referenced "*2540 Main Street Plaza, LLC v. Jiu-Jitsu Corp.*," Colorado Court of Appeals, purportedly awarding $89,950 in fees under Civil Code § 1717 — this citation could not be independently confirmed and the underlying address appears to correspond to an unrelated real business (a jiu-jitsu gym), suggesting the search engine's AI summary conflated unrelated facts into a fictitious case. It was excluded entirely per the "never invent a case" instruction, and is flagged here so it is not reintroduced later by another research pass pulling from the same search snippet.
