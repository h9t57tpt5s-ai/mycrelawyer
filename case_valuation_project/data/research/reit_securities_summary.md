# REIT & Real-Estate Securities Litigation — Research Summary

16 cases collected (target was 15-20), 12 of which have a real, disclosed dollar settlement/judgment/penalty figure and 4 of which are kept as "principle only" entries because they establish load-bearing doctrine even without a dollar outcome. All four in-scope claim types are represented: securities fraud under Rule 10b-5 (6 cases), breach of fiduciary duty / shareholder derivative suits against REIT sponsors, advisors, or boards (5 cases), proxy-disclosure claims tied to non-traded REIT liquidity-event or internalization votes (3 cases), and merger-objection suits (3 cases). Defendants span the full spectrum from a $20B-market-cap listed net-lease REIT (ARCP/VEREIT) down to small non-traded REIT sponsors (Luminent, RAIT), plus REIT-affiliated third parties (an auditor, a REIT sponsor's broker-dealer channel) reachable under an aiding/primary-violator theory. Courts cluster heavily in the Southern District of New York and the Circuit Court for Baltimore City, Maryland — unsurprising given how many non-traded REIT sponsors (AR Global/AR Capital, RMR, Wells/Piedmont) are Maryland-incorporated and litigate in New York or Maryland as a result.

## Patterns observed

**Dollar figures cluster into three very different tiers depending on defendant type and claim theory:**
- **Nine-and-ten-figure recoveries** are reserved for accounting-fraud class actions against the REIT issuer itself where a restatement or admitted misstatement drove a large market-cap decline: In re American Realty Capital Properties, Inc. Litigation (VEREIT/ARCP) settled for **$1.025 billion** in 2020 — one of the largest securities class action recoveries of any kind, not just in real estate — because it combined a listed REIT with a ~36% one-day stock drop, a criminally-convicted CFO, and a co-liable auditor (Grant Thornton, $49M) and external manager (AR Capital/AR Global entities, ~$225-286.5M).
- **Seven-and-eight-figure recoveries** are the norm for everything else with a real cash component: RAIT Financial Trust ($32M, 10b-5), Quinn v. Knight/Apple REIT Ten derivative ($32M, self-dealing merger), Milliken v. Hospitality Investors Trust derivative ($15.2M, related-party fee conduct), W.P. Carey SEC settlement ($30M, undisclosed broker compensation), Piedmont/Wells REIT proxy-disclosure settlement ($7.5M combined), Moses v. Apple Hospitality REIT DRIP claim ($5.5M), United Development Funding SEC settlement ($8.2M), and Luminent Mortgage Capital ($8M). A very rough rule of thumb: a REIT-issuer 10b-5 claim with a clean stock-drop-on-disclosure fact pattern and no criminal conduct alongside it tends to settle in the high-single-digit to low-nine-figure range; add a criminal conviction, an auditor co-defendant, or a controlling-shareholder self-dealing angle and the number jumps by an order of magnitude or more.
- **Five- and six-figure "settlements"** show up in a cluster of merger-objection and governance-driven fiduciary-duty suits that did not really recover damages at all — Cole Credit Property Trust III ($100K, a mootness/appeal-dismissal fee reimbursement after the underlying claim was dismissed with prejudice) and the CommonWealth REIT/Equity Commonwealth trustee litigation ($200K, a cost reimbursement paid after an activist proxy fight had already achieved the real remedy of replacing the entire board). These are useful negative data points: they show that the *existence* of a filed REIT fiduciary-duty or merger-objection suit is a weak predictor of a real dollar recovery on its own.

**Proxy-disclosure claims resolve very differently depending on what exactly was allegedly concealed:**
- Where the complaint can point to a *specific, quantifiable, undisclosed insider financial stake* in the outcome of the vote — the Lightstone REIT charter-amendment case ($59.8M in subordinated participation interests the chairman held, allegedly undisclosed) and the Piedmont/Wells REIT internalization-vote case ($175M internalization payment to insiders) — the claim survives a motion to dismiss (Lightstone) or draws a real cash settlement (Piedmont/Wells, $7.5M).
- Where the complaint instead alleges only a *generic, industry-wide risk* that the company arguably already disclosed in general terms (e.g., "internalization could cause the stock to trade at a discount," in St. Clair-Hibbard v. American Finance Trust), courts are much more willing to dismiss for failure to plead materiality — the Second Circuit held in 2020 that a REIT's boilerplate conflict-of-interest and trading-discount warnings can be enough to defeat a proxy claim even without quantifying the risk, especially where analysts and rating agencies had already flagged the same generic risk publicly.
- Straight merger-objection suits challenging routine M&A proxy disclosures (the Government Properties Income Trust/Select Income REIT merger, which drew four nearly-simultaneous duplicate suits in three different courts) follow the well-known "disclosure-only settlement" pattern seen across all of M&A litigation, not just REITs: the company adds supplemental proxy disclosures, the suits get mooted, and — especially post-Trulia — courts have grown skeptical of paying plaintiffs' counsel a "mootness fee" for it. This claim type should be modeled as high-frequency but low-dollar-value litigation risk.

**Breach-of-fiduciary-duty derivative suits recover real money almost exclusively when tied to a concrete, quantifiable self-dealing transaction, not a general governance complaint:**
- Big derivative recoveries (Quinn v. Knight, $32M; Inland Western, ~$90M in forfeited stock; Hospitality Investors Trust, $15.2M) all involved a specific, identifiable transaction that benefited insiders — an internalization at an inflated price, a merger allegedly timed and structured to enrich the founder, or a management-fee/related-party arrangement.
- Governance-complaint-style derivative suits that piggyback on an already-successful activist proxy contest (CommonWealth REIT/Equity Commonwealth) or a self-dealing fee allegation that gets resolved through a voting/cooperation agreement instead of litigation (Blackwells Capital v. Global Net Lease/Necessity Retail REIT, alleging $838M in excess management fees over time) tend to settle for governance changes plus a nominal fee reimbursement, with no disclosed cash recovery to the company or shareholders.

**Aiding-and-abetting exposure for REIT-affiliated third parties is real but secondary to the primary defendant's exposure:** in the ARCP/VEREIT settlement, the auditor (Grant Thornton, $49M) and the external manager/sponsor entities (AR Capital/AR Global, ~$225-286.5M) both paid meaningfully, but both amounts were a fraction of what the REIT issuer itself paid ($738.5M) — auditors and sponsors are reachable co-defendants, not primary payors, in the fact patterns surveyed here.

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **The Stanford Securities Class Action Clearinghouse — the primary source this task specifically directed toward — was inaccessible for the duration of this research.** Every direct attempt to fetch securities.stanford.edu (the filings database, individual case pages) returned an HTTP 403, and a web search independently surfaced a notice that the site is "under construction... as of August 2026, with expectations to return in Winter 2026." Only one entry in this dataset (Luminent Mortgage Capital) traces back to a Stanford SCAC case page, and even that was sourced through a search-engine cache/summary rather than a direct fetch of the primary page, so it is marked "medium" rather than "high" confidence. This is a meaningful gap relative to the task's instructions: a rerun of this research after the Clearinghouse returns online should be expected to surface additional REIT securities class-action settlements (particularly smaller mortgage-REIT and non-traded-REIT cases from the 2007-2012 credit-crisis era) that this pass could not find through general web search alone.
2. **Confidentiality and non-disclosure skew the sample toward the largest, most newsworthy, or SEC-adjacent matters.** Most REIT fiduciary-duty and proxy-disclosure disputes are non-traded-REIT investor grievances resolved through individual FINRA arbitration (not court litigation) or confidential settlement, neither of which generates a public dollar figure; this dataset can only see the subset that became public class/derivative litigation or drew SEC enforcement action, which likely skews toward more sophisticated plaintiffs' firms, larger REIT sponsors, and bigger-dollar disputes than the median non-traded-REIT investor complaint.
3. **Four entries carry no dollar figure at all** (St. Clair-Hibbard v. American Finance Trust, In re Lightstone REIT Proxy Litigation, Blackwells Capital v. Global Net Lease/Necessity Retail REIT, and the Government Properties Income Trust/Select Income REIT merger litigation). Two of these (St. Clair-Hibbard, Government Properties/Select Income) were kept because they illustrate a genuine "no real money changes hands" outcome that is itself a data point for the valuation tool — not every filed REIT proxy or merger-objection suit should be assumed to carry settlement value. The other two (Lightstone, Blackwells) are still-live or resolved-without-damages matters kept for their doctrinal value and because Lightstone was specifically flagged as an anchor case for this research.
4. **Two "settlements" are effectively fee reimbursements, not damages recoveries** — Cole Credit Property Trust III ($100,000) and CommonWealth REIT/Equity Commonwealth ($200,000). These are real, disclosed, sourced figures, but including them without this caveat would understate how little value the underlying claims actually recovered; they are flagged explicitly in each entry's notes and in the "Patterns observed" section above.
5. **Some dollar figures are aggregates across multiple settling defendants or a mix of cash and non-cash consideration**, which matters if the valuation tool wants a single defendant's exposure rather than total litigation exposure. The ARCP/VEREIT $1.025 billion figure spans four separately-settling defendant groups (issuer, manager/sponsor, auditor, individual CFO); the Inland Western $90 million figure is the valuation of forfeited stock at the time of the original transaction, not a cash payment; the Hospitality Investors Trust figure excludes a separately-awarded ~$2.25 million in plaintiffs' fees. Each entry's `dollarAmountBasis` field specifies exactly what the number represents.
6. **No verdict/settlement database or paid litigation-analytics platform access.** This research used public web search, SEC EDGAR full-text search results, and general news/law-firm coverage only — it did not have access to Westlaw/Lexis, Bloomberg Law's litigation analytics, Cornerstone Research's securities litigation databases, or paid M&A litigation trackers (e.g., ISS Securities Class Action Services) that would surface a denser sample of REIT-sector securities settlements, particularly smaller ones and ones from the 2000s credit-crisis era that no longer generate active web coverage. A production version of this valuation tool would benefit substantially from that kind of paid database access, and even more so once the Stanford SCAC comes back online given its explicit role as the intended primary source for this category.
7. **Time-period concentration.** The dataset skews toward two clusters: the 2007-2012 non-traded-REIT internalization/self-dealing wave (Inland Western, Piedmont/Wells REIT, Cole Credit Property Trust III) and the 2014-2020 AR Global/AR Capital-affiliated REIT scandal wave (ARCP/VEREIT, Hospitality Investors Trust, St. Clair-Hibbard, Blackwells v. GNL/Necessity Retail). Relatively few cases from 2000-2006 or 2021-2025 were surfaced in the time available; a more exhaustive pass should specifically target those gaps.
