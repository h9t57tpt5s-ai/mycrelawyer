# Unpaid Rent & Accelerated/Future Rent — Research Summary

16 cases collected (target was 15-20). 13 states/jurisdictions represented: CA (2), GA (3), OH, MA, IN, VA, TX, NY, IL, NC, CO, AZ, MD. Property types span retail (shopping centers, restaurants), fitness/health clubs, office, and industrial/warehouse. Tenant sizes range from small independent operators (a bagel shop, a single-restaurant franchisee, a real-estate brokerage) to national chains (Ross Dress for Less, Fitness International/LA Fitness-affiliated entities).

## Patterns observed

**Dollar figures, where disclosed, cluster around a few situations:**
- Straight unpaid-rent claims (money already accrued, no acceleration) tend to be modest — five to six figures (SVAP III Poway Crossings: ~$520K over several months of COVID closure; Sylva Shops v. Hibbard: ~$35.5K for a bagel shop's remaining 5-year term; Austin Hill Country Realty: ~$29.7K jury verdict).
- True accelerated/future-rent claims for the *entire remaining term* of a long lease scale directly with (a) monthly rent, (b) years remaining, and (c) whether mitigation/re-letting credit is deducted. The two largest awards in the sample — CSRA Columbus ($6.6M total / $4.6M specifically accelerated) and CPUS Anson v. Tradewinds ($3.5M, later remanded) — both involved multi-year industrial/fitness leases with several years remaining at default.
- A very rough rule of thumb across the sample: accelerated-rent awards approximate (monthly rent × months remaining), sometimes reduced by a mitigation/re-letting credit, sometimes not reduced at all where the lease validly waived mitigation (Cummings v. Hines, CSRA Columbus, Bistro Manila, Sylva Shops on remand).

**Acceleration/liquidated-damages clauses are enforced more often than not, but not automatically:**
- Enforced as written: Cummings Properties v. Hines (MA, 2023 — reversing an intermediate appellate court that had struck the same type of clause down as a penalty just a year earlier), CSRA Columbus v. Fitness & Sports Clubs (OH, 2025), Bistro Manila v. Alvah I (VA, 2025), Fifty States Management v. Pioneer Auto Parks (NY, 1979 — the oldest and most-cited case in the sample), Sylva Shops v. Hibbard (NC, 2006, on remand), Chopo Chicken (IL, 2022, when properly framed as liquidated damages).
- Struck down or reduced as an unenforceable penalty: Peterson v. P.C. Towers (GA, 1992) and Nobles v. Jiffy Market (GA, 2003) — both because the clause made no attempt to account for future market/re-letting value, and in Nobles because it accelerated an extreme 18 years of rent at once. Grand Prospect Partners v. Ross Dress for Less (CA, 2015) similarly struck a rent-abatement penalty provision while upholding a related termination provision.
- The single biggest predictor of enforceability across the sample is **whether the clause/lease explicitly addresses mitigation and reasonableness at the time of signing** ("single look" doctrine) rather than after the fact. Clauses that are silent on mitigation, or that accelerate an unreasonably long remaining term without any relet-credit mechanism, are the ones courts strike down.

**Mitigation is the recurring battleground, and states split on the default rule:**
- Texas (Austin Hill Country Realty, 1997), Colorado (Schneiker v. Gordon, 1987), and Arizona (Tempe Corporate Office Building, 1991) all impose a default common-law duty on commercial landlords to make reasonable efforts to re-let, unless the lease says otherwise.
- Ohio (CSRA Columbus, applying Frenchtown Square Partnership v. Lemstone) and North Carolina (Sylva Shops) both allow sophisticated commercial parties to *contractually waive* that duty, and courts in this sample enforced such waivers even where the landlord admittedly made little or no effort to re-let (CSRA Columbus: asked above-market rent and failed to re-let for 21 months; landlord still recovered the full accelerated amount).
- Where a landlord did re-let and mitigation was applied, the offset/timing of the credit itself became a source of appellate error — CPUS Anson v. Tradewinds (IN, 2026) was remanded specifically because the trial court applied the replacement tenant's rent credit at the wrong point in the interest/damages calculation, not because mitigation was legally required.

**Bankruptcy-adjacent context (not separately cited above but worth flagging for the valuation tool):** several of the underlying leases in this sample (and in the broader case law) involve tenant bankruptcy or de facto insolvency driving the default. Note that federal bankruptcy law caps a commercial landlord's claim for future/accelerated rent damages at the greater of one year's rent or 15% of the remaining rent (not to exceed 3 years), under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(6), whenever the tenant's lease rejection happens inside a Chapter 11/7 proceeding. This statutory cap is not itself a "case," but it materially changes exposure math whenever the disputant tenant is (or may become) a bankruptcy debtor, and should be flagged in the valuation tool as a hard ceiling that supersedes whatever the lease's acceleration clause says.

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **Confidentiality skews the sample toward litigated, appealed disputes.** The large majority of commercial lease defaults are resolved by negotiated settlement, forbearance agreement, or a simple default judgment/eviction with no reported opinion — none of which show up in public case law. This dataset necessarily reflects the tail of disputes where a party thought a legal issue (usually clause enforceability or mitigation) was worth appealing, which likely skews toward larger-dollar, more sophisticated-party, or more legally contested cases than the median real-world dispute.
2. **Several entries are "principle only" with no disclosed dollar figure** (Fifty States Management, Southeastern Land Fund, Peterson v. P.C. Towers, Nobles v. Jiffy Market, Schneiker v. Gordon, Tempe Corporate Office Building, Chopo Chicken, Copinol Restaurant) — 7 of 16 cases. These were kept because they establish load-bearing legal doctrine (the enforceability test itself, the mitigation default rule, procedural traps) that materially affects how much of a stated accelerated-rent claim a landlord will actually collect, even though they can't anchor a dollar-figure benchmark on their own.
3. **Not all figures are "final."** The CPUS Anson v. Tradewinds figure ($3,559,407.17) is a vacated trial-court number sent back for recalculation — it illustrates exposure magnitude for a multi-year industrial lease default, not a confirmed final judgment. The Sylva Shops figure required distinguishing the (incorrect) $13,110 jury verdict from the (correct, appellate-endorsed) $35,511.70 full-damages figure.
4. **Verification depth varies.** Four cases (Grand Prospect Partners, Cummings v. Hines, SVAP III Poway Crossings, CSRA Columbus) were checked against detailed case-summary extractions or, in CSRA Columbus's case, the full primary opinion text, and are marked "high" confidence. The remaining twelve rely on secondary summaries (law firm client alerts, case-brief sites, news coverage) of the underlying opinions and are marked "medium" confidence — the citations themselves are real and independently cross-referenced across multiple sources, but the narrative facts/figures were not verified against the full primary opinion text in every instance.
5. **No verdict/settlement database access.** This research used public web search and case-summary sites only; it did not have access to Westlaw/Lexis verdict-and-settlement databases (e.g., VerdictSearch, Jury Verdict Research) that would surface many more unappealed trial-level unpaid-rent judgments with disclosed dollar amounts. A production version of this valuation tool would benefit from that kind of paid database access to build a denser, less appeal-skewed sample.
6. **Jurisdictional coverage is broad but not exhaustive.** 13 states are represented; large commercial real estate markets not covered in this batch include New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Nevada, Washington, and Florida (a Florida search returned only statutory/procedural material, no citable case with a disclosed dollar figure, in the time available).
