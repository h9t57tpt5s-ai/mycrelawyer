# Lending & Foreclosure — Research Summary

19 cases collected (target was 15-20). 10 of 19 carry a disclosed, adjudicated or stipulated dollar figure (judgment, deficiency, or final sale/auction outcome); the remaining 9 are real, sourced, currently-pending or receivership-only matters where no dollar judgment has been entered yet, consistent with the task's own allowance that a pure receivership grant/denial often carries no dollar figure at all. 8 states/jurisdictions represented: Michigan (3), New Jersey, Tennessee (6th Cir.), California, Ohio, Florida (4), Colorado, Illinois (2), Virginia, Pennsylvania, New York (3). Property types span retail/shopping centers, downtown office towers, a hospitality/resort asset, a mixed-use marina/condo project, and a restaurant-franchise portfolio.

Coverage leans on two complementary source types, per the task's instruction to prioritize disclosed dollar outcomes over doctrine-heavy appellate opinions:
- **CREdocket's own tracked "lending-foreclosure" matters** (5 entries below — Republic Plaza, Steinway Tower, Via Mizner/Mandarin Oriental, Bank Midwest/Integritty Group) were used as verified starting points per the task instructions. Most of these are very recent (2025-2026) and still pending, so they mostly lack final dollar outcomes, but they're included because they illustrate live enforcement mechanics (receivership requests, UCC Article 9 sales, lender-liability counter-suits) with real, current dollar exposure figures even before final resolution.
- **Independent research into real estate trade press** (The Real Deal, Richmond BizSense, Crain's New York Business, Commercial Real Estate Direct, Bisnow, local business journals) surfaced the majority of the dollar-figure entries — recent (2024-2026) foreclosure judgments, receivership grants/denials, and one dramatic failed-auction outcome (750 Lexington Avenue).
- **Classic reported "bad boy" guaranty and lender-liability appellate opinions** (Cherryland Mall, Princeton Park, Gratiot Avenue, K.M.C. v. Irving Trust, Barrett v. Bank of America) fill in the guaranty-enforcement and lender-liability buckets, since recent examples of those two claim types are less consistently covered by trade press than straight foreclosure/receivership stories.

## Patterns observed

**Foreclosure deficiency judgments track loan balance plus accrued interest/fees, and the eventual recovery against that judgment varies enormously by asset quality:**
- On distressed but still-functioning assets, stipulated or agreed foreclosure judgments (AFF IV 200 Miami v. Stonerock Capital: $65.7M judgment on $41.1M principal) tend to track the loan balance closely, since the borrower isn't contesting the underlying default — only the auction/receivership mechanics.
- On severely impaired assets, the gap between judgment and actual recovery can be dramatic. 750 Lexington Avenue's $155.9M judgment corresponded to a building whose appraised value had fallen 86% (from ~$300M to ~$41M); the foreclosure auction drew zero bids and the property reverted to the lender for a nominal $1,000. The KeyBank Tower in Columbus (Ardent v. Baruch Broad Street) saw a $9.3M claimed debt produce only a $5.1M auction sale (roughly 45% recovery) on a building that had cost $11.8M to finance and $12M to buy just two years earlier.
- Lender-advanced protective payments (property taxes, insurance) get folded into the final judgment and can meaningfully inflate it beyond the original loan balance — in Hillsboro Beach Resort (Emerald Creek Capital), a $26M loan plus roughly $2.9M in lender tax advances became a $40M final judgment.
- Privately-financed (non-institutional, non-CMBS) foreclosures can take dramatically longer to reach final judgment than CMBS special-servicer-driven foreclosures. The Karlton v. JB Green judgment ($8.7M) followed a four-year litigation battle on a privately-financed loan, while several of the CMBS-driven receivership/foreclosure matters in this sample moved from default to receiver appointment in a matter of months.

**Receivership is granted far more often than denied in this sample, and the exceptions are instructive:**
- Of the six receivership_dispute entries, five resulted in a receiver being appointed (Republic Plaza pending decision; 625 N. Michigan Ave., Riverfront Plaza, One Parkway North Blvd./Deerfield, and Adelaide Pointe all had receivers appointed). The lone denial — Independent Bank v. Adelaide Pointe — was only temporary: the court denied the bank's first motion as premature (holding that a contractual, pre-default "as of right" receivership clause does not override judicial discretion when there are genuine factual disputes over collateral value, project viability, or whether an uncured default exists), then granted a renewed motion three months later once those factual disputes had been developed further.
- The typical fact pattern driving a granted receivership in this sample is occupancy/income decline on an office asset (anchor tenant departure, falling debt-service-coverage ratio) colliding with a loan maturity the borrower can't refinance — not fraud, waste, or borrower misconduct. Several of the granted receiverships here (625 N. Michigan Ave., Republic Plaza) involve institutionally sophisticated owners (Brookfield, MetLife, Golub & Company), underscoring that receivership in the current office-distress cycle is largely a function of loan structure and market conditions rather than sponsor quality.
- Receivership is consistently sought and obtained well before, and independent of, any completed foreclosure sale — it is the lender's fast, operational-control-first tool, with the foreclosure/sale process (often another 6-18+ months) following later. Several matters in this sample (Riverfront Plaza, Grant Building/Pittsburgh) remain in receivership for a year or more without a final sale.

**Guaranty enforcement ("bad boy" carve-out) litigation is where lenders are most likely to obtain the full loan balance, not just collateral value — and courts have generally sided with lenders on enforceability:**
- All three of the guaranty-enforcement appellate cases in this sample (Cherryland Mall, Princeton Park, Gratiot Avenue) resulted in the guarantor being held personally liable for the full deficiency, even though none involved classic bad-boy conduct like fraud or intentional waste — all three were "springing" carve-outs triggered by a technical covenant breach (insolvency, or taking on unauthorized subordinate debt that was later cured). This is the single most important contrast with straight (non-recourse) foreclosure: once a guaranty carve-out is triggered, the guarantor's personal exposure is the full note deficiency, not merely the shortfall the lender couldn't recover from the property.
- The Cherryland decision was significant enough to prompt a legislative response (Michigan's Non-Recourse Mortgage Loan Act), but that fix was prospective only and didn't disturb the judgment against the guarantor in Cherryland itself or in the nearly-identical Gratiot Avenue case decided the same year.
- The dollar figures in this sample's guaranty cases ($2.1M, $5.2M, $12.2M) scale with the size of the underlying loan, not with the severity of the triggering conduct — a $400K unauthorized loan that was fully repaid seven months later (Princeton Park) produced the same full-recourse outcome as outright insolvency (Cherryland, Gratiot Avenue).

**Lender-liability claims brought by borrowers succeed far less often than guaranty-enforcement claims brought by lenders, and tend to require either (a) an explicit notice/discretion failure or (b) allegations that the lender manufactured the default it's now enforcing against:**
- The two older, decided lender-liability cases in this sample (K.M.C. v. Irving Trust, Barrett v. Bank of America) both involved a jury finding the lender liable for exceeding its proper role — K.M.C. for cutting off a credit line without notice despite retaining contractual discretion to do so, and Barrett for exercising improper day-to-day control over a construction project. Both produced substantial ($6.6M-$7.5M) verdicts, but both are decades old (1985-1986) and reflect an earlier, more borrower-friendly era of lender-liability law; more recent lender-liability suits face a steeper uphill climb.
- The most doctrinally significant recent lender-liability development in this sample — 111 West 57th Investment v. 111 W57 Mezz Investor (Steinway Tower, NY Court of Appeals, 2026) — did not award any damages; it merely reinstated a good-faith claim and remanded for further proceedings, nearly nine years after the disputed 2017 loan sale and UCC foreclosure. This reflects how slowly lender-liability claims move relative to guaranty-enforcement or foreclosure cases, and how a "win" at the pleading/summary-judgment stage is not itself a dollar outcome.
- The live Via Mizner Owner III v. Via Mizner Lender 1 (Mandarin Oriental, Boca Raton) countersuit shows the newest variant of this claim type: a borrower alleging the lender manufactured the very default cited to justify a fast-track UCC Article 9 equity-pledge foreclosure. Its practical value so far has been procedural (a temporary restraining order delaying the UCC sale by about six weeks) rather than monetary.

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **Confidentiality and recency both skew this sample.** Foreclosure and receivership filings are recent, public-record-driven trade-press stories; many are still pending (no final dollar judgment yet), which is why 9 of 19 entries carry a null `dollarAmount`. A production version of this tool should periodically re-check the sourced matters (especially Republic Plaza, Riverfront Plaza, Grant Building, Adelaide Pointe, Steinway Tower, Via Mizner, and Bank Midwest/Integritty Group) for follow-on dollar resolutions as they occur.
2. **The guaranty-enforcement and lender-liability buckets rely more heavily on older, doctrinally significant appellate opinions (1985-2011) than on recent trade-press-reported settlements**, because recent guaranty and lender-liability disputes are less consistently covered by real estate trade press than straight foreclosure/receivership auctions and judgments (which generate concrete, newsworthy dollar figures at every stage). This is the opposite skew from the prior lease-dispute research pass, but it means the guaranty/lender-liability dollar figures in this sample may be less representative of a "median" 2024-2026 outcome than the foreclosure/receivership figures are.
3. **One entry (K.M.C. Co. v. Irving Trust) is not a real-estate-secured loan** — the underlying collateral was accounts receivable and inventory on a grocery wholesaler's working-capital line, not commercial real property. It was kept because it is the foundational precedent for the implied-covenant-of-good-faith theory that recurs throughout CRE lender-liability litigation (including the Steinway Tower case in this same sample), and it is one of the only pre-2000 lender-liability cases with a clean, disclosed dollar verdict, but it should be weighted cautiously as a CRE-specific benchmark.
4. **Some dollar figures represent amounts *sought* rather than amounts finally adjudicated or collected.** The Ardent Companies v. Baruch Broad Street (KeyBank Tower) figure of $9,320,807 is the amount sought in the foreclosure complaint's prayer for relief, not a confirmed entered judgment amount — though the subsequent $5.1M auction sale price is independently confirmed. Riverfront Plaza's $131M figure and Via Mizner's $500M+ figure are similarly complaint-stage figures, not adjudicated amounts, which is why those entries carry a null `dollarAmount` rather than the sought figure.
5. **Case citations for some very recent trade-press-only matters (Ardent v. Baruch Broad Street, Adelaide Pointe, several receivership entries) could not be independently verified against a court-docket case number** in the time available; these are sourced to multiple converging news reports (NBC4 Columbus, AOL/Yahoo, Columbus Underground for the KeyBank Tower matter; WZZM13, WOODTV, and Yahoo Finance for Adelaide Pointe) but the docket number itself was not confirmed against a court records system.
6. **No verdict/settlement database access.** This research used public web search and trade-press sources only; it did not have access to Westlaw/Lexis verdict-and-settlement databases or PACER/state court e-filing systems that would surface additional, non-newsworthy foreclosure deficiency judgments and confirm exact docket numbers and entered-judgment dates with certainty.
7. **A note on the Barrett v. Bank of America figure:** the $6.6M reported in secondary sources is confirmed as the jury's punitive damages verdict; the separately-tried compensatory damages amount, and the case's final disposition after the Court of Appeal's remand on an unrelated jury-instruction issue, were not confirmed in the sources reviewed. Treat the $6.6M figure as a partial (punitive-only) data point, not the case's total value.
8. **Jurisdictional coverage skews toward states with an active 2025-2026 CRE distress cycle** (Florida, Illinois, Michigan) plus a handful of doctrinally important older cases from New Jersey, Tennessee/6th Circuit, and California. Large CRE markets not well represented in this batch include Texas, Georgia, and most of the Northeast outside New York.
