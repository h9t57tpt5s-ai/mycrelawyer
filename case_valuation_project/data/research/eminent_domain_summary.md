# Eminent Domain & Takings — Research Summary

20 cases collected (target was 15-20). 10 states/jurisdictions represented: CA (4), TX (4), VA (2), plus NC, CO, OH, ND, SC, MD, FL, and 3 U.S. Supreme Court / federal cases with nationwide reach (AR-origin, TX-origin, CA-origin). Property types span retail (a national chain's access point, a shopping-center-adjacent produce market), industrial (billboards/outdoor advertising leaseholds, an industrial building pair, a heavy-manufacturing headquarters), a medical office building, a nonprofit/institutional building (a veterans post), commercial reserve/development land along highway corridors, and utility/pipeline rights-of-way crossing commercial and agricultural land. Claim-type distribution: 9 just_compensation_valuation, 3 quick_take_challenge, 4 pre_condemnation_access_dispute, 4 regulatory_taking.

## Patterns observed

**Just-compensation trial/settlement awards routinely land well above the condemning authority's initial offer or deposit — often by 2x to 50x:**
- The gap is smallest in disputes with objectively comparable market sales (NC DOT v. AJA Investments: $1.15M settlement vs. $693.8K initial offer, a 66% increase) and largest in access/severance-damage and business-value disputes where the agency's offer methodology and the owner's methodology start from fundamentally different premises (VDOT's Fairfax County retailer case: $5.6M jury verdict vs. a $115,000 offer, nearly 50x; State v. Gleannloch Commercial Development: $19.4M jury verdict, 292% above TxDOT's offer; Inglewood VFW Post 2122 v. LA Metro: $3.36M vs. a $650K offer).
- A rough rule of thumb across the just-compensation sample: routine land/building-value disputes (comparable-sales-driven) settle in the 1.5x-2x range above the initial offer; disputes involving severance/access damages, lost business value, or specialized-use improvements (billboards, medical buildings, a veterans post) show far wider and less predictable spreads, sometimes 3x-50x the initial offer, because there is no single accepted valuation methodology for the disputed component.
- City of North Canton v. Julius Brown, LLC (OH, 2025) is a striking outlier showing the other direction: the condemning city's own valuation of the improvements was $0, and a first-trial jury actually returned a $0 verdict for the buildings before the appellate court reversed on an evidentiary ruling (exclusion of county tax-assessment evidence) and remanded — a reminder that agency lowball positions can, without a strong evidentiary record, actually stick at trial.

**Quick-take procedures create a distinct and separately litigated risk: agencies can occupy commercial property on a small fraction of what a jury later says it's worth, and the gap itself becomes a second fight.** Medical Acquisition Co. v. Tri-City Healthcare District (CA, 2018) is the clearest example: a $4.7M quick-take deposit against a jury-determined $16.83M value (3.6x), which then triggered a separate appellate dispute over what bonding/security conditions could be imposed on withdrawing the increased post-judgment deposit. LA County MTA v. Alameda Produce Market (CA, 2011) shows the same dynamic playing out over seven years, plus establishes that withdrawing a quick-take deposit does not waive a right-to-take challenge — meaning an owner does not have to choose between needed cash now and continuing to fight the taking's legality.

**Right-to-take and blight-designation challenges are the one pattern in this set that can zero out an already-adjudicated compensation award entirely, rather than just raise or lower it.** PKO Ventures v. Norfolk Redevelopment & Housing Authority (VA, 2013) is the standout: a jury had already valued the taking at roughly $3.4-3.75 million, but the Virginia Supreme Court's finding that the area was not validly "blighted" voided the taking altogether and returned the property, mooting the dollar figure completely. This is a materially different outcome shape than every other case in this set and should be flagged in the valuation tool as a distinct "full defense" branch, separate from valuation-dispute upside/downside.

**Pre-condemnation survey/access disputes essentially never carry a compensation figure, because that's not what's being litigated** — the fight is over whether the entity has eminent-domain/common-carrier authority at all (Texas Rice Land Partners v. Denbury Green Pipeline, TX 2012) or whether it can force entry before formal condemnation or certification (Summit Carbon Solutions v. Malloy, ND 2025; PSEG Renewable Transmission v. Arentz, 4th Cir. 2026; the ongoing Elba Express mass-petition litigation in South Carolina, 2026). Courts in this sample have consistently sided with the entity seeking access once it shows a plausible path to eminent-domain authority, treating brief survey entry as categorically different from a compensable physical taking — but a landowner's ability to challenge the underlying authority itself (as in Texas Rice Land Partners) remains a live, separate track that can still defeat the taking down the line.

**Regulatory-takings claims differ from direct condemnation in three consistent ways:** (1) they take far longer to resolve and often bounce between courts multiple times before any dollar figure is fixed (DeVillier v. Texas was remanded in 2024 purely on the threshold cause-of-action question, with the compensation merits still pending; Arkansas Game & Fish Commission v. United States took a Supreme Court trip plus a Federal Circuit remand before its original $5.7M figure was reinstated); (2) when a compensable regulatory taking is found, the measure of damages tends to be full pre-regulation value rather than a market negotiation (Lost Tree Village Corp. v. United States: $4.2M, equal to the parcel's full appraised value immediately before the permit denial that wiped out 99.4% of it); and (3) many prominent recent regulatory-takings/exactions rulings (Sheetz v. County of El Dorado) resolve only the legal framework and remand the dollar question, leaving no final compensation figure to report even years after the "win."

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **Eight of the twenty entries have no disclosed dollar figure** (City of North Canton v. Julius Brown — remanded for a new trial after a $0 first verdict; LA MTA v. Alameda Produce Market; PKO Ventures v. Norfolk RHA; Summit Carbon Solutions v. Malloy; Texas Rice Land Partners v. Denbury; the Elba Express petitions; PSEG v. Arentz; and DeVillier v. Texas). Six of these eight are the quick_take_challenge and pre_condemnation_access_dispute entries, which by their nature usually precede or bypass the valuation question entirely; a production valuation tool should treat those two claim types as largely non-dollar-generating on their own and route users toward the eventual (separate) just-compensation proceeding for dollar exposure. The other two (North Canton and DeVillier) are just_compensation_valuation and regulatory_taking cases, respectively, still awaiting a final number on remand — kept because the procedural posture itself (a $0 first verdict overturned on appeal; a threshold cause-of-action ruling with the compensation merits not yet reached) is useful signal for the valuation tool even without a terminal dollar figure.
2. **Two entries are not commercial real estate in the strict sense** (Arkansas Game & Fish Commission v. United States involves state-owned wildlife-management timberland; Sheetz v. County of El Dorado involves a single-family residential permit). Both were kept because they are the load-bearing precedents for damages methodology (temporary-flooding takings) and permit-exaction doctrine (legislative fees), respectively, that directly govern how courts will treat analogous commercial-property claims, and because true commercial-property regulatory-takings cases with a disclosed final dollar figure proved very hard to find in the time available — this is a real gap, not a stylistic choice.
3. **Several entries rely on a condemning party's or law firm's own case-results page rather than a court record** (VDOT Fairfax retailer case, Inglewood VFW Post 2122 v. LA Metro, Colorado DOT v. Mickels) — marked "medium" confidence. These pages reliably report real, named, won cases (verifiable law firms with malpractice/bar exposure for false advertising), but omit case numbers, exact dates, or the opposing party's name, so the underlying court docket could not be independently pulled to cross-check the figures.
4. **One figure (San Gabriel Valley COG v. Sahagun) could not be verified against its primary source.** The $1,372,000 verdict figure is consistent across secondary AI-search synthesis of the law firm's press release, but the release itself (businesswire.com, morningstar.com, bwslaw.com) returned 403/timeout errors on direct fetch and could not be read firsthand.
5. **No verdict/settlement database access.** As with the prior lease/eviction research pass, this research used public web search and case-summary/law-firm sites only, without Westlaw/Lexis VerdictSearch-style database access. State DOT eminent-domain settlement/award databases (where they exist as structured public records, e.g., some states' DOT right-of-way division annual reports) were not directly queried — a production version of this tool would benefit substantially from that kind of structured access, since just-compensation figures are unusually likely to be public record compared to most other litigation categories.
6. **Jurisdictional coverage skews toward Texas, California, and Virginia** — three states with unusually active, well-organized eminent-domain plaintiffs' bars that publish detailed case-results pages (Waldo & Lyle, Dawson & Sodd, Deal Sikes, and others), which is a real reporting-availability bias rather than necessarily reflecting where the most eminent-domain activity actually occurs nationally. States with major infrastructure/redevelopment programs but less visible case-results marketing (e.g., Florida, Pennsylvania, Illinois, Georgia) are underrepresented here as a result.
7. **Two site-provided leads were confirmed but not independently duplicated with new figures.** The task pointed to the site's three already-tracked eminent-domain cases (Township of Jackson v. Getzel Bee, NJ; SCLS Realty v. Town of Johnston, RI; PSEG Renewable Transmission v. Arentz Family, MD) as leads — none of the three disclose a final compensation dollar figure (they are a voided land-swap condemnation, a voided-then-recontested affordable-housing condemnation, and a pre-condemnation survey-access case, respectively), which is itself informative: it confirms the site's existing eminent-domain coverage already skews toward doctrine/validity disputes rather than dollar outcomes, which is exactly the gap this research batch was built to fill. Only the PSEG case is re-included here (for claim-type completeness); the other two were left out of this file rather than duplicated.
