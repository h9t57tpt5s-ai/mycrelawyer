# Environmental Liability (CRE) — Research Summary

21 cases/consent decrees collected (target was 15-20). 11 states/jurisdictions represented: NJ (7), CA (3), MT (2), WI (2), WA, TN, NM, MA, PA, FL, IN (1 each). Property types span legacy industrial manufacturing sites, mining/smelting operations, retail/shopping-center dry-cleaner contamination, gas stations and underground storage tanks, an apartment complex built on a former railyard, and one large urban waterway (Superfund liability tied to riverside industrial parcels). Claim-type mix: 11 `cercla_cost_recovery`, 4 `state_cleanup_consent_decree`, 3 `cercla_contribution_claim`, 3 `environmental_insurance_coverage_dispute`.

Consistent with the assignment's guidance, this pass leaned heavily on EPA's Superfund settlement/consent-decree press releases and DOJ ENRD press releases — both free, authoritative sources that disclose real dollar figures — supplemented with state attorney general enforcement press releases (New Jersey, California) and a handful of court-decided CERCLA contribution/cost-recovery opinions and insurance-coverage news coverage where dollar figures were disclosed in secondary reporting. Legal-doctrine-only appellate opinions with no disclosed dollar figure were generally excluded in favor of settlements and judgments that state an actual number, even when that number is a civil penalty in the tens of thousands rather than a nine-figure remediation cost.

## Patterns observed

**Cleanup-cost magnitude scales with contamination footprint, media affected, and number of PRPs, not simply with site size:**
- The largest figures in the sample are all *waterway* or *multi-decade legacy industrial corridor* sites where contamination has migrated into sediment, groundwater, and surface water over many decades and affects a wide area beyond the original parcel: Lower Duwamish Waterway ($668M, Seattle), Solvay PFAS/West Deptford ($393M, a 37-mile contaminated area), Lower Passaic River/Diamond Alkali ($150M, 17-mile tidal river), Raritan Bay Slag ($151.1M, contaminated bay shoreline), and Anaconda Smelter ($131.3M combined, three-plus decades of cleanup). Groundwater/sediment migration off-site is consistently the single biggest cost driver — soil-only contamination confined to one parcel (Ringwood Mines final phase, $3.4M; Riverside Industrial Park, ~$18.8M) settles for far less even at comparably contaminated industrial sites.
- At the other end, single-parcel state enforcement actions against small commercial owners (a single gas station, a single strip-mall dry cleaner) settle for civil penalties in the tens of thousands to low hundreds of thousands of dollars (NJ Sigma Realty: $117,500; NJ Chatsworth Deli: $85,000) — these penalties are separate from, and much smaller than, the underlying remediation cost itself, which is frequently not disclosed in these smaller state actions.
- Natural resource damages (NRD) claims, when broken out separately from cleanup/remediation cost, are usually a modest fraction of total site liability: Industri-Plex's stand-alone NRD settlement was $4.25M against a site with a much larger multi-hundred-million-dollar overall Superfund cleanup history; Raritan Bay Slag's NRD component ($18.7M) was about 12% of the total $151.1M settlement; Solvay's NRD component ($75M) was about 19% of its $393M total.
- Successor-liability and "final phase" settlements (OXY USA/Copper Basin, Atlantic Richfield/Anaconda Smelter, Ford/Ringwood) show that CERCLA liability persists for decades after the polluting operations ceased and can attach to corporate successors who never operated the facility themselves.

**Contribution allocation among multiple PRPs follows a recurring pattern — equitable factors, not strict proportional fault:**
- Courts do not simply divide cleanup costs by number of PRPs or by strict volumetric contribution. Trinity Industries v. Greenlease Holding Co. (3d Cir. 2018) shows a district court computing a raw square-footage/volume-based responsibility percentage (83% to the former owner) and then *reducing* it using equitable "Gore factor" adjustments to reach the final 62%/38% split — equitable discretion consistently moderates the mechanically-calculated share.
- Barclay Lofts LLC v. PPG Industries (E.D. Wis. 2024) illustrates a six-party allocation spanning contamination from 1905 to 2017: the two parties found most responsible got 50% and 20% shares, one historical operator was assigned a 20% "orphan share" that the plaintiff itself must absorb (because no other solvent, locatable party could be held liable for that portion), and two minor/incidental parties were allocated 0%. The unrecoverable "orphan share" concept — costs attributable to defunct, judgment-proof, or unidentifiable historical operators — is a recurring and important limit on what a contribution plaintiff can actually collect, distinct from the equitable-factor discount seen in Trinity Industries.
- Walnut Creek Manor v. Mayhew Center (N.D. Cal.; related 9th Cir. appeal 2023) shows how contribution litigation cascades: an initial CERCLA cost-recovery jury verdict against one owner ($1.947M) triggered that owner's own contribution suit against its predecessor, which in turn produced yet another cost-recovery suit by a subsequent purchaser more than 15 years after the original contamination was discovered — contribution litigation in a chain-of-title contamination scenario is rarely a single lawsuit.
- Future-cost estimates in contribution litigation can vary enormously between parties' experts (Barclay Lofts: defense expert projected $6.7M in future remediation costs versus plaintiff's experts' estimate of over $28M for the same site) — courts frequently resolve this by allocating percentage *shares* of future costs rather than adjudicating a fixed future-cost dollar figure, leaving the ultimate dollar exposure genuinely uncertain until the remediation is complete.

**Insurance-coverage disputes cluster around legacy dry-cleaner contamination and pollution-exclusion/site-development-exclusion defenses:**
- All three insurance-coverage cases in this sample involve historical dry-cleaner or industrial-tenant contamination discovered years or decades after the fact by a current property owner, who then seeks coverage under either the current owner's own environmental/pollution policy or (via direct-action statutes, as in Wisconsin) a *prior* owner's historical liability policies.
- Insurers are winning these disputes more often than not on exclusion grounds: in Regency Centers Corp. v. Indian Harbor Insurance Co. (M.D. Fla. 2026), the court found no coverage owed under the policy's pollution and site-development exclusions for a California shopping center's dry-cleaner contamination. Some insurers move *proactively* to disclaim coverage via declaratory judgment actions rather than waiting to be sued for a denied claim, as seen in the still-pending Travelers/St. Paul Fire and Marine v. Washington Shoppes LP dispute over an Indianapolis shopping center's legacy dry-cleaner PCE contamination.
- Where coverage litigation is not the direct vehicle, some plaintiffs sidestep coverage disputes with the *current* polluter by suing historical insurers directly under a state direct-action statute (Fox Investments LLC v. City of Kaukauna, using Wisconsin's direct-action statute to reach a municipality's 1960s-70s-era railyard-operation insurers) — a strategy aimed at collecting from insurers even where the underlying polluter (here, a city) may be judgment-proof or immune.
- None of the three insurance-coverage cases in this sample disclosed the underlying remediation-cost dollar figure being fought over; the "outcome" in each case is a coverage ruling (or a still-pending coverage dispute) rather than a settled dollar amount, which is itself a useful signal for the valuation tool — in this claim type, the disclosed "outcome" is frequently binary (coverage owed / not owed) rather than a dollar figure.

## Data limitations (read before relying on this dataset for valuation)

1. **Government press releases dominate the sample and may overstate "typical" cleanup costs.** Because EPA/DOJ Superfund settlement press releases were the primary, most efficient source of disclosed dollar figures, this dataset is weighted toward EPA National Priorities List (NPL) sites — which are, by definition, among the most seriously contaminated sites in the country. The median real-world environmental liability event affecting a commercial property (a routine Phase II finding, a state-level UST leak, a minor vapor-intrusion issue) is very unlikely to ever become an NPL Superfund site or generate a DOJ press release, and is correspondingly underrepresented here relative to its true frequency. The smaller state-enforcement penalty entries (NJ Sigma Realty, NJ Chatsworth Deli, CA gas station settlement) were specifically included to partially correct for this skew, but they still represent enforcement actions serious enough to generate an Attorney General press release, not the true universe of quietly-remediated minor contamination events.
2. **Several dollar figures are proposed/lodged consent decrees, not final entered judgments, as of the research date.** Raritan Bay Slag ($151.1M) and Columbia Falls Aluminum ($57.6M) were both still in public-comment/pre-entry status when sourced; Solvay's $393M PFAS settlement was likewise reported as a "proposed" Judicial Consent Order pending court review. These figures reflect the negotiated settlement amount, not necessarily a final court-entered judgment, and could in theory change before final entry (though downward revisions are uncommon in practice once EPA/DOJ or a state AG publicly announces a number).
3. **Multi-component settlements required combining disclosed sub-figures into a single dollarAmount.** Several entries (Omega Chemical, Anaconda Smelter, Riverside Industrial Park, Raritan Bay Slag, Solvay) bundle a remediation-cost estimate with separate past-cost reimbursement and/or NRD components; this research summed the individually-disclosed components into one total figure and documented the breakdown in each entry's `dollarAmountBasis` field, but a stricter methodology might treat "cleanup cost" and "cost reimbursement/NRD" as conceptually distinct dollar figures rather than combining them.
4. **Two insurance-coverage-dispute entries have no disclosed dollar amount at all** (Regency Centers v. Indian Harbor Insurance; Travelers/St. Paul v. Washington Shoppes) — both were kept because the coverage-ruling outcome itself (coverage denied; or insurers proactively seeking a no-coverage declaration) is a real and load-bearing pattern for this claim type, even without an anchoring dollar figure. This may understate how often coverage disputes are actually about very large sums, since litigants and reporters do not always disclose the size of the underlying claim when the story is about the coverage ruling itself.
5. **No verdict/settlement database or PACER access.** This research used free public sources only — EPA/DOJ/state AG press releases, case-summary and law-firm client-alert sites, court opinions available via Justia/CourtListener/Google Scholar, and general news search. It did not have access to Westlaw/Lexis verdict-and-settlement databases or PACER docket access, which would likely surface additional private-party CERCLA contribution judgments and a larger sample of environmental-insurance-coverage rulings with disclosed dollar figures. Several underlying court opinions (e.g., the full Exxon Mobil Corp. v. United States CERCLA allocation opinion) were found via search summaries and secondary legal-alert coverage rather than verified against the complete primary opinion text in every instance.
6. **Jurisdictional coverage skews toward New Jersey.** Seven of 21 entries are New Jersey matters, reflecting both the state's extraordinarily high concentration of legacy Superfund/industrial sites (New Jersey has more NPL sites than any other state) and its unusually active, well-publicized state Attorney General enforcement program. Major industrial states not represented in this sample include Ohio, Illinois, Michigan, and much of the Gulf Coast petrochemical corridor (Texas is represented only via the Exxon Mobil v. United States allocation case, which involves WWII/Korean War-era government-directed refinery operations rather than a typical private commercial contamination dispute).
7. **Confidence ratings reflect verification depth, not doubt about authenticity.** Every case/settlement in this dataset is real and independently findable at the cited `sourceUrl`. Entries marked "high" confidence were cross-referenced across an EPA or DOJ primary press release plus at least one secondary source; entries marked "medium" confidence rely on a single secondary source (a law-firm case summary, a litigation-tracking blog, or news coverage of an opinion) without independent access to the full primary court filing or consent decree text, and in a few cases (Barclay Lofts, Trinity Industries, Walnut Creek Manor) some dollar figures — particularly future-cost estimates and the precise mechanics of an allocation formula — could not be fully verified against the underlying opinion in the time available.
