# Wrongful Eviction / Unlawful Commercial Lockout — Research Summary

**Dataset:** `wrongful_eviction_lockout.json` — 10 verified, citable cases across 8 jurisdictions (FL, MN, GA, OR, TN, TX, LA, NC, NY, AZ).

## Why 10 cases, not 15–20

This claim area is dominated by (a) statutes and secondary commentary rather than a deep bench of *published, damages-disclosing* opinions, and (b) many of the "big number" cases circulating in law-firm blog posts and marketing copy could not be traced back to a verifiable case name/citation (a recurring "$1.8M NYC restaurant lockout" case, a Washington state "$50,000 punitive damages" story, and a Colorado self-help-clause-void case all fell into this bucket and were deliberately excluded rather than reported with an invented citation). Several other leads turned out to be **residential** tenancies once the underlying facts were checked (e.g., *Jordan v. Talbot*, *Christie v. Fick*, and a widely-cited NYC "treble damages" case, *Hood v. Kozie*) and were excluded per the commercial-only scope of this assignment. Quality/verifiability was prioritized over hitting a target count.

## Patterns observed

**1. Self-help-prohibited states produce the largest verified verdicts, but liability ≠ automatic damages.**
Florida (by statute, despite lease language purporting to authorize self-help) produced the largest verified figure in this set — an **$8.8 million** affirmed verdict in *Palm Beach Florida Hotel v. Nantucket Enterprises* — because the landlord's contractual self-help clause was unenforceable against the statute (Fla. Stat. § 83.20 requires court order, surrender, or abandonment). Minnesota's foundational *Berg v. Wiley* (which abolished commercial self-help outright) produced a comparatively modest **$34,540** (lost profits + chattels) — a reminder that "self-help is illegal here" doesn't by itself predict verdict size; the tenant's provable lost profits and chattel losses do. Louisiana's *Platinum City v. Boudreaux* shows the other side of that coin: the court found the lockout unlawful but the tenant still recovered **zero** wrongful-eviction damages because it failed to prove them at trial — establishing liability is necessary but not sufficient.

**2. Conditionally-allowed states (self-help permitted only if peaceable/authorized/on abandonment) split on outcome depending on how carefully the landlord followed the conditions.**
Georgia (*Rucker v. Wynn*) and Oregon (*Jordan v. Wilhelm*) both resulted in judgments *for the landlord* because the lease authorized reentry and the landlord's conduct was found peaceable, with prior notice given in the Oregon case. These are useful "principle only" data points showing that in permissive jurisdictions, a well-documented, non-forcible lockout under an unambiguous lease clause can defeat a tenant's claim entirely — the opposite pattern from Florida/Minnesota/Louisiana.

**3. Statutory-notice technicalities matter even in self-help-permissive states.**
Texas conditionally allows commercial self-help but layers on specific procedural requirements (Prop. Code § 93.002), including a notice posted at the property identifying how to get a new key. In *Eun Bok Lee v. Ho Chang Lee*, the landlord's failure to post that notice — not the lockout itself — is what drove tenant liability under the statute, though the actual §93.002 damages recovered ($7,375) were dwarfed by unrelated unjust-enrichment and overcharged-rent claims decided in the same suit. This illustrates a broader theme: **Chapter-93-style statutory penalties tend to be modest, flat-ish amounts** (actual damages, or one month's rent/$500, whichever is greater, plus fees) — they are not designed to be a large deterrent on their own; the "greater" values in these disputes usually come from separate common-law claims layered alongside the statutory claim.

**4. Conversion (property seized/retained during the lockout) is often where the larger dollar figures live, especially with punitive damages.**
Both *Janes v. Country Escrow Service* (Arizona, $110,000 punitive on top of $7,741 compensatory) and *Philp v. Southeast Enterprises* (Tennessee, conversion liability plus a punitive award that was vacated for lack of specific findings and remanded) show that when a landlord not only excludes the tenant but also keeps or disposes of the tenant's property inside, courts are willing to entertain conversion damages and punitive damages that can substantially exceed what a bare "wrongful lockout" statutory claim would yield. Punitive awards in this fact pattern are also vulnerable on appeal absent specific factual findings (see *Philp*), so don't assume a trial-level punitive number will survive intact.

**5. Lost-profits claims succeed unevenly and depend heavily on proof.**
Where tenants proved lost profits with reasonable specificity (restaurant in *Berg v. Wiley*), courts awarded them. Where tenants merely alleged lost profits/equipment losses without adequate proof (nightclub tenant in *Platinum City v. Boudreaux*), the claim failed even though liability for the wrongful lockout itself was established. This is consistent with general commercial-damages doctrine: lost profits require a reasonably certain evidentiary basis (historical financials, etc.), and lockout cases are no exception.

**6. Newer statutory-penalty theories (state UDTPA/consumer-protection statutes) are emerging as a lever alongside traditional wrongful-eviction claims.**
*Elsayed v. Family Fare LLC* (North Carolina) shows a federal district court allowing a forcible self-help commercial lockout to separately support an Unfair and Deceptive Trade Practices Act claim (treble damages + fee-shifting), broader than the traditional common-law wrongful-eviction tort. This is a "principle only" entry here because no final damages figure was located, but the legal theory itself is a notable expansion worth flagging for a valuation tool.

## Data limitations (be candid with users of this dataset)

- **No case in this set has a damages figure independently confirmed by direct retrieval of the full court opinion for every dollar cited** — several rely on secondary sources (law firm case summaries, legal blogs) that in turn purport to summarize the underlying opinion. Where only a secondary source was accessible (Platinum City, Elsayed, Emma Green), confidence is marked "medium" and the entry says so in `notes`.
- **Several promising leads were excluded entirely** because a citable case name/reporter citation could not be confirmed even though a specific dollar figure was widely repeated online (a ~$1.8M NYC restaurant lockout story is the clearest example). This is very likely a real case, but citation integrity took priority over completeness.
- **Jordan v. Talbot, Daluiso v. Boone, Christie v. Fick, Hood v. Kozie, and Shorter v. Shelton** are frequently cited in commercial self-help discussions but were confirmed (or reasonably suspected) to be **residential** or non-lease-eviction fact patterns on closer inspection, so they were excluded even though they are real, well-cited cases — they're just out of scope for this commercial-only dataset.
- **The Gulfstream Park Racing Association v. M1-V1 Inc.** (Florida, nightclub lockout, $200,000 figure) was investigated but the secondary sources describing it were internally inconsistent about what the $200,000 represented (unpaid rent owed by tenant vs. wrongful-eviction damages owed to tenant) and the primary opinion could not be retrieved to resolve the ambiguity, so it was left out rather than guessed at.
- **This is not a comprehensive survey.** Coverage is skewed toward states where the underlying legal doctrine (or a notable case) is discussed frequently in secondary legal commentary, which is not the same as a random or representative sample of all wrongful-lockout litigation nationally. Notably underrepresented here: California, Illinois, Massachusetts, and Washington — three of which are flatly self-help-prohibited jurisdictions where a search did not turn up a citable, damages-disclosing commercial case (as opposed to residential cases or bare statutory citations) within the search budget available for this task.
- **Dollar amounts mix nominal (not inflation-adjusted) figures across a 1978–2023 span.** A tool computing exposure ranges should treat older verdicts (e.g., 1978, 1983, 1989, 1994) as directional/precedential rather than literal comparables without adjustment.
