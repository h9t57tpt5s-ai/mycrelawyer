## 1. An Illinois landlord wins on liability, gets almost nothing for it

Link Media Midwest tore down billboard structures on a Quincy, Illinois lot it leased
from Michelmann Steel Construction -- and an Illinois appeals court just agreed that was
conversion. But because Michelmann never put on evidence of the structures' fair market
value (only what it would cost to replace them), the court limited its recovery to
nominal damages. The same ruling held Michelmann to a win on unpaid rent, because
treating Link Media as a holdover tenant had quietly kept the original 1987 lease's
terms -- including its 10%-a-year rent escalator -- alive for decades.

For landlords: winning the conversion fight over abandoned tenant fixtures is only half
the job. Walk in with fair-market-value proof -- comparable sales, depreciation-adjusted
replacement cost, or an appraisal -- not just a rebuild estimate.

## 2. Alexandria Real Estate's $185.9M fight to keep its NYC life-science expansion option alive

Alexandria Real Estate Equities disclosed in its Q3 2026 SEC filing that it's still
fighting New York City Health + Hospitals and the city's Economic Development
Corporation over a 2006 option to expand its Manhattan life-science megacampus. ARE has
sunk $185.9 million into pre-construction costs on the option; the city agencies issued
a default notice threatening escalating daily charges, and ARE went to the Commercial
Division in May 2026 seeking a Yellowstone injunction to freeze the cure-period clock
while the underlying contract claims are litigated.

A reminder for anyone holding an option or expansion right under a government ground
lease: know before a dispute arises whether a Yellowstone injunction (or your state's
equivalent) is available to freeze default deadlines -- it's the difference between
litigating on your own timeline and losing the right while the case is still pending.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-337.html

## 3. Can a data-center zoning overlay be unwound after the fact?

Four Goochland County, Virginia property owners are trying to void the county's
4,400-acre Technology Overlay District -- adopted to let data centers locate there
by right -- not by attacking the land use itself, but by arguing the county's adoption
process cut procedural corners: improperly noticed affected properties and same-night
changes to the measure before the vote. The case reached a hearing on the county's
motion to dismiss on October 20, 2026; the county has set aside a $250,000 legal
defense budget.

Worth watching for data-center developers who've leaned on by-right overlay zoning to
skip case-by-case fights: an overlay's procedural pedigree is its own front for
community opposition, long after the vote that created it.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-335.html
