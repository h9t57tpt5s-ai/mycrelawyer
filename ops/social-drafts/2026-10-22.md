## 1. A data-center moratorium beat a $3.6B developer at the Ohio Supreme Court

Bristolville 25 Developer filed its site plan for a roughly $3.6 billion data-center
campus in Lordstown, Ohio — then the village passed an emergency ban on data centers
the very next week. Bristolville sued. The village repealed the outright ban... and
replaced it with a renewable moratorium that's now frozen new applications through the
end of 2026. The Ohio Supreme Court just sided with the village, holding a moratorium
enacted mid-litigation still left it with no clear duty to review the plan.

For data-center and other large-footprint developers: filing first under today's
zoning rules doesn't lock in review rights if local law changes before the application
is actually acted on. Treat zoning risk as live all the way through permit issuance.

## 2. Neighbors sue to void a $26M hotel financing deal — 90 minutes' notice

Two West Palm Beach neighborhood groups are suing Palm Beach County and Related Ross
affiliates to void the county's approval of a $26 million sale-leaseback package for a
planned 400-room convention center hotel. Their claim: commissioners voted on a
715-page, 34-part transaction document that wasn't made public until roughly 90
minutes before the vote — a Florida Sunshine Law problem, they argue, even though the
county says it cleared the statutory 24-hour minimum.

A ruling that the approval is void ab initio would put the financing, the sale-leaseback,
and the construction timeline back in limbo. A cautionary data point for anyone
structuring public-private hotel or convention-center financing on a tight notice window.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-318.html

## 3. A landlord's own unpaid utility bill revives a $1M Brooklyn rent judgment

VK Dental Group won a $1,004,426.20 summary judgment against its sublessee for unpaid
rent — and lost it on appeal. New York's Appellate Division, Second Department held
the sublessee raised a real fact dispute by swearing that VK Dental itself never paid
its share of the premises' utilities and let the gas service get disconnected.

A useful reminder for commercial sublessors: a breach-of-contract plaintiff has to
prove its own performance too. An unpaid ancillary obligation — splitting a utility
bill — can be enough to send a six-figure rent claim to trial instead of summary judgment.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-319.html

## 4. "As is" meant as is: a $411K arbitration award survives a tenant's appeal

A Detroit retail tenant walked away from its lease after the city flagged the space for
code violations — most notably, no required sprinkler system, a $60,000+ fix. The
arbitrator ruled the tenant, who'd taken the space "as is" and promised to comply with
all laws, bore that cost, not the landlord — and awarded Padilla Investments
$411,131.50, reaching the tenant's principals personally through their guaranties.
Michigan's Court of Appeals just affirmed it.

A well-drafted "as is" plus compliance-with-law clause can shift an unanticipated code
cost onto the tenant, and courts give an arbitrator's reading of that allocation heavy
deference. Tenants (and their guarantors) taking raw or previously differently-used
space should diligence this before signing, not after the citation arrives.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-320.html
