## 1. Lake Meadows investor buyout fight (Chicago) — suggested lead post

A Chicago investor group says it bought in at $30.5M, the stake is now worth roughly
$70M — and the sponsor won't let them cash out.

Straus Group affiliates have sued Antheus Capital in Cook County Circuit Court over
the 1,869-unit Lake Meadows apartment complex in Bronzeville, alleging Antheus refused
to honor a contractual put option, abandoned the renovation plan investors were sold
on, and used investor funds as collateral for a principal's unrelated personal loan.

Put options and buyout mechanics are easy to negotiate at the term sheet stage and
expensive to honor once an asset's value takes off. This one is worth watching for
how courts treat enforcement when a sponsor has every incentive to stall.

## 2. $1.4M mechanic's lien judgment, split on appeal (NYC) — suggested lead post

A contractor won summary judgment on a $1.4M mechanic's-lien claim against a Chelsea
developer. On appeal, the liability finding held — but part of the damages award got
sent back because the winning side's proof came in on reply papers the other side
never got to rebut.

Waterworks Operating Co. v. 514 W. 24th Owner LLC is a clean lesson for anyone
litigating a construction-payment dispute in New York: front-load every invoice,
delivery record, and supporting affidavit in your opening papers. Courts will toss proof
that shows up for the first time in a reply.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-300.html

## 3. One business day late — and a tenant loses its day in court (Alabama)

A commercial tenant that vacated a shopping-center lease over a mold dispute just lost
its chance to fight a $65,827.50 rent judgment on the merits — because its opposition
to the landlord's summary-judgment motion was filed one business day too late.

The Alabama Supreme Court affirmed, underscoring something commercial landlords and
tenants both learn the hard way: in summary-judgment practice, deadlines run in
business days, not calendar days, and courts enforce the line even when a filing
misses it by hours.

## 4. Developer sues the planning commission over a denied project — and loses (Alabama)

A developer whose two apartment-complex applications were denied by Fairhope, Alabama's
Planning Commission sued the city and its volunteer board members for negligence,
alleging the denial was made in bad faith.

The Alabama Supreme Court shut that theory down: municipal immunity — and, for unpaid
board members, Alabama's Volunteer Service Act — bars an ordinary negligence claim even
where bad faith is alleged. The Court's practical point for developers: if you think a
planning body acted with an improper motive, mandamus or an administrative appeal of the
denial itself is the vehicle, not a tort claim against the people who voted.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-303.html
