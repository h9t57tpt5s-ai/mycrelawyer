## 1. Twitter/X's rent-credit win against its Boulder landlord

X Corp. (formerly Twitter) just got an $8.2 million rent judgment thrown out. A Boulder
County judge had found X in default for withholding rent it said it was owed as a
tenant-improvement credit on its flagship Railyards at S'PARK office lease -- the
landlord said it hadn't yet gotten the lien-clearing paperwork to apply the credit. The
Colorado Court of Appeals reversed: the lease's TI rent credit took effect automatically
once the buildout crossed the threshold and written notice was given, regardless of
whether the landlord had the draw documents in hand. The case goes back to the trial
court.

For landlords: if a lien waiver or draw package is supposed to be a condition of a TI
rent credit, the lease needs to say so explicitly -- not just make it a condition of the
cash disbursement.

## 2. Sierra Club settles, and a $15B data center project keeps its permits

The Sierra Club just settled its suit over environmental permits for Vantage Data
Centers' $15 billion Lighthouse campus in Port Washington, Wisconsin. The permits stand
with no further environmental review required. In exchange, Vantage will fund a
statewide study of AI data center build-out and its impacts, due by January 2028, run by
the Wisconsin DNR. It's a template other data center developers facing environmental
litigation may want to look at: keep your permits, fund the bigger-picture study instead
of refighting the project-level one.

## 3. A jury sides with the state in an eminent domain fight over a highway interchange

Two New Hampshire developers argued the state's taking of their land for the I-93 Exit
4A interchange mainly served a private development next door. After a four-day trial, a
jury unanimously disagreed, finding the $161.3 million project serves a public use, is
reasonably necessary, and produces a net public benefit.

The takeaway for owners whose land sits in the path of infrastructure that also happens
to benefit nearby private development: a public-use challenge is a hard fight once a
project has years of planning behind it. Compensation is often the more productive one.

## 4. The Ninth Circuit revives a $2.7M insurance claim -- and resets the test for "fortuitous" loss in Arizona

A Tempe, Arizona building owner's $2.7 million water-damage claim looked dead after a
federal court sided with its insurer: an engineering firm had flagged preventive repairs
(a waterproof coating, a vapor barrier, more drainage) that were never installed, and the
insurer denied the claim as not "fortuitous." The Ninth Circuit just revived it, applying
a new test the Arizona Supreme Court adopted in September: coverage turns on what the
owner actually knew when it bought the policy, not on whether the eventual failure was
statistically foreseeable.

If you've deferred a recommended repair on a property with all-risk coverage, this is
worth documenting now -- the loss not being a certainty at the time you bought or renewed
the policy is your answer to the foreseeability argument insurers will otherwise raise.

Full write-up: https://credocket.com/matters/live-333.html
