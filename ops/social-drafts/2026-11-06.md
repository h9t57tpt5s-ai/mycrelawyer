## 1. A closed-session vote, not the deal itself, is now the biggest risk to a Google data center project

A Botetourt County, Virginia landowner sued the county, its Board of Supervisors and
Google subsidiary Helio Capital LLC, seeking to void a $14 million economic-development
agreement that cleared the way for a data center at the Botetourt Center at Greenfield
business park. The petition alleges the Board discussed and effectively agreed to the
deal's terms in a closed session in June 2025, then approved it in open session without
disclosing its substance — a Virginia Freedom of Information Act violation that, if
proven, could void the approval outright.

A reminder that developers negotiating incentive or performance agreements with
localities should confirm the substantive terms are disclosed in open session before
the final vote, not just voted on as an unnamed closed-session product.

## 2. Phoenix must prove its data-center zoning rules are necessary or pay $54 million

A Maricopa County Superior Court judge ruled the City of Phoenix must prove at a full
evidentiary hearing that its 2025 data-center zoning ordinance is a necessary public
health, safety or welfare measure — or face a $54 million Proposition 207 takings claim
from Texas developer West Buckeye Road L.P. More than a dozen other landowners have
filed similar claims against the same ordinance, so the outcome of the November 17
hearing is likely to shape settlement leverage across that entire docket.

## 3. American Dream Mall beats back its own bondholder to keep an $850 million tax cut

New Jersey's Tax Court ruled the state's Freeze Act bars the Borough of East Rutherford
from raising the American Dream Mall's assessment back above $1.65 billion for 2026 and
2027 — rejecting a bid by the Borough, which had reassessed the mall to $1.9 billion, and
by the mall's own bondholder trustee, US Bank, which intervened against the owner's
interest to protect PILOT-backed debt service.

A rare look at a mega-mall's bond trustee fighting its own borrower in court to keep tax
exposure (and debt service risk) down.

## 4. A single lost city lease triggers CMBS default and receivership on a Brooklyn office tower

Wilmington Trust, as CMBS trustee, sued to foreclose Clipper Realty's 250 Livingston
Street office tower in Brooklyn after New York City agencies terminated and vacated all
342,496 square feet of leased space, triggering a $125 million loan default. A Kings
County judge granted the lender a receiver in April 2026, and Clipper's most recent
quarterly report confirms the lender still hasn't exercised its foreclosure or deed-in-
lieu rights.

A clean illustration of how fast a single large government-tenant loss can cascade into
a CMBS default — and how special servicers are increasingly moving straight to a
receiver instead of negotiating an extended workout.

## 5. A foreclosure dismissed for inaction in 2018 doesn't erase the mortgage, Ohio rules

Ohio's Tenth District reversed a trial court that had barred mortgagee Wiltshire Capital
Partners from claiming any share of the City of Columbus's eminent domain payment on a
property, after the trial court treated a 2018 dismissal of Wiltshire's own foreclosure
case — entered for failure to prosecute — as res judicata. The appeals court held a
dismissal for failure to prosecute is not a decision on the merits and can't support res
judicata, sending the case back for further proceedings.

A reminder that an abandoned foreclosure from years earlier doesn't quietly extinguish a
lender's mortgage interest for a later dispute over the same property's proceeds.
