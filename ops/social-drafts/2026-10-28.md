## 1. A second data-center moratorium town loses in court — this time to the developer

A Chatham County, North Carolina judge granted summary judgment to data-center developer
ECO TIP West, ruling its $11 million project had vested rights because the company's
zoning permit predated the county's later moratorium by more than three months — and
ordered the county to pay damages for the delay. The ruling is among the first North
Carolina trial decisions on vested rights now pending in roughly thirty other localities
with similar data-center moratoria.

For developers sitting on a permit issued before a moratorium: this is a live precedent
for your own vested-rights claim. For counties weighing one: inventory recently approved
permits first — a moratorium that reaches back can cost more than it saves.

## 2. Thor Equities says it spent $19.6M before Urbana, Ohio blocked its data center

Thor Equities sued the City of Urbana, Ohio in federal court, alleging the city passed an
emergency 12-month moratorium and then rezoned Thor's own parcel out of the district that
had allowed data centers — after Thor says it had already sunk $19.6 million into
pre-construction costs on a planned $1 billion, 460,000-square-foot facility. The suit
claims the moratorium and the city's handling of Thor's administrative appeal violated
Ohio law, Urbana's city charter, the zoning code as it stood when Thor applied, and
federal due process protections.

A third data-center moratorium fight in this one batch of filings — a sign of how often
local pushback on data-center siting is now landing in court, not just the news.

## 3. A New York landlord forfeits an unpaid-rent claim by skipping one notice

Renaissance Midtown West sued former office tenant Lividini & Company for unpaid rent
after Lividini vacated early — but New York's First Department affirmed dismissal,
holding the landlord was required to give notice under the lease's conditional-limitation
clause before reentering and reletting the space. The same ruling dismissed the landlord's
guaranty claim because the attached guaranty form was never signed, and its veil-piercing
claim against the tenant's principal for lack of factual allegations.

Three losses, one root cause: don't accept a vacating tenant's surrender without
reserving your rights in writing and serving the lease's own default notices first.

## 4. Washington court: a city's rent rules can add up to a taking, even if no single one does

Washington's Court of Appeals revived GRE Downtowner's regulatory-takings suit against
Seattle, holding the company adequately pleaded that a series of tenant-protection
ordinances adopted since 2018 could collectively amount to a compensable taking of its
affordable-housing property — alleging nearly $6 million in cumulative operating losses
through 2023 on a $39 million investment. The ruling doesn't decide whether a taking
actually occurred; the case returns to King County Superior Court on the merits.

The takeaway for multifamily owners and lenders: a city layering new tenant-protection
rules onto existing rent-regulated housing can be challenged on their cumulative effect,
not just ordinance by ordinance.
