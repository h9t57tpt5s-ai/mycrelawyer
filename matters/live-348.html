Seneca Ins. Co. Inc. v. Cobleskill Enter., LLP (Smoke-Detector Warranty Breach Voids Hotel Fire Coverage)

New York's Third Department ruled Seneca Insurance owes no coverage for a 2018 fire at a Cobleskill hotel and bowling-alley complex, finding the owner breached the policy's smoke-detector warranty.

On October 29, 2026, New York's Appellate Division, Third Department, reversed a Schoharie County order and declared that Seneca Insurance Company Inc. is not obligated to cover a December 2018 fire at a hotel and restaurant complex owned by Cobleskill Enterprise, LLP in the Town of Cobleskill. The court held that the owner breached, as a matter of law, the policy's protective safeguards endorsement requiring operational smoke detectors in units and common areas, and that the breach materially increased the risk of loss under Insurance Law § 3106. It also dismissed Seneca's fraud and negligent-misrepresentation claims against broker Links Insurance Services, LLC and the owner's third-party claims against Links.

Why it matters

The decision enforces a protective safeguards endorsement by its plain terms: detectors that would not sound an alarm were treated as not operational, and a public space such as a bowling alley was clearly a common area. Because coverage was denied on that ground, the owner's claims against its broker, which were tied to other grounds for denial, also failed.

Full write-up

Cobleskill Enterprise, LLP owned a hotel and restaurant complex in the Town of Cobleskill, Schoharie County, with a bowling alley, banquet room, kitchen and pool. After a fire there in December 2018, it sought coverage under its commercial property policy. Seneca Insurance Company Inc. denied the claim on several grounds, including misrepresentations in the application and the claim and breach of the policy's protective safeguards endorsement, or PSE, and sued for fraud and declarations that the policy was void and that it owed no coverage. Cobleskill Enterprise counterclaimed and brought a third-party action against Links Insurance Services, LLC, the retail broker that placed the policy. Seneca later added fraud and negligent-misrepresentation claims against Links, alleging Links filed the claim knowing a third party had cancelled the policy before the fire.

In an order entered March 4, 2025, Supreme Court (Justice James Ferreira) denied Links' motion to dismiss and Seneca's cross-motion for summary judgment on the PSE, finding Seneca's own submissions raised fact issues. Seneca's evidence included an October 2018 Village of Cobleskill citation for inadequate carbon monoxide detectors and testimony from a technician with the fire-equipment firm hired about a month later. He testified that there were no smoke detectors in the pool area, bowling alley, basement or dining room ceiling, only heat detectors in the banquet room and kitchen, and a few nonfunctional "dummy detectors." He also said the reporting panel for the restaurant, kitchen, banquet room and bowling alley could not communicate with the main panel, so a fire in those areas would not set off the building's alarms.

Writing for the panel, Justice Stan L. Pritzker held that Seneca established breach and materiality as a matter of law. Giving "operational" its ordinary meaning, detectors that might light up but would not sound an alarm were in effect nonoperational, and some areas had none at all; the bowling alley, a public space, was clearly a common area. The lack of operational detectors in a public space was material to the risk of fire loss, and the property suffered fire and smoke losses, including in areas without them. Cobleskill Enterprise offered no substantive opposition.

The court also dismissed Seneca's claims against Links. The fraud claim rested on concealment and required a fiduciary duty that an arm's-length broker relationship did not supply, and Seneca's reliance was not reasonable because, as a sophisticated insurer, it could have checked whether the policy was in force. The owner's third-party claims against Links were dismissed because they were limited to denials based on cancellation or application misrepresentations, not the PSE breach on which coverage was denied.
