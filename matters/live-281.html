Recycled Properties, LLC v. City Council of the City of Chesapeake: Virginia Appeals Court Upholds Denial of Warehouse and Office Site Plan as Expansion of Nonconforming Landfill Use

Zoning & Land Use Ruling Issued
Date
October 13, 2026
Court or forum
Court of Appeals of Virginia
State
Virginia
Presiding judge
Kevin M. Duffan
Property type
Industrial
Amount / scale
Site plan approval; statutory damages claim dismissed
Parties
Appellant (Property Owner): Recycled Properties, LLCAppellee: City Council of the City of ChesapeakeAppellee: City of Chesapeake

A Virginia appeals court upheld Chesapeake's refusal to approve a warehouse and office site plan at Recycled Properties' construction-debris landfill as an expansion of a nonconforming use.

On October 13, 2026, the Court of Appeals of Virginia affirmed a circuit court order sustaining the City of Chesapeake's demurrer to Recycled Properties, LLC's challenge to the city's disapproval of a site plan for a warehouse and office building at its construction-debris landfill. The city had found the buildings would expand a legally nonconforming use and asked for more information and a conditional use permit. The court held that the owner's complaint, read with the city's denial letter attached to it, did not plead facts showing the denial was arbitrary, capricious or outside the zoning ordinance.

Why it matters

The ruling shows that a Virginia locality may refuse a site plan for new buildings that would support a legally nonconforming use, and may ask for more information before approving one. It also shows that a denial letter attached to a complaint can defeat a site plan appeal at the pleading stage when the complaint does not factually dispute the letter's stated grounds.

Full write-up

Recycled Properties, LLC operates a construction and demolition debris landfill and borrow pit in Chesapeake, Virginia, operations the court described as legally nonconforming. In May 2024 it submitted a site plan to the city's Department of Development and Permits for a warehouse and an office building on the property. In August 2024 the city disapproved the plan on three grounds: the buildings would expand the nonconforming use, contrary to a provision of the Chesapeake zoning ordinance; approval would wait until the owner provided more information about how the buildings would be used; and a conditional use permit for the nonconforming operations was required first. The city had also cited notices of past zoning violations, a ground it later withdrew.

The owner appealed to the Chesapeake Circuit Court under the Virginia statutes that let an applicant challenge a site plan disapproval as not based on the applicable ordinance or as arbitrary or capricious, and attached the city's denial letter to its complaint. It argued that the ordinance did not require it to prove the buildings were not an expansion, to provide "adequate information," or to obtain a conditional use permit, and it sought damages under a state statute on unconstitutional conditions. The city demurred. Circuit Judge David J. Whitted sustained the demurrer, holding the owner had not adequately pleaded its appeal on the requirement to submit more information, and noted the owner "may file a new site plan application."

In an unpublished memorandum opinion by Judge Kevin M. Duffan, for a panel that also included Judges Beales and White, the Court of Appeals affirmed. It relied on the denial letter the owner had attached, which cited an ordinance provision barring additional buildings or structures that support a lawfully nonconforming use and the city's authority to review site plans for conformity with the zoning ordinance, and it noted Virginia Supreme Court precedent allowing localities to preclude the expansion of nonconforming uses. Because nothing in the complaint factually disputed that the buildings would alter or expand the nonconforming use, or that more information was needed, the court treated the owner's contrary assertions as legal conclusions that need not be accepted on demurrer.

The court also rejected the owner's argument that the circuit court had decided the case on a summary judgment standard rather than the demurrer standard, finding the circuit court had ruled on the adequacy of the pleading. It did not reach the owner's argument that the denial was unconstitutional, because that argument was not included in an assignment of error or argued in the opening brief.
