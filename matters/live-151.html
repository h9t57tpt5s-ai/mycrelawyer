Full write-up

Florida's Second District Court of Appeal has affirmed a jury verdict, reported at $6.8 million, against Wal-Mart Stores East, LP in a premises liability suit brought by an independent contractor's technician who was shocked and fell from a ladder while installing an automatic door at a Walmart store. The decision, issued June 24, 2026 in Docket No. 2D2025-0567, rejects Walmart's argument that it owed no duty of care to a contractor's employee injured while performing the very work the contractor was retained to do, and clarifies where Florida law draws the line between hazards inherent in a contracted job and hazards the property owner itself creates or conceals.

The case traces to July 2020, when Joseph Johnson, a service technician for D.H. Pace Door Company, went to a Hillsborough County Walmart to remove a manual door and install an automatic one at the store's order pick-up area. The installation required tying the new door into the store's electrical supply, which meant accessing an existing metal junction box housed inside a locked room that only Walmart employees could enter. Johnson had no way to know, from the outside, that the box's wiring was improperly capped and ungrounded, a defective condition the evidence at trial indicated had existed for years. When Johnson touched the exterior of the box to open it, he was shocked, lost his balance, fell from the ladder he was working on, and suffered a head injury. Because his own employer's workers' compensation coverage barred a direct suit against D.H. Pace, Johnson sued Walmart, and a Hillsborough County jury returned a verdict in his favor.

On appeal, Walmart invoked a well-established rule in Florida premises liability law: a property owner generally is not liable to an independent contractor's employees for injuries arising from the very work the contractor was hired to perform, on the theory that a contractor and its employees are expected to identify and guard against the ordinary hazards of their own trade. But that rule has a recognized exception, and it is the exception the Second District's panel found controlling here: an owner can still be liable where it created or had actual or constructive knowledge of a dangerous condition that was not inherent in the work the contractor was retained to do, and failed to warn the contractor's employees of it. The panel concluded the record supported the jury's finding that Walmart had, at minimum, constructive knowledge of the concealed electrical hazard, since the junction box sat inside a room only Walmart controlled, its defective condition had persisted for years, and the danger had nothing to do with the door-installation job Johnson had actually been hired to perform.

The ruling matters beyond its facts because premises owners routinely rely on the general independent-contractor no-duty rule to insulate themselves from liability whenever an outside contractor's employee is hurt on site. This decision is a reminder that the rule protects owners only from claims tied to hazards inherent in the specific work contracted for, not from separate, undisclosed dangers lurking in areas the owner itself controls and maintains. The case also underscores how persuasive longevity of a defect can be on the knowledge element: evidence that a hazardous condition existed for years is powerful support for an inference of constructive knowledge, even without proof the owner was subjectively aware of the specific problem.

For large retailers, property managers, and any commercial owner who regularly brings in specialty contractors for narrow-scope work, doors, HVAC, signage, and the like, the case is a caution against treating a contractor's trade expertise as a blanket shield. Liability risk concentrates precisely at the seams between a contractor's assigned scope and the owner's own building infrastructure, especially in restricted-access spaces the contractor has no independent ability to inspect, and building a practice of proactively flagging known or suspected defects in areas a contractor will need to access is a comparatively low-cost way to blunt this category of claim before it becomes a seven-figure verdict.
