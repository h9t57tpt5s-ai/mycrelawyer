Commercial Properties, Inc. v. Montgomery Civic Ballet, Inc. (Commercial Lease Default, Indemnity and Untimely Opposition)

Alabama's Supreme Court upheld the striking of a ballet school's late response in its landlord's rent and indemnity case, but reversed an order that had dismissed the whole case.

The Alabama Supreme Court on October 16, 2026, affirmed a Montgomery trial court's orders striking as untimely the response that Montgomery Civic Ballet, Inc. and two individual plaintiffs filed to their landlord's motion for partial summary judgment. That motion, left unopposed, sought $65,827.50 in unpaid rent and a ruling that the Ballet owes Commercial Properties, Inc. defense and indemnity on mold-related injury claims. On the landlord's cross-appeal, the court affirmed the trial court's Rule 54(b) certification of the partial summary judgment but reversed its statement that the whole case was dismissed, because the individual plaintiffs' claims and the landlord's damages are still pending.

Why it matters

The opinion turns on deadline arithmetic: under Alabama's Rules 56(c)(2) and 6(a), a response due two days before a Monday hearing was due the prior Thursday, so a Friday filing was late, and the tenant never asked for more time under Rule 56(f) or Rule 6(b). It also holds that an order cannot certify a partial judgment as final while declaring dismissed claims that remain pending.

Full write-up

The Supreme Court of Alabama on October 16, 2026, affirmed a Montgomery County circuit court's decision to strike as untimely the response that Montgomery Civic Ballet, Inc., doing business as Montgomery Ballet Studios, and two individual plaintiffs filed to their landlord's motion for partial summary judgment. On the landlord's cross-appeal, the court affirmed in part, reversed in part and remanded, holding that the trial court could not certify the partial summary judgment as final under Rule 54(b) while also declaring the entire case dismissed. Justice Will Sellers wrote the opinion, and four justices concurred.

According to the opinion, Commercial Properties, Inc. leased two suites in a shopping center it owns and manages to the Ballet under leases signed in May 2008 and January 2012. The leases contained tenant indemnification, default and attorney-fee provisions. In December 2020 the Ballet vacated, citing the landlord's alleged failure to eliminate mold, and in January 2021 the Ballet and two individuals sued the landlord, alleging the individuals were injured by mold exposure and the Ballet's property, including costumes, was damaged. The landlord counterclaimed for unpaid rent and for defense and indemnity on the individuals' claims, and the Ballet did not respond to its written demand for a defense.

In February 2023 the landlord moved for partial summary judgment on the claims involving the Ballet, seeking a declaration that the Ballet owes $65,827.50 in unpaid rent and owes defense and indemnity on the individuals' claims, plus judgment on the Ballet's own claims. A hearing was not set until March 2025; the landlord attributes the delay to the original judge's injuries and retirement, not to the parties. The plaintiffs filed their response on Friday, April 18, 2025, for a hearing on Monday, April 21. The trial court struck it as untimely, later refused to accept it when it was resubmitted, and on December 22, 2025, entered partial summary judgment for the landlord.

The Supreme Court agreed the response was late. Rule 56(c)(2) requires opposition to be served two days before the hearing, and Rule 6(a), as then in effect, excluded the weekend from that count, so the response was due Thursday, April 17. The court found the plaintiffs had notice of the hearing and the motion to strike, said it would not fault the trial court if their counsel did not attend, and noted they never sought a continuance under Rule 56(f) or more time under Rule 6(b). The plaintiffs did not challenge the merits of the summary judgment in their opening brief, and the court declined to consider merits arguments raised for the first time in reply. On February 3, 2026, the trial court had certified the partial summary judgment as final under Rule 54(b) and stated that "this case is dismissed." The Supreme Court called that order "legally inconsistent": the individual plaintiffs' claims against the landlord were still pending, as was the determination of damages owed to the landlord. It affirmed the certification, reversed the order to the extent it dismissed the entire case, and remanded for further proceedings.
