GSL Holdings, LLC v. Charter Township of Lyon, Michigan (Sixth Circuit Rejects Developer's Civil-Rights Claims Over Permitting Delay)

The Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for Lyon Township, Michigan, and its supervisor on a developer's civil-rights claims over the township's refusal to issue building permits before a county septic permit.

The Sixth Circuit, in a published opinion by Judge Andre B. Mathis filed November 9, 2026, affirmed summary judgment for the Charter Township of Lyon and Township Supervisor John Dolan on GSL Holdings, LLC's equal-protection, due-process and First Amendment retaliation claims. GSL had agreed to build a facility for Moba Americas, Inc. on its industrial-park parcel by October 1, 2021, but the township would not issue building permits until GSL obtained a septic permit from the Oakland County Health Division, which required GSL to first combine two lots. The court held the township had a rational basis for its position, GSL had no protected property interest in the permits, and the township's request that GSL stop emailing the entire board was a permissible content-neutral restriction.

Why it matters

The ruling shows how hard it is to turn a permitting dispute into a federal civil-rights claim: one earlier permit granted to a neighbor, which the township called a mistake, did not show irrational treatment, and Michigan's vested-rights rule requires substantial construction under a permit. It also illustrates how a requirement held by a separate county agency can control a build-to-suit timeline.

Full write-up

GSL Holdings, LLC, a Michigan real estate developer, owns a roughly six-acre parcel in an industrial complex in Lyon Township. In December 2020 it leased the site to Moba Americas, Inc., a machinery manufacturer for the egg industry, agreeing to use its best reasonable efforts to have a new facility ready for occupancy by October 1, 2021. The parcel had no public sewer access, so GSL needed a septic permit from the Oakland County Health Division. The county had denied a 2018 application for the same property and said it would not issue a permit unless GSL combined the parcel with an adjacent lot.

The township preliminarily approved GSL's site plan, which called for an on-site septic field, in January 2021, but its ordinance requires permits from other agencies before construction starts. On March 8, 2021, a GSL representative emailed the township's entire board of trustees about the pace of review. The township's attorney called that inappropriate and later said it risked a violation of Michigan's Open Meetings Act. The same day, GSL signed a hold-harmless agreement so the township would go ahead with building-plan review. GSL applied for the septic permit on March 9, and on March 22 the county again said it would not consider the application until the lots were combined; GSL then applied to combine them. GSL says a township coordinator told it in late March that foundation and shell permits were ready, but it did not pick them up. On April 5 the township said that, at Supervisor Dolan's direction, no building permit would issue until after a utility pre-construction meeting, which required the septic permit. On May 3 the township proposed a second hold-harmless agreement that would have let GSL build without the septic permit if GSL agreed to extend the public sanitary sewer at its own cost should the permit not be obtained. GSL considered the terms atypical and unduly burdensome, and the agreement was never signed. The facility was not ready by October, and GSL and Moba negotiated an end to the lease.

GSL sued the township and Dolan in the Eastern District of Michigan. Judge Matthew F. Leitman granted summary judgment to the defendants on all claims except a later First Amendment retaliation claim over the township's denial of a speculative-building designation and a Michigan Freedom of Information Act claim, which the parties settled shortly before trial.

The Sixth Circuit panel of Judges White, Thapar and Mathis affirmed. On equal protection, GSL pointed to a neighboring owner that received a building permit around 2017 before it had a septic permit. Assuming that owner was similarly situated, the court held the township's sewage-related reasons were rational and that it was conceivable the earlier permit was a mistake; the township conceded the former coordinator who issued it had breached township policy. Irritation by the township's outside counsel and engineering consultant at GSL's email did not show animus by a final decisionmaker. On procedural due process, the court held that even if permits had issued, GSL did no substantial construction under them, so under Michigan law it had no vested property interest. The substantive due-process claim failed because the township followed its ordinance, and the township rationally denied the speculative-building designation because its tax-abatement guidelines bar applicants with pending suits against it. The First Amendment claim failed because the request not to email the whole board at once was content neutral, narrowly tailored to the township's interest in complying with the Open Meetings Act, and left other channels open.
