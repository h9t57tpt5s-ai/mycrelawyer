148 State Street Owner v. City of Boston (Commercial Assessment "Add-Back Policy" Retaliation Class Action)

A legal group suing Boston for 148 State Street's owner says city data shows 182 assessment increases on 150 commercial properties after tax appeals, with about $19 million disputed.

The Pioneer New England Legal Foundation, which represents the owner of 148 State St. in a class action against the City of Boston, said on August 18, 2026 that data the city provided shows assessments rose on 150 commercial properties, in 182 instances, after owners appealed to the state Appellate Tax Board, Banker & Tradesman reported. The foundation puts the disputed payments at about $19 million. Suffolk Superior Court Judge Peter B. Krupp let the case proceed in May, writing that the alleged Add-Back Policy "plausibly chills all commercial property owners from exercising their right to seek an abatement." City officials have said they "stabilized" the values of properties with open appeals at prior-year levels.

Why it matters

The case tests whether raising assessments on owners who appeal can be challenged as retaliation for petitioning, separate from the abatement process itself. If the city-provided figures hold up, the dispute is much larger than the roughly 60 properties first estimated.

Full write-up

The owner of 148 State St., a Financial District office building, sued the City of Boston in Suffolk Superior Court on December 17, 2025, in a proposed class action alleging that the Assessing Department retaliated against commercial owners who appealed their assessments to the state Appellate Tax Board. The Pioneer New England Legal Foundation represents the owner. According to the foundation, the complaint alleges assessors overrode the city's CAMS valuation software and added back value under a "discretionary adjustment" for appealed properties.

The suit asserts four claims: failure to determine fair cash value under Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 59, Section 2A(a); retaliation for petitioning in violation of the First Amendment; denial of free access to the courts under Articles XI and XVI of the Massachusetts Constitution; and violation of Amendment Article CXII, which requires property within a class to be taxed proportionally and equally. It seeks to stop the practice, a declaration that it is unlawful, and repayment of excess taxes for fiscal years 2024 and 2025.

The city argued the case should be dismissed because the plaintiff had not exhausted its administrative appeals, Banker & Tradesman reported. In a ruling issued May 11, 2026, Judge Peter B. Krupp allowed the suit to proceed to "at a minimum, some limited discovery," noting the plaintiff's allegation that at least 60 commercial taxpayers had been subject to the policy. He also noted that the chairman of the Assessing Department's Board of Review had said the city "stabilized" the values of properties with open appeals to align with prior years.

On August 18, 2026, the foundation said data the city voluntarily provided on commercial properties valued at $6 million or more showed 150 properties and 182 instances in which taxes rose after an appeal, with disputed payments of about $19 million, Banker & Tradesman reported. The foundation's president said the practice appeared designed to limit the revenue lost to falling commercial assessments. The foundation sent its fourth request asking Department of Revenue Commissioner Geoffrey Snyder to investigate; a DOR spokesperson did not respond to Banker & Tradesman.
