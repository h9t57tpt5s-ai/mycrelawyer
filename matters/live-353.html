Kentucky's Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal of Lampton Securities' reverse condemnation suit over an Ohio River floodplain tract, holding the claim wasn't ripe because Lampton never sought a variance from the floodplain board.

Lampton Securities Partnership, LLP sued Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government and the Louisville and Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District for reverse (inverse) condemnation, alleging that 1997 floodplain ordinances stripped all economically beneficial use from a roughly two-acre Ohio River tract it bought for $800,000 in 1998. On October 30, 2026, the Kentucky Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for the government defendants, holding that Lampton's claim was not ripe because it never applied to the Metro Sewer District's Floodplain Board for a variance -- relief the ordinance made available and that the Board had granted to other landowners.

Why it matters

The decision applies federal takings ripeness rules, through Kentucky Supreme Court precedent, to a floodplain restriction: a claim is not ripe until the landowner has a final decision from the authority that can grant a variance. A staff lawyer's opinion that development was barred did not show that applying would be futile.

Full write-up

The Kentucky Court of Appeals on October 30, 2026 affirmed summary judgment against Lampton Securities Partnership, LLP in its reverse condemnation suit against Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government and the Louisville and Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District, holding the claim was not ripe. In an opinion by Judge Lisa Payne Jones, joined by Judges Combs and Easton, the court found that Lampton never sought the variance the Sewer District's Floodplain Board may grant, so there was no final decision on how the property could be used.

In the early 1990s, the owners of a roughly four-acre Ohio River tract received the Sewer District's approval for a two-phase condominium plan. Phase One was built on about two acres; on the remaining two acres, the Tract, only an electrical equipment building and utility lines were installed. In 1997, in response to catastrophic flooding, Metro Government enacted floodplain ordinances that bar most new construction in the conveyance zone, which includes the Tract. The ordinances allow construction already begun to continue and let landowners seek a variance from the Floodplain Board, and the record shows the Board has granted variances to other landowners.

Lampton bought the Tract for $800,000 in 1998, after the ordinances took effect. Around 2019 it marketed the Tract as commercially developable, and at a later meeting the Sewer District's counsel told Lampton the ordinances prevented development. Lampton neither sought a variance nor tried to start Phase Two and instead sued in Jefferson Circuit Court in May 2020, alleging it had lost all economically beneficial use. In July 2025, Judge Jessica E. Green granted summary judgment to the defendants, finding Lampton had not exhausted administrative remedies and had not filed on time.

The Court of Appeals affirmed on a different ground, ripeness. Relying on U.S. Supreme Court decisions including Palazzolo v. Rhode Island, MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County, Suitum v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency and Pakdel v. City and County of San Francisco, and on the Kentucky Supreme Court's 2002 decision in Spanish Cove Sanitation, Inc. v. Louisville-Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District, it held there was no final decision on the Tract's permitted uses. It rejected Lampton's futility argument: counsel's opinion did not bind the Board, the record showed variances granted to others, and the Sewer District's designated representative testified that no similar variance had been requested. The court noted the trial court's statement that Lampton may bring a future takings claim once it is ripe.
