The owners of a 98,000-square-foot industrial building at 2000 Folsom Street in San Francisco's Mission District sued tenant Physical Intelligence in San Francisco Superior Court, alleging the robotics startup stayed on after its lease expired and also expanded into unleased areas of the building without consent. The landlords are seeking possession and holdover damages exceeding $13,000 a day; the complaint states a new tenant is ready to move in, and sources have identified that incoming tenant as Anthropic.

Why it matters

The dispute illustrates how San Francisco's tightening market for ground-floor industrial and lab space, driven by AI and robotics companies competing for hardware-testing space the city's office stock cannot supply, is turning ordinary end-of-lease holdovers into fast-moving litigation once a landlord has a specific, well-capitalized replacement tenant waiting on the building.

Full write-up

The owners of a 98,000-square-foot industrial building at 2000 Folsom Street in San Francisco's Mission District filed suit against tenant Physical Intelligence in San Francisco Superior Court, alleging the robotics startup has refused to vacate the space since its lease expired and is now accruing holdover damages of more than $13,000 a day. The property had sat largely vacant as a former food-manufacturing site before the recent wave of AI and robotics companies began competing for San Francisco industrial space suited to hardware testing and lab work.

Physical Intelligence signed a lease in January covering roughly 29,000 square feet on the ground floor, at a reported rate of $60,000 a month. According to the lease terms attached to the complaint, the term ran from February through May, with an option for the tenant to continue on a month-to-month basis after expiration. The landlords allege Physical Intelligence exercised that rollover but never properly terminated it, and that the company compounded the problem by expanding into additional, unleased areas of the building and making improvements there without the owners' consent. Physical Intelligence had not filed a response to the complaint as of early October, and neither the startup nor the reported incoming tenant responded to press inquiries.

The complaint states that a new tenant is ready to move into the space as soon as it is vacated, and sources have identified that tenant as Anthropic, which is reportedly eyeing the building for a research-and-development lab. Neither Anthropic nor Physical Intelligence is reported to have commented on the arrangement, and no lease between Anthropic and the building's owners had been confirmed as signed at the time of the filing.

Commercial holdover disputes like this one turn on contract language that is easy to overlook until a landlord has a replacement tenant lined up. A month-to-month rollover clause lets a tenant stay past a fixed lease term without immediately becoming a trespasser, but it does not give the tenant an indefinite option to remain: California law and most commercial leases allow a landlord to terminate a month-to-month tenancy on notice, after which continued occupancy becomes an unlawful holdover subject to damages, often calculated at a daily or monthly rate well above the original rent. The complaint's separate allegation that Physical Intelligence occupied unleased portions of the building without consent adds an independent theory of liability, since expanding a tenant's footprint beyond the leased premises can expose a tenant to damages for the reasonable value of that additional space regardless of how the holdover claim is resolved.

For a landlord with a deal lined up for a replacement tenant, a daily holdover-damages rate well above the market rent is also a practical lever: it is designed to make delay expensive enough that the existing tenant has a strong incentive to vacate quickly rather than litigate the underlying lease terms, which is part of why these disputes are often filed, and resolved, within weeks rather than months. Tenants negotiating rollover rights should treat the termination mechanics, and not just the right to stay, as the operative language, since a landlord sitting on a signed or near-signed deal has every incentive to terminate on the shortest notice the lease allows; landlords in that position should document the termination notice and any unauthorized expansion into adjacent space contemporaneously, since both are what converts a slow-moving holdover into a quickly enforceable damages claim. More broadly, in a submarket where a handful of well-capitalized tenants are chasing the same limited industrial inventory, both sides of a lease should expect that a holdover fight will move at the pace of the waiting deal rather than the pace of ordinary litigation.
