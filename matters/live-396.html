Lamar Obie Company Appeal (Oregon Court of Appeals Affirms $1.24 Million Judgment Over Portland Wall-Sign Permit)

Oregon's Court of Appeals affirmed a $1,237,191.92 judgment against Lamar Obie Company for breaching the implied duty of good faith by turning in the ODOT permit for a Portland building's wall sign after its lease ended.

On November 12, 2026, the Oregon Court of Appeals affirmed a $1,237,191.92 judgment against billboard company Lamar Obie Company, LLC for breaching the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing in its lease of an exterior wall sign on a West Burnside building in Portland. The owners, an individual, his trust and his company, J.A. Atwood Corporation, had consented to an Oregon Department of Transportation permit for the sign on condition that it name them, but ODOT issued it only to the broker then holding the lease and later transferred it to Lamar. When the lease ended in 2019, Lamar refused to sell or transfer the permit and turned it in to ODOT for a relocation credit it could not use, ending the owners' ability to display commercial advertising on the wall.

Why it matters

The court held that the implied duty can limit how a tenant handles a government permit tied to the leased use even though the lease never mentions the permit, because the duty fills gaps the parties did not address. It also held that future lost revenue was not too speculative for the jury even though the owners' separate challenges against ODOT were still pending at trial.

Full write-up

The owners of a building on West Burnside in Portland had for decades leased its large west wall to advertising companies that sold space for signs. In 2009 they leased the wall to Onsite Advertising Services, LLC under a revenue-sharing arrangement, and in 2010 Onsite assigned the lease to OnDisplay Advertising. A 2014 addendum extended the lease to June 30, 2019, with automatic five-year renewal unless either side gave notice.

After the state designated that stretch of West Burnside a state highway in 2012, ODOT began requiring a permit for the sign as of June 30, 2015. The owners agreed to give OnDisplay a consent letter for ODOT on the condition that they would own the permit when the lease ended, and the letter stated the permit "shall be in the name of" OnDisplay and the owners. ODOT issued the permit only to OnDisplay. OnDisplay sold its assets, including the lease, to Lamar in a deal that closed June 16, 2015, and ODOT reissued the permit in Lamar's name alone. The owners were not told.

The owners gave notice that they would not renew, and in February 2019 Lamar said that if the lease ended it would remove the lighting and cabling and relinquish the permit, which the court said was the owners' first notice that the permit was in Lamar's name. Lamar declined to sell the permit, telling the owners through counsel that doing so would not serve its interest because it would "allow a competing advertising agency to saturate the market even further." On July 18, 2019, despite the owners' written instruction not to surrender it, Lamar used the permit to obtain a relocation credit from ODOT, and no location was available where that credit could be used. ODOT refused to issue a new permit to the owners, whose separate challenges against ODOT were later rejected.

The owners sued OnDisplay and Lamar, settled with OnDisplay, and went to trial against Lamar in Multnomah County. The jury found for the owners on the good-faith claim but not on conversion, and the judgment, including costs, was $1,237,191.92. In an opinion by Presiding Judge Darleen Ortega, joined by Judges Joyce and Hellman, the Court of Appeals held two of Lamar's nine assignments of error unreviewable and rejected the rest. It held that, as a matter of law, the parties could have held the expectations the owners claimed, grounded in the lease's limited term and its renewal and termination provisions, including that Lamar would not act to prevent the owners from leasing the wall to others once the lease ended. Whether the parties actually held those expectations and whether Lamar's conduct breached them were questions for the jury. The court also found the future-damages evidence sufficient and no error in the jury instructions.
