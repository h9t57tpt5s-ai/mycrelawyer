Full write-up

A CMBS special servicer has filed suit to foreclose on a two-floor retail condominium at the base of a 21-story tower on Chicago's State Street, the latest sign that loan maturity defaults continue to work through the Loop's distressed commercial debt even in property types that once looked more resilient than office. Rialto Capital Advisors, acting as special servicer for a securitized commercial mortgage loan trust, filed the foreclosure action on April 1, 2026 in the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, against Jenel Management Corp. and affiliated Jenel Real Estate entities, the New York-based landlord that owns the roughly 20,211-square-foot retail condominium occupying the first two floors of the tower at 36 South State Street, at the corner of State and Monroe.

The dispute traces back to a 2018 transaction in which Jenel purchased the retail condominium from Thor Equities, the New York investment firm led by Joe Sitt, for approximately $26 million, assuming roughly $16.8 million of existing mortgage debt in the process. According to Rialto's complaint, that loan reached its scheduled maturity date and went unpaid, triggering the default now at issue. Rialto's filing puts the total amount owed at approximately $17.7 million, reflecting not just the original principal balance but also default interest, late fees, and liquidation costs that accrue once a CMBS loan transfers to special servicing and enforcement begins. Cook County's circuit court has scheduled a hearing in the matter for September 28, 2026, an early test of how quickly the foreclosure can proceed and whether Jenel intends to contest the default or negotiate a resolution before judgment.

The case is procedurally a straightforward commercial mortgage foreclosure, but its context matters more than its mechanics. Retail condominiums at high-profile downtown corners were, for much of the past decade, treated as a relatively defensive CRE asset class: unlike office towers grappling with a structural shift toward remote and hybrid work, ground-floor retail in a dense urban core depends primarily on foot traffic and tenancy rather than corporate space decisions. The State Street corridor has nonetheless weathered its own version of the broader Loop's struggles, and financing underwritten against pre-pandemic rent and occupancy assumptions is increasingly colliding with maturities in a materially higher interest-rate environment, pushing otherwise performing properties into technical default simply because the maturity balloon cannot be refinanced on comparable terms.

For landlords and lenders alike, the dispute is a reminder that special servicers move deliberately once a maturity default occurs. CMBS pooling and servicing agreements generally require the servicer to act in the collective interest of certificate holders, which in practice means pursuing foreclosure, receivership, or a negotiated workout on a timeline dictated by the trust's governing documents rather than by any particular borrower's preferences. A borrower facing an approaching maturity wall has meaningfully more leverage before a default occurs and a loan transfers to special servicing than after a servicer has already filed suit, at which point the practical range of outcomes narrows toward a pressured forbearance, a receivership that displaces the borrower from day-to-day control, or a foreclosure sale that extinguishes the borrower's equity entirely.

Owners and lenders active in downtown Chicago should treat this filing as a data point rather than an isolated incident: a loan secured by a well-located, seemingly stable retail asset can still default on maturity without any precipitating tenant bankruptcy or occupancy collapse, simply because 2018-vintage debt-service coverage and loan-to-value assumptions no longer clear today's underwriting bar. Commercial borrowers with loans approaching maturity, particularly those originated at pre-2022 rate and valuation assumptions, should engage lenders on extension or refinancing terms well before the maturity date rather than waiting for a monetary default to force the conversation, and buyers evaluating distressed or maturing CRE debt in the Loop should model maturity risk independently of a property's clean operating history, since strong performance to date is no longer sufficient assurance that a loan will refinance on its own terms.
