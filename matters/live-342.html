GRE Downtowner LLC v. City of Seattle: Washington Appeals Court Revives Affordable-Housing Owner's Takings Claims Over Tenant-Protection Ordinances

Presiding judge

Amount / scale

Parties
Plaintiff-Appellant: GRE Downtowner LLCDefendant-Respondent: City of Seattle

A Washington appeals court reinstated GRE Downtowner's takings claims against Seattle over its tenant-protection ordinances, holding the owner of Addison on Fourth pleaded enough to proceed.

On October 26, 2026, Washington's Court of Appeals, Division One, reversed the dismissal of GRE Downtowner LLC's inverse condemnation suit against the City of Seattle over a series of rental housing ordinances adopted since 2018. In a published opinion by Judge David S. Mann, the court held that the company pleaded enough facts to support both a partial regulatory takings claim and a per se takings claim at Addison on Fourth, its low-income apartment complex. It also held that a six-year limitations period applies to the partial takings claim, making it timely.

Why it matters

The decision is a pleading-stage ruling: it lets the takings claims go forward but does not decide whether a taking occurred. Its holdings that Seattle's Roommate Ordinance can support a per se takings claim and that a six-year limitations period governs partial regulatory takings claims in Washington are points multifamily owners and cities will watch.

Full write-up

GRE Downtowner LLC operates Addison on Fourth, a low-income apartment complex near downtown Seattle. According to the opinion, the company bought the property in 2012 for $12.5 million and invested another $26.5 million to qualify it for the federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credit program, with bond financing from the Washington State Housing and Finance Commission. The complex has 254 studio and one-bedroom apartments for residents earning 60 percent of the area median income, along with 46 artist studios and four commercial spaces, and HUD sets its maximum rents.

The company alleges that ordinances and a resolution adopted by the Seattle City Council starting in 2018 made the property impossible to run sustainably: the Fair Chance Housing Ordinance, the 2019 Roommate Ordinance, the 2020 Winter Eviction Ban, a COVID-19 eviction moratorium in effect from March 2020 to February 2022, a 2021 ordinance extending the notice period for rent increases from 60 to 180 days, and the 2021 Economic Displacement Relocation Assistance ordinance. As the court summarized the complaint, the share of maximum rental income collected fell from about 90 to 94 percent in 2015 through 2018 to 44.1 percent in 2023, and the property lost $683,353 in 2020, $980,043 in 2021, $1.8 million in 2022 and $2.7 million in 2023. GRE Downtowner alleges the complex is now functionally valueless on an income-approach appraisal.

GRE Downtowner sued in King County Superior Court on October 22, 2024, alleging partial and per se regulatory takings under article I, section 16 of the Washington Constitution. The trial court granted the City's motion to dismiss under CR 12(b)(6) in February 2025, and a Washington Supreme Court commissioner later denied the City's motion to transfer the appeal to that court.

Division One reversed. Applying Washington's CR 12(b)(6) standard, which is more lenient than the federal one, the court held the complaint pleaded enough on each of the three Penn Central factors to support a partial takings claim. It rejected the City's arguments that the losses flowed from the tax-credit restrictions and that an owner in a highly regulated field cannot have reasonable investment-backed expectations. Rejecting the City's call for a three-year limit, it adopted the six-year period in RCW 4.16.040(3) for partial regulatory takings claims, so that claim was timely. It also held that the Roommate Ordinance, by giving occupancy rights to uninvited third parties who are not tenants, supports a per se takings claim under Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid, over the City's argument that a landlord-tenant regulation cannot effect a per se taking.
