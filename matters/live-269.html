New York's First Department affirmed dismissal of a commercial tenant's suit against ESRT Empire State Building LLC, holding the tenant waived constructive eviction in its lease and identified no lease provision that was breached.

On October 6, 2026, New York's Appellate Division, First Department unanimously affirmed the dismissal of a commercial tenant's suit against its landlord, ESRT Empire State Building LLC. The court held that the tenant had expressly waived its constructive eviction rights in the lease, and that even without the waiver it had not shown the substantial loss of use the claim requires. Its breach-of-lease claim also failed because it identified no lease provision that was breached.

Why it matters

The decision applies First Department precedent enforcing a commercial tenant's express lease waiver of constructive eviction, and it did so on a motion to dismiss. It also shows that a breach-of-lease claim must identify the specific provision the landlord is said to have violated.

Full write-up

A commercial tenant of ESRT Empire State Building LLC sued its landlord in New York County Supreme Court (Index No. 655283/24), asserting constructive eviction and breach of the lease. Justice Lyle E. Frank granted the landlord's motion to dismiss the complaint in an order and judgment entered June 6, 2025, and the tenant appealed.

In a short unanimous decision dated October 6, 2026, a four-justice panel affirmed on independent grounds. First, the tenant had expressly waived its constructive eviction rights in the lease, which barred the claim; the court cited its earlier decisions in 558 Seventh Ave. Corp. v E&B Barbers Inc. and Schwartz, Karlan & Gutstein v 271 Venture.

Second, even without the waiver, the claim was not adequately pleaded. Quoting the Court of Appeals' standard in Barash v Pennsylvania Terminal Real Estate Corp., the panel said neither the complaint nor the affidavits explained how the tenant was "substantially and materially deprive[d] . . . of the beneficial use and enjoyment of the premises." Third, the breach-of-lease claim was properly dismissed because the tenant did not identify any lease provision that was breached. The court did not reach the parties' remaining arguments.
