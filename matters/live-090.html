A Richmond court on October 21, 2026 largely refused to dismiss Four Mile Capital's $40M+ fraud suit against developer Reynolds South Tower over water leaks at the 212-unit River's Edge at Manchester apartments.

Four Mile Capital, which bought the 212-unit River's Edge at Manchester apartment building in Richmond for nearly $50M in 2021, sued developer Reynolds South Tower LLC and principals Guy Blundon and Mark Purcell in Richmond Circuit Court in May 2025, alleging fraud, breach of contract, negligence, and business conspiracy over water leaks a 2023 investigation attributed to a building envelope not built to its design, including missing waterproof sheathing Four Mile says it was told had been installed. At an August 7 hearing, the defense moved to dismiss the fraud counts, arguing Four Mile was not misled; Chief Judge Jacqueline S. McClenney said she expects to rule within a few weeks. On October 21, 2026, the court largely denied the motion to dismiss, allowing the fraud and related claims to proceed, according to Richmond BizSense.

Why it matters

Tests how far a seller's negotiated post-closing repair commitment for a known defect can survive scrutiny once an independent investigation reveals the underlying construction problem was materially different, and larger, than represented at the time of sale. Buyers who accept a repair arrangement in lieu of a price reduction for a known issue should treat the case as a guide to preserving a later fraud claim if the seller's account of the defect's cause proves false.

Full write-up

A $40 million dispute between the developer of a Richmond riverfront apartment tower and the investment firm that bought it is now before the Richmond Circuit Court, testing how far a seller's post-closing repair promises can shield it from a fraud claim once the underlying defects turn out to run deeper than disclosed. Four Mile Capital, the Denver-based investment firm that paid nearly $50 million in 2021 for River's Edge at Manchester, a 212-unit riverfront apartment building at 505 Porter Street in Richmond, Virginia, is asking the court to let a fraud claim against the building's developer proceed to trial. The developer, Reynolds South Tower LLC, an entity associated with Guy Blundon of CMB Development and Mark Purcell of Purcell Construction Corp., is fighting to have that fraud count dismissed, arguing at an August 7 hearing that Four Mile was never misled about the condition of the building it bought.

The dispute traces back to the 2021 sale itself. Four Mile has acknowledged it knew River's Edge had experienced water leaks before the sale closed, and that the sellers agreed, as part of the deal, to perform repairs both before and after closing. For roughly a year, that arrangement held, with repair work continuing into 2022. But Four Mile alleges that by 2022, Purcell had stopped making good on the repair commitment, and the leaks persisted. In 2023, after the water intrusion continued despite the earlier repairs, Four Mile hired an outside engineering firm to investigate the building envelope. That investigation, the complaint alleges, revealed that River's Edge was not built in accordance with its own architectural design, and specifically that the building lacked the waterproof sheathing Four Mile says it was told had been installed. First filed in Richmond Circuit Court in May 2025, the suit asserts fraud, breach of contract, negligence, and business conspiracy, and seeks more than $40 million in damages.

The legal fight now centers on a distinction that separates ordinary construction-defect claims from fraud claims: whether a seller's affirmative misrepresentation about how a building was actually constructed, as opposed to a general failure to disclose a known defect, survives a motion to dismiss under Virginia law. Reynolds South Tower's attorney, Stephen Faraci of Whiteford, Taylor & Preston, argued that Four Mile was not misled about the state of the building, framing the pre-sale water leaks and subsequent repair arrangement as evidence that Four Mile bought the property with eyes open to its condition. Four Mile's attorney, Dylan Graham of the Fairfax-based Wise Law Firm, countered that the misrepresentations extended beyond the leaks Four Mile already knew about, to specific, verifiable claims about the building's underlying construction that a 2023 investigation showed were false.

If Judge McClenney allows the fraud count to survive, the case will proceed toward discovery on the theory that a seller's repair commitments during and after a sale process do not necessarily cure, or waive, an underlying misrepresentation about how the asset was built in the first place, a sequence increasingly common in multifamily transactions where sellers negotiate a known-issue, ongoing-repair framework to keep a deal moving rather than disclosing the full scope of a defect before closing. A dismissal, by contrast, would reinforce the more buyer-protective baseline: that a purchaser who proceeds to close with actual knowledge of a defect assumes substantial risk that later-discovered aggravating facts will not resurrect a fraud theory once the deal is done.

For buyers of existing multifamily assets, the case is a reminder to document precisely what representations a seller makes about the underlying cause and construction of a known defect whenever a repair commitment is negotiated in lieu of a price reduction or holdback, since a promise to fix the problem does not necessarily waive a later fraud claim if the seller's account of the defect's cause turns out to be false. Commissioning an independent forensic investigation promptly once negotiated repairs stall or fail to resolve a recurring issue can be decisive both for quantifying damages and for establishing that a construction-method misrepresentation, rather than merely a disclosed defect, is what is actually being litigated, and sellers extending post-closing repair support for a known issue should recognize that continuing that support, rather than disclosing its full scope up front, will not necessarily insulate them from fraud exposure if the underlying defect proves more extensive than represented at the time of sale.
