Full write-up

A Pennsylvania developer has taken its fight over a massive data center campus back to court, suing Upper Merion Township, its Planning Commission, and its Board of Supervisors for a second time in five weeks after the township formally rejected the project. The case is an unusually sharp example of the litigation risk now attaching to data center development nationally, as AI-driven demand for power-hungry computing facilities collides with municipal zoning codes never written with hyperscale campuses in mind. The developer, MLP Ventures, led by longtime regional real estate executive Brian O'Neill, is seeking to build what it has called the Renaissance Park and Innovation Data Center Campus, five separate data center buildings totaling more than 4.6 million square feet, spread across O'Neill-owned parcels in a stretch of Upper Merion Township between West Conshohocken and Bridgeport, near King of Prussia in Montgomery County. The project would rank among the larger data center proposals to reach a Philadelphia-area suburb, and it has drawn organized local opposition, including a resident petition against the plans that gathered more than 18,000 signatures.

The current suit is the second round of litigation in this dispute. On August 12, 2026, the day before the Board of Supervisors was scheduled to vote on the applications, MLP Ventures sued the township, alleging officials had violated its right to a requested extension, until September 30, that O'Neill's team said it needed to respond to dozens of outstanding township review letters on the five applications. Montgomery County Court of Common Pleas Judge Garrett D. Page granted a temporary injunction pausing any township action or decision on the data center applications. That order proved short-lived, it was vacated the next day, just hours before the supervisors met and voted unanimously to deny all five plans, citing a lack of specificity in the applications, including on fire-safety issues, and noncompliance with the township's zoning requirements.

In the new complaint, filed Wednesday, September 16, 2026, MLP Ventures asks a judge to reverse the supervisors' denial outright, grant the five data center plans preliminary approval, and appoint an independent third-party referee to conduct an evidentiary hearing and oversee the remainder of the approval process. The developer's filings reportedly argue that township officials had previously indicated, in various communications over recent years, that data centers qualify as a permitted warehouse use under Upper Merion's existing zoning code, and that officials only reversed that position after residents mobilized against the project, a sequence the complaint frames as a pretextual, politically driven denial rather than one genuinely grounded in the zoning ordinance's text.

The dispute sits squarely within a body of Pennsylvania land-use litigation in which a rejected applicant challenges a municipality's denial as unsupported by, or inconsistent with, the governing zoning ordinance and the administrative record compiled below. Township supervisors enjoy real discretion to deny an application that genuinely fails to meet objective code requirements, but that discretion is not unlimited, a denial that departs from a municipality's own prior interpretation of its ordinance, or that rests more on public pressure than on a defensible reading of the code, is vulnerable on appeal. MLP Ventures' request for an independent referee to run a fresh evidentiary hearing is a notable tactical choice, an attempt to move the fact-finding away from a Board of Supervisors it portrays as having already prejudged the outcome, and toward a neutral record a reviewing court can rely on more comfortably than a contested municipal hearing transcript.

The case also illustrates a broader pattern now playing out in suburbs across the country as data center demand accelerates: local zoning codes drafted years before generative AI's power and cooling requirements became a live land-use question are being tested against proposals that dwarf the warehouse or light-industrial categories they were written to cover, while resident concerns about noise, water use, and grid strain increasingly shape the political environment a governing body has to navigate before it acts. Litigation like this MLP Ventures dispute is likely to become more common, not less, as more of these projects reach a formal vote.

For municipalities, the case is a reminder to document, contemporaneously and in detail, exactly how a data center proposal fails specific, objective zoning or code requirements, since a denial that reads as reactive to public pressure rather than grounded in the ordinance's text invites exactly this kind of appeal, and any prior, even informal, position that a use category like warehouse covers data centers can become a significant liability if officials later reverse course. Developers facing a contested denial should weigh the value of seeking an independent fact-finder or referee where the governing body's own hearing record may look tainted by public pressure, since a fresh, neutral evidentiary record can carry more weight on judicial review, and both sides should expect data center zoning disputes to keep escalating nationally as owners and municipalities alike are forced to take a fresh look at how their codes treat warehouse, light-industrial, and similar use categories before the next large-scale proposal forces the question in court.
