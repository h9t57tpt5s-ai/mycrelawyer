Shadowbox Studios and affiliate Henrico 183 sued DeKalb County, Georgia on August 5, 2026, alleging the county's moratorium on new data-center applications unlawfully blocks a data center project on their roughly 200-acre site at 3600 International Park Drive, after planning staff had represented the use was permitted there. The suit seeks a declaration that the moratorium is invalid as applied to the property, permission to build, 'just compensation' for the alleged taking, and attorneys' fees.

Why it matters

The case tests whether informal planning-staff representations that a use is permitted can support a takings claim once a moratorium later blocks that same, already-represented project, a theory with growing appeal to developers caught by the nationwide wave of local data-center moratoria. Municipalities that rely on staff correspondence to manage developer expectations without clearly disclaiming legal effect may find that correspondence used against them once a freeze intervenes.

Full write-up

Shadowbox Studios, the Atlanta-area production company behind films including Cocaine Bear, has sued DeKalb County, Georgia, alleging that a moratorium on new data center development unlawfully blocks a project the county had already told the company it could build. The lawsuit, filed August 5, 2026 by Shadowbox and an affiliated entity, Henrico 183, in DeKalb County Superior Court, asks a judge to declare the moratorium invalid as applied to the company's roughly 200-acre property at 3600 International Park Drive, to clear the way for construction, and to award just compensation for what the complaint characterizes as a violation of the company's property rights, along with attorneys' fees.

According to the complaint, Shadowbox and Henrico 183 have spent more than $30 million developing the International Park Drive site for data center use, acting in reliance on representations from DeKalb County planning staff that a data center was an allowed use on the property. The county has pushed back on that characterization, with planning officials stating that any letters sent to the company were never intended to function as legally binding commitments. DeKalb enacted its first temporary moratorium on new data center applications in July 2025 and has extended it multiple times since, with reporting indicating the freeze now runs through March 2027 while the county drafts permanent rules addressing the power, water, and infrastructure demands of large-scale data center campuses.

The financial stakes described in public reporting vary depending on the source and the stage of the dispute: Shadowbox has cited figures ranging from roughly $30 million to more than $37 million invested or at risk in different filings, and the company's chief executive, Peter Rumbold, has been quoted putting the company's total exposure above $100 million. The lawsuit itself alleges the moratorium has cut the property's value by as much as $200 million. Those figures have not been tested in court and reflect one side's characterization pending further proceedings.

The litigation has not stayed confined to the courthouse. On September 9, 2026, DeKalb County's Zoning Board of Appeals voted to affirm its earlier denial of Shadowbox's data center proposal, with board members citing the pending lawsuit as a reason for leaving the underlying zoning question to the courts. Counsel for Shadowbox argued before the board that the county never provided proper public notice or an opportunity to be heard when it originally passed, and later extended, the moratorium, a procedural objection that, if it gains traction, could complicate the county's defense of the moratorium independent of the takings question.

Data center moratoria have become one of the fastest-growing sources of land-use litigation in the country over the past two years, as local governments confronted with power-hungry, politically contentious projects reach for temporary freezes while they study infrastructure impacts and draft more durable zoning frameworks. Developers on the losing end of those freezes are increasingly pairing the traditional vested-rights argument, that an application submitted before a moratorium took effect should be judged under the old rules, with regulatory-takings claims seeking money damages for the diminution in value a prolonged freeze causes. The Shadowbox case shows why that pairing has obvious appeal: if a county's own planning staff can be shown to have represented, even informally, that a use was permitted, a subsequent moratorium blocking that specific, already-represented project looks far more like a targeted taking than a neutral, generally applicable pause.

For developers evaluating a large-scale commercial project in a jurisdiction considering or already enforcing a moratorium, the practical lesson is to get any planning-staff representation about permitted use in writing, confirm whether the jurisdiction treats that writing as binding, and build a paper trail documenting reliance and expenditures from the date of that representation forward, since both will matter once a moratorium intervenes; to treat a moratorium as more than a timing problem and, with counsel, weigh whether the facts support a regulatory-takings theory rather than relying on vested-rights arguments alone, particularly where the freeze appears to target a specific project rather than a land-use category generally; and to scrutinize the moratorium ordinance's own procedural history, including whether required public notice and hearing procedures were followed and whether it has been extended beyond its original term, since defects in the moratorium's adoption can provide an independent line of attack alongside the takings claim itself.
