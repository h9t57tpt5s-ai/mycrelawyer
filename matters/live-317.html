New York's First Department, 4-1, upheld the city's pandemic-era guaranty law against a Contracts Clause challenge and dismissed a landlord's claims against the individual guarantor of a gallery's lease.

New York's Appellate Division, First Department held on October 20, 2026, in a 4-1 decision, that New York City's pandemic-era guaranty law (Administrative Code § 22-1005), which barred enforcement of certain personal guaranties of commercial leases for defaults between March 7, 2020 and June 30, 2021, does not violate the Contracts Clause of the U.S. Constitution. The court reversed a Supreme Court, New York County order that had declared the law unconstitutional and reinstated landlord 513 West 26th Realty LLC's claims against the individual guarantor of tenant George Billis Galleries, Inc., and it granted the guarantor's and the City's motions to dismiss. Justice David Friedman dissented.

Why it matters

The ruling gives New York's trial courts a state appellate holding that the guaranty law is constitutional, after a federal district court ruling the other way was vacated on standing grounds in Bochner v. City of New York. The majority stressed that the law left the debt enforceable against the tenant itself, and the dissent shows the question divided the panel.

Full write-up

New York's Appellate Division, First Department ruled on October 20, 2026 that New York City's "guaranty law," Administrative Code § 22-1005, does not violate the Contracts Clause of the U.S. Constitution. The law makes certain personal guaranties of commercial leases unenforceable against individual guarantors for defaults between March 7, 2020 and June 30, 2021. Justice Llinét M. Rosado wrote for the majority in 513 West 26th Realty LLC v. George Billis Galleries, Inc.; Justice David Friedman dissented.

According to the opinion, landlord 513 West 26th Realty LLC alleges that tenant George Billis Galleries, Inc., which operates an art exhibition space, breached its lease, and that the tenant's individual guarantor breached his guaranty, after the tenant stopped paying rent during the pandemic. The City Council introduced the guaranty law on April 22, 2020 as part of a small-business relief package and passed it on May 13, 2020; the mayor signed it on May 26, 2020, and it was later extended twice. Supreme Court, New York County first dismissed the claims against the guarantor under the law. The First Department reversed in 2023 and sent the case back so the record could be developed on the law's constitutionality, in light of Melendez v. City of New York, a federal district court decision that found the law unconstitutional and was later vacated by the Second Circuit in Bochner v. City of New York for lack of standing. The City intervened. On remand, in an order entered on or about December 31, 2024, Supreme Court declared the law unconstitutional, reinstated the claims against the guarantor and denied the guarantor's and the City's motions to dismiss.

The First Department reversed. Applying the three-part Contracts Clause test from Energy Reserves Group, Inc. v. Kansas Power & Light Co., the majority relied on the presumption that statutes are constitutional and on the deference owed to the City Council's judgment, which it called particularly appropriate given the declared state emergency when the law passed. It concluded the landlord had not carried its burden, noting that relief applied only to individual guarantors of businesses hit by the governor's executive orders, only for the 16-month period, and that the debt remained enforceable against the tenant. The majority said it made "no judgment as to the wisdom of the guaranty law."

The court also held that its 2023 order barred reviving the landlord's claims for amounts due outside the guaranty law's period. It vacated the declaration of unconstitutionality and granted the guarantor's and the City's motions to dismiss.
