Massachusetts' highest court ruled that White Stadium's parcel is not protected parkland and that opponents lack standing to enforce the trust that owns it, letting Boston's $325 million rebuild continue.

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled on Aug. 19, 2026 that Boston can continue rebuilding White Stadium in Franklin Park, a $325 million public-private project with National Women's Soccer League team Boston Legacy FC, WBUR reported. In an opinion by Justice Gabrielle R. Wolohojian, the court held that the Emerald Necklace Conservancy and 20 individual plaintiffs lack standing to enforce the terms of the George Robert White Fund, the charitable trust that owns the stadium, and that 1947 and 1950 legislation removed the parcel from parkland status before Article 97 was ratified in 1972. The ruling affirms a Superior Court judgment for the defendants.

Why it matters

The decision limits who may enforce the terms of a public charitable trust when the Attorney General declines to act, and it makes a parcel's legislative history, not its current recreational use, decisive for Article 97 protection. Both points bear on development of trust-held or formerly park-designated public land in Massachusetts.

Full write-up

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court on Aug. 19, 2026 affirmed a Superior Court judgment allowing Boston's rebuild of White Stadium in Franklin Park to continue, WBUR reported. The project is a public-private partnership that splits its $325 million cost between the city and Boston Legacy FC, which is paying $190 million. The suit (SJC-13865) was brought by the Emerald Necklace Conservancy and 20 individual plaintiffs, who argued that the rebuild illegally privatizes public parkland and would require an act of the Legislature.

Writing for the court, Justice Gabrielle R. Wolohojian first held that the plaintiffs have no cognizable individual interest in the George Robert White Fund, the public charitable trust that owns the stadium, and so lack standing to challenge alleged violations of its terms. The plaintiffs urged a more relaxed standing test because the Attorney General had decided not to bring an enforcement action. The court declined, holding that membership in an organization whose mission is in harmony with a trust's purpose, or recreational use of trust land as a member of the public, is not enough.

On the main issue, the court held that the stadium parcel is not subject to Article 97 of the state constitution, which requires a two-thirds vote of the Legislature to dispose of parkland or convert it to another use. The city took the land by eminent domain for a public park in 1883, but a 1947 statute authorized conveying the parcel to the White Fund and a 1950 statute designated the stadium as a school building and yard. Together, the court held, those statutes removed the parcel from parkland status more than 20 years before Article 97 was ratified in 1972. The court also rejected the plaintiffs' challenges to planned uses of areas of Franklin Park outside the stadium parcel.

Mayor Michelle Wu praised the decision and said construction was on track for a planned summer 2027 opening. The Conservancy's president said the group was disappointed and repeated its call for a smaller, fully public project, and Boston Legacy FC called the ruling a "tremendous victory," according to WBUR. The suit was filed in Superior Court on Feb. 20, 2024; after a three-day bench trial, the trial judge entered judgment for the defendants, and the SJC transferred the appeal from the Appeals Court on its own initiative.
