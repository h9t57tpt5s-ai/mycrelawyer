State ex rel. Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. v. Village of Lordstown (Ohio Supreme Court Denies Data-Center Site-Plan Mandamus)

Ohio's Supreme Court denied Bristolville 25 Developer's bid to force Lordstown to review its site plan for a reported $3.6 billion data center, holding the village's moratorium leaves it no clear duty to act.

The Ohio Supreme Court on October 20, 2026 unanimously denied a writ of mandamus sought by Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. and landowner BHGH Properties, L.L.C. to make the Village of Lordstown review Bristolville's data-center site plan under the zoning rules in effect on October 20, 2025. The court held that while the village's moratorium on data-center permits and applications is in effect, the village has no clear legal duty to proceed, and that Bristolville had no vested right to the earlier rules because it had not completed site-plan review or applied for a permit. The village had passed an outright ban days after Bristolville's submission, repealed it before it took effect, and replaced it with a 180-day moratorium that it later extended through 2026.

Why it matters

The decision holds that Ohio's vested-rights rule from Gibson v. Oberlin protects a developer only once it has met all the legislative requirements for a permit, and a site-plan submission is not enough. While a duly enacted moratorium is in place, mandamus will not force a village to keep reviewing a project, and the court said the remedy for the developer's policy concerns lies with the legislature.

Full write-up

The Ohio Supreme Court on October 20, 2026 denied a writ of mandamus sought by data-center developer Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. and BHGH Properties, L.L.C. against the Village of Lordstown and its planning-and-zoning administrator. In a per curiam opinion joined by all seven justices, State ex rel. Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. v. Lordstown, 2026-Ohio-4073, the court held that the village's temporary moratorium on data-center permits and applications means it has no clear legal duty to continue reviewing Bristolville's site plan.

According to the opinion, Bristolville develops large-scale data centers and in February 2025 agreed to buy industrially zoned land in Lordstown from BHGH Properties. The Vindicator reported that the site plan covers 98.68 acres in Lordstown and 34.38 acres in neighboring Jackson Township, and The Vindicator and The Business Journal both reported the project at $3.6 billion. On October 20, 2025, Bristolville took the first step in the village's site-plan-review process by delivering a project outline and site plan. The next day the zoning administrator said the office could not proceed until the village council acted on a proposed data-center ban, and on November 3, 2025, the council passed Ordinance No. 57-2025 as an emergency measure, barring data centers in the village. The village solicitor then wrote that the village was not refusing to review the submission and that its engineer had begun reviewing it, but that the project would likely be prohibited by the new ordinance.

Bristolville and BHGH then sued. The court dismissed the claim against the solicitor and granted an alternative writ against the village and the administrator. Before the ban took effect, the council repealed it and unanimously adopted a moratorium of up to 180 days on issuing data-center permits and on receiving or processing applications, which suspended any duty to conduct site-plan review. The review halted on January 5, 2026, and the council also declined to approve the village engineer's work authorization. On June 15, 2026, the council extended the moratorium another 180 days, through the end of 2026.

The court first rejected the village's arguments that the case was a disguised declaratory-judgment action, that it was moot because the ban was repealed, and that the village was already doing the review. On the merits, it held that Gibson v. Oberlin gives a property owner a vested right to the law in effect when it applies for a permit only if it has met all legislative requirements for that permit. Under Lordstown's code, site-plan approval must come before a zoning-permit application, and Bristolville had not applied for a permit. The court distinguished State ex rel. Fairmount Ctr. Co. v. Arnold on the same basis and declined to create a duty on policy grounds, calling that a legislative function. It denied the writ without reaching whether an administrative appeal was an adequate remedy.
