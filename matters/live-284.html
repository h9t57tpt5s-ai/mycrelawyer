Conroe WM LLC v. HREF Senior Worthington LLC: Delaware Supreme Court Affirms Preferred-Equity Lender's Takeover of Texas Assisted-Living Project

Delaware's Supreme Court affirmed that preferred-equity investor HREF took control of the holding company behind a 117-bed Conroe, Texas assisted-living facility after missed payments on its $4.5 million investment.

On October 8, 2026, the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed a Court of Chancery judgment holding that HREF Senior Worthington LLC is now the sole manager of the holding company that controls a 117-bed assisted-living facility in Conroe, Texas. The Supreme Court issued a short order adopting the reasons in the Chancery memorandum opinion of January 12, 2026. That opinion found that HREF's contractual step-in right had been triggered when the holding company stopped making monthly payments on HREF's $4.5 million investment. The sponsor-side entities had argued that HUD approval rules on the project's mortgage-insured loan blocked the change of control. The courts rejected that argument.

Why it matters

The ruling shows a preferred-equity investor's step-in right at the holding-company level enforced in a summary proceeding under Section 18-110 of the Delaware LLC Act, even though the property-owning subsidiary carries a HUD-insured Section 232 loan with its own approval rules. The Court of Chancery held those HUD consent requirements bound the borrower, not the upstream holding company, and the Supreme Court adopted its reasoning.

Full write-up

The dispute concerns a 117-bed assisted-living facility built on land in Conroe, Texas. The property-owning company, Conroe WM LLC, financed construction with a loan from Greystone Funding Company LLC, insured by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development under its Section 232 program for residential care facilities. HUD required Greystone to collect three escrows: working capital, minor movable equipment and an initial operating deficit. HREF Senior Worthington LLC, which once operated under a MidHudson name, came into the deal in May 2019 to fund them.

HREF put $4.5 million into WM Conroe Property Holdings LLC, the holding company that manages the property owner. About $3.24 million covered the HUD escrows. The other $1.27 million funded the first 25 months of HREF's monthly return. HREF took special membership interests rather than common equity or management rights. In exchange, it bargained for the right to become the holding company's sole manager immediately if it was not paid. The redemption date was later extended to August 2024, and the monthly payment was raised to $40,000. According to the Chancery opinion, payments stopped after February 2024.

HREF declared its step-in right in November 2024 and sued in the Court of Chancery that month. One count sought a summary declaration under 6 Del. C. Section 18-110 that HREF was now the holding company's sole manager. That count was tried on a paper record in August 2025. The defendants were the property owner and WM Property Holdings LLC, the sponsor-side manager. They argued that the HUD documents and the property owner's operating agreement barred any change in management without HUD's prior written approval. They also argued that HREF's step-in addendum was illegal and that HUD had to be part of the case.

In a January 12, 2026 memorandum opinion, Vice Chancellor Morgan T. Zurn rejected each argument. The court held that the HUD approval provisions bound the property-owning borrower, not the holding company above it. It also held that the HREF addendum was not illegal and that the parties before the court were enough to resolve the control dispute. The court ruled that HREF was entitled to become the holding company's sole manager immediately. It held HREF's fee request in abeyance while the remaining claims proceed.

The defendants appealed. After briefing and oral argument, the Delaware Supreme Court affirmed by order on October 8, 2026, on the basis of and for the reasons in the Court of Chancery's opinion. The panel was Chief Justice Seitz and Justices Traynor and Griffiths. When the Court of Chancery ruled in January, five other claims remained pending there.

For investors in HUD-financed senior housing, the ruling shows step-in rights placed at a holding company outside the HUD-regulated borrower being enforced, because the court read the HUD approval provisions as binding the borrower rather than the holding company. Sponsors in similar structures should not expect approval rules written for the borrower to block an upstream investor's contractual takeover once its triggers are met.
