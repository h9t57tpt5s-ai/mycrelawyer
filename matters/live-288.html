In re Duplex at Sleepy Hollow, LLC: Ninth Circuit BAP Upholds Retroactive Stay Annulment That Validated Enterprise Bank's Nine Laguna Beach Foreclosures

The Ninth Circuit BAP upheld retroactive annulment of the automatic stay, validating Enterprise Bank's nine Laguna Beach foreclosure sales after bad-faith involuntary Chapter 11 petitions were filed minutes earlier.

On October 7, 2026, the Ninth Circuit Bankruptcy Appellate Panel affirmed a bankruptcy court order that retroactively annulled the automatic stay. The order validated nine nonjudicial foreclosure sales that Enterprise Bank and Trust held on October 15, 2025 for Laguna Beach properties owned by ten single-asset entities. Involuntary Chapter 11 petitions had been filed against the owners minutes before the sales began. A prospective bidder appealed, arguing all the sales should be re-noticed. The panel found no abuse of discretion, given the bankruptcy court's finding that the filings were made in bad faith. The memorandum decision is not for publication and has no precedential value.

Why it matters

Although nonprecedential, the decision shows the panel upholding a retroactive annulment that validated foreclosure sales held minutes after involuntary petitions were filed, where the bankruptcy court found bad faith and the lender had no notice and already held stay relief from an earlier bankruptcy. The panel also said a bidder who knew of the filings but neither told the trustee nor bid had weakened his own claim of prejudice.

Full write-up

Enterprise Bank and Trust held the first deed of trust on ten Laguna Beach, California parcels. Each parcel was owned by its own single-asset real estate entity, including Laguna Festival Center, LLC, Laguna Art District Complex, LLC, 891 Laguna Canyon Road, LLC, 694 NCH Apartments, LLC and Duplex at Sleepy Hollow, LLC. The properties had a collective book value of about $95 million, and Enterprise was owed roughly $80 million. Each parcel carried at least three deeds of trust. The owners had stopped paying property taxes and regular loan payments. Enterprise sent a default notice in November 2024 and recorded notices of default in February 2025. The defaults came against the backdrop of a long-running dispute between two investors over control of the ownership entities.

Within three weeks of the default notices, the owners and twelve affiliates filed voluntary Chapter 11 cases in Delaware, jointly administered under MOM CA Investco LLC. Enterprise obtained unopposed stay relief on July 15, 2025, and the Delaware cases were dismissed in August 2025. Enterprise then sued in California state court, which appointed a receiver over the properties on October 7, 2025. Enterprise also noticed nonjudicial foreclosure sales for 3:00 p.m. on October 15, 2025.

On the afternoon of the sales, between 2:28 and 3:02 p.m., involuntary Chapter 11 petitions were filed against all ten owners in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Central District of California. The petitioning creditors included Vierergruppe Management, Inc., which the panel described as connected to one of the investors. Neither Enterprise nor the foreclosure trustee knew. The sales ran from 3:13 to 3:52 p.m. About twenty unrelated parties attended, but only one outside bid was placed, on the 694 NCH property. That sale was later canceled and the money returned. Enterprise took the other nine properties by credit bid. The owners opposed stay relief by offering a $102 million purchase agreement with Special Assets Laguna, LLC; the panel noted it was signed for both the sellers and the buyer by the same person, an officer of Vierergruppe.

Bankruptcy Judge Scott C. Clarkson found the involuntary cases were filed in bad faith. At a December 12, 2025 hearing he granted Enterprise prospective and in rem relief and retroactively annulled the stay as to the nine properties without outside bids, which validated those sales; the orders were entered December 15, 2025. He also ordered the petitioning creditors to post a $1 million bond. They did not, and the ten cases were dismissed on March 18, 2026. The owners dropped their own appeals in June 2026, which left only the appeal of a prospective bidder. That bidder said he held back because he had heard of the filings, and argued that the petitions chilled the bidding; the panel noted he reached the sale site only after the property he most wanted had been sold.

The panel first held that the appeal was not moot and that the bidder had standing, because he had changed his conduct in reliance on the stay. On the merits, it held that the equities analysis under the Fjeldsted factors properly looked at the debtors' bad faith, the lender's lack of notice, its earlier stay relief and how quickly it moved, and not only at the bidder's position. It noted that the bidder had not alerted the trustee or placed a bid. It also found nothing inconsistent in treating the rescinded 694 NCH sale differently, because that property is the subject of a separate appeal.

The decision is a nonprecedential memorandum. It shows how the panel weighed the equities of a last-minute involuntary filing: the debtors' bad faith, the lender's lack of notice and prior stay relief, and its prompt motion all favored annulment, while a bidder who knew of the filings and stayed silent had little room to claim harm.
