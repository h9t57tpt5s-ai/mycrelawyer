It is the third distinct Missouri data center matter to reach a court in September 2026, alongside separate developer-driven disputes in Greene and Benton counties.

Full write-up

Cole County Circuit Judge Cotton Walker ruled on September 18, 2026 that the advocacy group Preserve Montgomery County can continue its lawsuit challenging a data center development in Montgomery County, Missouri, rejecting the bulk of a motion to dismiss brought by the county and the Missouri Department of Economic Development. The ruling is a procedural one, it does not resolve the case on the merits, but it keeps alive a challenge with real consequences for how Missouri counties handle public notice and transparency when courting large data center investments, at a moment when similar fights are erupting in at least two other Missouri jurisdictions over the same industry.

Preserve Montgomery County filed suit in February 2026 against Montgomery County and the Missouri Department of Economic Development in an effort to halt a proposed Amazon-linked data center project. The complaint centers on two distinct theories. First, the group alleges county officials repeatedly violated Missouri's Sunshine Law and failed to provide legally required notices to taxpayers and local taxing districts before approving incentives or agreements tied to the project. Second, the complaint targeted the state Department of Economic Development directly, alleging the agency failed to properly monitor and administer a grant agreement involving American Rescue Plan Act funds connected to the development. The county and the department moved to dismiss the suit in March 2026, arguing among other things that the advocacy group lacked standing to sue.

Judge Walker's September 18 order split the difference. He found that Preserve Montgomery County does have standing to assert its Sunshine Law and public-notice claims and can proceed with those counts against the county, rejecting the county's threshold challenge to the suit's viability. At the same time, he dismissed the claim against the Department of Economic Development concerning its oversight of the ARPA grant, removing the state agency as a defendant and narrowing the case to the transparency and notice claims against Montgomery County itself. The practical effect is that the case moves forward, but on a narrower theory than the advocacy group originally pled, and against one defendant rather than two.

The ruling arrives amid a broader wave of Missouri data center litigation that has little to do with each other procedurally but shares a common cause: a surge of AI-driven data center proposals meeting local resistance and, increasingly, courtrooms. In Greene County, a developer sued the City of Springfield on September 17 to block a moratorium from applying to a project the city had already told him in writing was permitted. In Benton County, a different developer spent early September arguing for a restraining order against a county-level moratorium adopted in August. The Montgomery County case inverts that dynamic: here it is a citizen group rather than a developer pressing the litigation, and the target is not a moratorium but the process by which the county extended incentives and approvals to a project in the first place.

For commercial real estate developers, REITs, and public agencies negotiating data center incentive packages, the ruling is a reminder that sunshine-law and public-notice compliance is not a formality that can be cleaned up after the fact once a citizen group has standing to challenge it. Missouri, like most states, imposes specific notice obligations on local governments before they commit tax abatements, grants, or other incentive packages, particularly where those commitments affect other taxing districts such as school boards and fire districts that depend on the same tax base, and a county that treats those notice requirements loosely risks exactly the kind of standing-clearing lawsuit Montgomery County now faces. Developers negotiating incentive packages have a direct stake in their local government partner's procedural compliance, since a successful sunshine-law challenge can delay or unwind the very incentives a project's underwriting may depend on, and sophisticated developers increasingly ask their local government counterparties to document notice and hearing compliance contemporaneously rather than reconstructing it after a challenge is filed. More broadly, as Missouri's three concurrent data center disputes show, both sides of these fights, developers seeking certainty and communities seeking a voice in siting decisions, are increasingly turning to procedural zoning and transparency law rather than the merits of data centers themselves as the actual battleground.
