State ex rel. Sommers Development Group, L.L.C. v. Cuyahoga County Board of Elections (Zoning Initiative Challenge)

Date
October 19, 2026
Court or forum
Supreme Court of Ohio
State
Ohio
Property type

Amount / scale
Not a monetary claim; dispute over whether a zoning referendum stays on the ballot, with no dollar figure stated in the opinion
Parties
Relator: Sommers Development Group, LLCRespondent: Cuyahoga County Board of Elections

Ohio's Supreme Court refused to remove a citizen initiative from the Mayfield Heights ballot that would undo a council rezoning for Sommers Development Group's planned townhome community.

On October 19, 2026, the Ohio Supreme Court denied writs of prohibition and mandamus sought by developer Sommers Development Group, L.L.C. to remove a citizen-initiated ordinance from the November 3, 2026 Mayfield Heights ballot. The initiative would return a Rockwell Automation-owned parcel, which the city council rezoned in May 2026 for Sommers' planned townhome community, to its prior office-park zoning. The court rejected a laches defense raised against Sommers but held Sommers failed to show the Cuyahoga County Board of Elections abused its discretion or clearly disregarded the law in placing the initiative on the ballot and denying Sommers' protest.

Why it matters

The decision shows that in an Ohio charter city, voters may use an initiative to propose reversing a council rezoning after the charter's 30-day referendum window has closed, although the rezoning stays in effect unless voters adopt the initiative. It also shows the court declining to fault a board of elections for following a city charter's initiative timeline rather than the 90-day certification deadline in state law.

Full write-up

Sommers Development Group has a contract with Rockwell Automation, Inc., which owns property in Mayfield Heights that Sommers plans to redevelop as a townhome community. At Sommers' request, the city council on May 27, 2026, adopted an ordinance rezoning the property from the "Headquarters or Executive Offices Park Districts" classification to "Planned Unit Development Districts" and a resolution approving a density variance. Sommers also sought approval of a preliminary site plan.

On July 24, a committee opposed to the project filed an initiative petition proposing an ordinance to return the property to its prior zoning. The council held a hearing on August 18 without voting and on September 1 voted 4-3 to reject the petition. The committee filed a supplemental petition on September 2, the board of elections found 691 valid signatures, enough to qualify, and on September 9 the council adopted an emergency resolution certifying the initiative for the November 3 ballot. Sommers filed a protest the next day, the board denied it after a September 21 hearing, and Sommers filed this original action four days later.

In a per curiam opinion joined by all seven justices, the court first rejected the board's and the committee's laches defense, holding Sommers could not have presumed the initiative would reach the ballot until the board confirmed sufficient signatures on September 4 and acted promptly after that. It then rejected each of Sommers' four arguments. The petition was a valid initiative, not an untimely referendum in disguise: Ohio law has long allowed voters to repeal council legislation by initiative, and under the charter an initiative, unlike a timely referendum, does not suspend the ordinance in the meantime. The initiative is a zoning measure and therefore legislative, not administrative, and any question about its effect on Sommers' existing approvals was premature. Sommers did not show the petition was not submitted to the clerk of council or that the clerk failed to determine its sufficiency.

On timing, the council certified the initiative 55 days before the election, within the charter's timeline for supplemental petitions but after the 90-day deadline in R.C. 3501.02(F)(1). Noting that no case had resolved such a conflict, the court held the board did not abuse its discretion or clearly disregard the law in placing the measure on the ballot, citing the statute's wording and precedent on municipal home rule over elections. The ruling left the initiative on the November 3, 2026 ballot.
