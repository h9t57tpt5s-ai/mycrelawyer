The Anchorage Community Development Authority, a quasi-governmental redevelopment agency that holds an 80% interest in the five-lot downtown site of the former Nordstrom department store, sued its minority co-owners, Hughes Family LLC and Anchorage Innovation District LLC, seeking a court-ordered partition sale of the entire assembled property after reaching what it calls an impasse over redevelopment. The complaint asks the court to order all five parcels sold and the proceeds divided among the three ownership interests proportionately.

Why it matters

Shows a structural risk inherent in public-private redevelopment sites assembled through incremental, partial acquisitions: co-ownership arrangements that make sense during acquisition can become the primary obstacle once entitlement and financing decisions must actually be made, and a majority co-owner facing deadlock retains partition as a real, often successful remedy that can extinguish a minority co-owner's own development plans. Public redevelopment authorities and private co-investors in multi-parcel assemblies elsewhere should treat this as a caution to negotiate binding deadlock-breaking and exit mechanisms at acquisition rather than relying on informal cooperation later.

Full write-up

The Anchorage Community Development Authority, the quasi-governmental agency tasked with revitalizing downtown Anchorage, has sued two of its own co-owners in a bid to force the sale of the sprawling, five-lot property that once housed the city's Nordstrom department store. The complaint, filed July 21, 2026, in the Superior Court for the State of Alaska, Third Judicial District at Anchorage, names Hughes Family LLC and Anchorage Innovation District LLC as defendants and asks the court to order all five parcels sold, with proceeds divided proportionately among the three ownership interests. ACDA, which holds an 80% stake in the property, says it has reached an impasse with its co-owners over how, and whether, to move the long-vacant site toward redevelopment, and that a court-ordered sale is now the only way to break the deadlock.

The property has sat largely dormant since 2019, when Nordstrom closed its three-story downtown store after 44 years in the building, part of a broader retreat by department-store anchors from struggling downtown retail corridors nationwide. ACDA moved to take control of the site's future in 2020, acquiring a majority interest in the former Nordstrom building along with the neighboring J.C. Penney parking garage, in a deal explicitly framed around eventual redevelopment rather than passive ownership. The remaining interest in the fifth of the five lots is held by Hughes Family LLC, whose ownership traces to Anchorage businesswoman Mary K. Hughes, one of four individual owners of the LLC. Hughes Family LLC has in turn entered into an agreement with Anchorage Innovation District LLC, an entity owned and managed by Mark Pfeffer, one of Anchorage's most prominent commercial developers, governing how that fifth lot's share of the property would be developed.

According to ACDA's complaint, that three-way ownership structure has proven unworkable in practice. Redeveloping a downtown superblock assembled from five separately titled lots typically requires unanimous or near-unanimous cooperation among co-owners on financing, entitlements, and construction sequencing; a single holdout, or two co-owners pulling in different directions, can stall a project indefinitely regardless of how much the majority owner wants to move forward. ACDA's chosen remedy, a partition action seeking a court-ordered sale of the entire assembled property with proceeds allocated by ownership share, is a standard tool under Alaska co-tenancy law for breaking exactly that kind of deadlock, though it is a blunt one: if granted, it would force Hughes Family LLC and Anchorage Innovation District LLC out of their existing redevelopment plans for the site regardless of the value they believe their interest could eventually realize on their own terms.

The litigation lands as Anchorage Mayor Suzanne LaFrance's administration has publicly pushed to convert the former Nordstrom building into housing and ground-floor retail, describing the site as a potential anchor for a more active downtown streetscape. A forced sale would give the city and ACDA a cleaner mechanism to bring in a single developer capable of executing that vision across the full assembled footprint, rather than negotiating around two other ownership interests with their own, possibly divergent, redevelopment timeline. For Hughes Family LLC and Anchorage Innovation District LLC, the suit puts real pressure on their existing arrangement, since a partition sale could realize less value, or hand control to a different buyer entirely, than a negotiated, phased redevelopment might have achieved.

For commercial owners and public redevelopment authorities elsewhere watching downtown revitalization efforts stall out, the case is a useful illustration of a structural risk inherent in any redevelopment site assembled through partial acquisitions over time: co-ownership arrangements that made sense as an acquisition strategy can become the primary obstacle once it's time to actually build. Public and private co-owners assembling redevelopment sites through incremental acquisitions would be well served to negotiate binding governance and exit mechanisms, such as buy-sell provisions, deadlock-breaking procedures, or agreed redevelopment timelines, at the time of acquisition rather than relying on informal cooperation once entitlement and financing decisions actually need to be made, since minority co-owners retain real exposure to a majority owner's partition remedy and municipalities structuring public-private land assemblies should expect that unresolved disputes of this kind add years of delay and legal cost to projects already competing against downtown retail vacancy.
