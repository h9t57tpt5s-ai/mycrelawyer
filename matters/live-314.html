A California appeals court affirmed a judgment confirming an arbitration win, including about $2.2 million in fees, for Westwood landlord Tucker Investment Group in a restaurant tenant's grease-interceptor suit.

On October 19, 2026, California's Second Appellate District affirmed a judgment confirming an arbitration award for landlord Tucker Investment Group, LLC (TIG) and its managing member in a lease dispute with restaurant tenant The Waffle, LLC over a 3,000-gallon grease interceptor TIG agreed to install at a Westwood property. The Waffle alleged it was fraudulently induced to sign the lease. After a bench trial went to TIG on the contract claim and a jury deadlocked on most misrepresentation claims, the parties stipulated to binding arbitration, and the arbitrator ruled for the Tucker defendants and awarded them about $2.2 million in fees and costs. The unpublished opinion rejected The Waffle's arguments that the arbitrator exceeded his powers, failed to issue a reasoned award and improperly awarded fees tied to settled co-defendants.

Why it matters

The opinion shows how little room a court has to revisit an award the parties agreed would be binding: the fee challenge was treated as a claim of legal error, which is not reviewable. It also sets out what a 'reasoned award' requires under the ADR Services rules, which the court said no California decision had defined: the arbitrator's basic reasoning on the central issues, not a response to every argument.

Full write-up

California's Second Appellate District, Division Seven, on October 19, 2026 affirmed a Los Angeles County Superior Court judgment confirming an arbitration award for Tucker Investment Group, LLC (TIG) and its managing member in a lease dispute brought by restaurant tenant The Waffle, LLC. Justice Feuer wrote the unpublished opinion, joined by Presiding Justice Martinez and Justice Segal. According to the opinion, TIG held a 10-year master lease on a commercial property in Westwood and on March 30, 2017, signed a 10-year lease with The Waffle, which wanted to open a second restaurant there. Under an addendum and construction work letter, TIG agreed to provide a four-inch grease waste line connected to a 3,000-gallon grease interceptor. The lease set no deadline for that work. Its cure provision said the landlord was not in breach unless it failed to perform within a reasonable time, at least 30 days after written notice, or, for work needing longer, failed to start within 30 days and diligently pursue it.

Installing the interceptor would have required excavating a slope behind the premises, and from late 2017 into early 2018 the parties discussed a smaller Schier grease trap instead. The Waffle sent a notice of breach on March 19, 2018, and on April 6 rejected the Schier as final; TIG's managing member replied that he was going back to engineers about the 3,000-gallon unit and was interviewing shoring engineers. The Waffle sued on May 2, 2018, for fraud and negligent misrepresentation against the Tucker defendants and TIG's project manager and his firm, Coastline Construction Management, Inc., and for rescission, breach of contract and declaratory relief against TIG.

After a six-day bench trial in November 2021, the trial court found TIG did not breach the lease because installation was not a 30-day cure and TIG had started within 30 days. In a 2022 jury trial on the misrepresentation claims, the jury found for the Coastline defendants on negligent misrepresentation and deadlocked on the rest, and the court declared a mistrial. The parties then stipulated to binding arbitration of the remaining claims, waived any appeal except on the grounds in Code of Civil Procedure section 1286.2, and agreed the arbitrator could use the evidence from both trial phases. Coastline settled with The Waffle for $5,000 in January 2024. In a 43-page final award in March 2024, the arbitrator found the fraud and negligent misrepresentation claims not proven and awarded the Tucker defendants about $2.2 million in fees and costs. The trial court confirmed the award, and judgment was entered on July 31, 2024.

The Court of Appeal held the arbitrator did not exceed his powers by referring to the phase I findings: the stipulation allowed use of that evidence, and the award rested on 63 findings of his own. It held the award was reasoned because it set out the arbitrator's reasoning on the central issues, even though it did not address The Waffle's argument that partial performance does not defeat a fraud claim. And it held The Waffle's objection to fees for work connected to the settled Coastline defendants was a claim of legal error, which courts cannot review after a binding arbitration.
