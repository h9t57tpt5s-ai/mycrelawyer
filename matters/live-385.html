First Citizens Community Bank v. SA Living Fund LLC (Pennsylvania Court Upholds Confessed Judgment on $3.85 Million Loan)

Lending & Foreclosure Ruling Issued
Date
November 9, 2026
Added to tracker
November 10, 2026
Court or forum
Superior Court of Pennsylvania
State
Pennsylvania
Presiding judge
Maria C. McLaughlin
Amount / scale
$3.85 million loan; judgment amount to be reassessed after the bank conceded calculation errors
Parties
Appellee/Lender: First Citizens Community BankAppellant/Borrower: SA Living Fund LLC

Pennsylvania's Superior Court affirmed the denial of a borrower's and its guarantors' petition to strike or open a bank's confessed judgment on a $3.85 million loan secured by 827 Spring Garden Street in Philadelphia.

In a non-precedential memorandum by Judge Maria C. McLaughlin filed November 9, 2026, the Superior Court of Pennsylvania affirmed a Philadelphia County order denying SA Living Fund LLC's and its two individual guarantors' petition to strike or open a confessed judgment entered for First Citizens Community Bank on a $3.85 million loan secured by 827 Spring Garden Street. The court held that the trial court erred by looking beyond the record as it stood when judgment was entered, but that the error was harmless because the complaint adequately averred a default. It also held that an error in the bank's damages figures, which the bank conceded, was not a meritorious defense, and that the borrower's good-faith-and-fair-dealing argument was waived and would fail anyway.

Why it matters

The decision applies the narrow review that governs a petition to strike a confessed judgment and treats a miscalculated amount as something to fix by reassessing damages rather than by opening the judgment. It also restates that a lender's unwillingness to renegotiate or grant more accommodations is not, by itself, bad faith.

Full write-up

According to the trial court's summary quoted in the opinion, SA Living Fund LLC borrowed $3,850,000 in July 2022 for 18 months under a loan secured by mortgaged premises at 827 Spring Garden Street in Philadelphia. Two individuals guaranteed it. The note, the loan agreement and the guaranty each contained confession-of-judgment provisions. First Citizens Community Bank is the successor by merger to Huntingdon Valley Bank, the original lender.

In March 2024 the borrower extended the maturity by one month for a $9,625 extension fee and a $750 legal fee. In November 2024 a second modification called for a payment of $103,433.33 to bring the loan current, plus a prepayment of interest for the coming year, and extended maturity to October 2029 unless the bank called the loan sooner. Both modifications contained confession-of-judgment provisions. A January 2025 letter from the bank said the borrower still owed $18,463.33 on the modification payments and $30,010.55 for a principal and interest payment due January 1, 2025, and gave it 15 days to cure. The bank filed a complaint in confession of judgment in March 2025, and the prothonotary entered judgment.

The borrower and guarantors petitioned to strike or open the judgment, arguing that the complaint alleged default only in conclusory terms and that the bank's figures were wrong. At the July 9, 2025 hearing the bank agreed its calculations were incorrect, and the trial court denied the petition while directing the bank to file a motion to reassess damages. That motion has been deferred until the appeal is resolved.

Writing for a panel with Judges Bowes and Olson, Judge McLaughlin held that the trial court should not have relied on the later-submitted letter and testimony in deciding the motion to strike, because that review is limited to the record when judgment was entered. The error was harmless: the complaint itself averred the default that Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 2952(a)(6) requires, and the loan documents provided for acceleration on default. On the petition to open, the court noted that the borrower did not claim it was not in default; an error in the amount is not a meritorious defense, and the corrected figure will be set on the motion to reassess damages. The borrower's argument that the bank breached the duty of good faith by pressing it to refinance or sell and by demanding a year of prepaid interest was waived because the petition did not raise it, and the court said it would fail anyway, because an unwillingness to renegotiate is not bad faith, particularly where the bank had agreed to two modifications.
