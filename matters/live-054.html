The Second Circuit upheld Nassau County's $4.8 million fine on Roosevelt Field Mall's Simon-affiliated owner for skipping two years of required income and expense filings, rejecting an excessive-fines claim.

The Second Circuit affirmed a $4.8 million fine Nassau County imposed on The Retail Property Trust, the Simon Property Group-affiliated owner of Roosevelt Field Mall, for failing to file two consecutive years of mandatory financial disclosures under the County's Annual Statement of Income and Expense (ASIE) Law. The panel held the value-linked penalty was not grossly disproportional under the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause given the property's value and the owner's willful, repeated noncompliance, affirming summary judgment for the County out of the Eastern District of New York.

Why it matters

Confirms that municipalities can tie escalating disclosure penalties directly to a commercial property's assessed value and have those penalties survive constitutional scrutiny, even at multimillion-dollar levels, so long as noncompliance is willful and repeated. A separate opinion questioning whether corporations can invoke the Excessive Fines Clause at all signals an unresolved issue that could shape how commercial owners contest other value-linked regulatory fines going forward.

Full write-up

A commercial property owner's bid to strike down one of the steepest municipal reporting penalties in the country has failed, but not without a notable wrinkle. On August 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed a $4.8 million fine levied against The Retail Property Trust, the entity that holds title to Roosevelt Field, one of the largest shopping malls in the United States, for failing to file mandatory financial disclosures with Nassau County's Department of Assessment. The decision is a clean win for local tax authorities, but a separate opinion from one judge on the panel raises a constitutional question that could echo well beyond Long Island.

At issue is Nassau County's Annual Statement of Income and Expense Law, known as the ASIE Law, which requires owners of income-producing commercial property to report detailed financial data, including rent rolls and operating expenses, to the county assessor each year. The purpose is straightforward: assessors cannot accurately value large commercial properties, and therefore cannot tax them fairly, without visibility into the income those properties actually generate. Owners who decline to comply do not face a flat penalty; the ASIE Law calculates fines as a percentage of the property's market value, an approach designed to make noncompliance costly in direct proportion to what is at stake.

The Retail Property Trust, an entity within the ownership structure controlled by Simon Property Group, did not file the required statements for two consecutive years, and Nassau County assessed a cumulative fine of $4.8 million. Rather than pay and report, the Trust sued, arguing the ASIE Law's penalty scheme violates the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause, the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, and several New York state-law provisions. The case had been working its way through the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York for years before the district court granted summary judgment in the county's favor, teeing up the appeal.

The Second Circuit's core holding tracks a familiar proportionality analysis. Excessive Fines Clause claims turn on whether a penalty is grossly disproportional to the gravity of the underlying offense, a standard that looks at both the harm caused and the defendant's culpability. The panel reasoned that a $4.8 million fine, while large in absolute terms, amounts to a small fraction of one percent of the assessed value of a multi-hundred-million-dollar regional mall. Weighed against two straight years of willful noncompliance with a valid, generally applicable reporting law, rather than a one-time lapse or a good-faith interpretive dispute, the court found nothing grossly disproportional about the county's response, and the due process and state-law claims fared no better.

The more consequential thread runs through a separate opinion by one member of the panel, who agreed the fine should stand but declined to join the majority's apparent assumption that the Excessive Fines Clause protects corporate entities in the first place. Whether a constitutional guarantee rooted in protecting individuals from punitive government overreach extends with equal force to corporate landowners has never been squarely resolved by the Supreme Court in this context, and the concurrence's skepticism signals the issue remains live even where, as here, it did not change the outcome.

For commercial owners, the immediate lesson is procedural rather than constitutional: disclosure mandates like the ASIE Law are not paperwork to treat as optional, because the penalty structure is built to scale with exactly the kind of high-value assets that draw the least sympathy from reviewing courts, and owners in Nassau County and the growing number of jurisdictions with comparable income-and-expense reporting regimes should treat annual filing deadlines as fixed compliance obligations tied directly to dollar exposure rather than discretionary submissions weighed against the cost of preparing the underlying data. At the same time, the panel's internal disagreement over corporate standing under the Excessive Fines Clause is worth watching closely, since if that reasoning gains traction it could reshape how commercial owners contest not just ASIE-style penalties but a broader universe of value-linked regulatory fines, including code-violation penalties and rent-registration fines, that municipalities increasingly use as revenue and compliance tools against large corporate property owners; portfolio owners with holdings across multiple jurisdictions would be well served to audit their local reporting-compliance calendars now, given how readily other tax authorities appear to be adopting Nassau County's model.
