The Second Circuit upheld Nassau County's $4.8 million fine on Roosevelt Field's Simon-affiliated owner for skipping two years of income and expense filings, holding businesses can invoke the Excessive Fines Clause but this fine was not excessive.

The Second Circuit on August 6, 2026 affirmed summary judgment for Nassau County in a challenge by The Retail Property Trust, which owns the Roosevelt Field mall in Garden City, to a $4.8 million fine for not filing Annual Statements of Income and Expense for 2013 and 2014. Writing for the panel, Judge Richard J. Sullivan held that the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause protects business entities but that the fine, a fraction of one percent of the property's value, was not grossly disproportional. Judge Richard C. Wesley concurred, writing that the court did not need to decide whether the clause covers businesses.

Why it matters

The ruling makes it Second Circuit precedent that trusts and corporations can invoke the Excessive Fines Clause, while upholding a penalty scaled to a commercial property's market value. Nassau County owners who fail to file income and expense statements face fines of 0.25% to 0.75% of market value, and the court held that an Article 78 proceeding is adequate process to contest one.

Full write-up

On August 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed a judgment for Nassau County in The Retail Property Trust's challenge to a $4.8 million fine for failing to file income and expense statements for Roosevelt Field in Garden City, which the court described as the second-largest shopping mall in New York State, with over 2 million square feet of leasable space. The Trust, a Massachusetts business trust, is listed as a subsidiary of Simon Property Group in Simon's annual reports filed with the SEC. Judge Richard J. Sullivan wrote for the panel, which also included Judges Richard C. Wesley and Steven J. Menashi.

According to the opinion, Nassau County's Annual Statement of Income and Expense (ASIE) Law requires commercial property owners to report financial data so assessors can value properties accurately; the county, which alone pays refunds when properties are overvalued, faced tax certiorari refunds of roughly $100 million a year. In late 2013, the county replaced a $500 penalty with fines of 0.25% to 0.75% of a property's fair market value, depending on how long an owner takes to comply. The Trust did not file statements for 2013 and 2014, and in 2016 the county imposed $4,753,209 in penalties. The Trust says it never received the preliminary notice letters the law requires. Rather than use the law's remedies or a state Article 78 petition, it sued in the Eastern District of New York in 2017. Judge Joanna Seybert, adopting a magistrate judge's recommendation, granted the county summary judgment, denied the Trust's sanctions motion and dismissed its state-law claims without prejudice.

The panel first held that the Excessive Fines Clause shields business entities, including trusts and corporations. Applying the Bajakajian factors, it then found the fine not grossly disproportional: the Trust knowingly and willfully failed to comply over years, it was the kind of income-producing owner the law targets, the penalty scaled with the property's value and was lower than New York City's 5% penalty, and the noncompliance undermined accurate assessment. The court wrote that the owner of a multi-hundred-million-dollar property fined 'a fraction of one percent of the property's value' for willful, repeated refusal to comply cannot claim gross disproportionality. It also held that Article 78 review gave the Trust adequate procedural due process, that the law has a rational basis, and that the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying sanctions.

Judge Wesley concurred, arguing that the court did not need to decide whether the Excessive Fines Clause applies to business entities, or to a Massachusetts business trust, because the fine was not excessive in any event. The majority responded that the question was squarely presented and that resolving it gives district courts needed guidance.
