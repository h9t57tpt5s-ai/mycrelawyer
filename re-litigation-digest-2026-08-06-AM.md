# New York's Top Court Curbs "Sole Discretion" Clauses in Mezzanine Loan Agreements

### In a closely watched ruling arising from the Steinway Tower financing collapse, the Court of Appeals holds that a lender's absolute contractual discretion to assign a loan does not immunize it from good-faith-and-fair-dealing claims — reshaping the risk calculus for mezzanine lenders and capital-stack workouts statewide.

New York's highest court has revived a $65 million equity investor's claim that a mezzanine lender used its "sole discretion" assignment rights as cover for a scheme to strip the investor out of a Manhattan supertall project, in a decision with immediate consequences for anyone structuring or enforcing complex commercial real estate debt in New York. In *111 West 57th Investment LLC v. 111 W57 Mezz Investor LLC*, 2026 NY Slip Op 03376 (May 28, 2026), the Court of Appeals held that a party's unqualified, "sole discretion" contractual right does not, by itself, defeat a claim that the party breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The court reinstated the plaintiff's implied-covenant claim against the Apollo-affiliated lender entities and remitted the case to the New York County Supreme Court for further proceedings, while affirming dismissal of related tortious interference claims for insufficient pleading.

The case grew out of the long-running litigation over 111 West 57th Street, the ultra-slender "Steinway Tower" on Billionaires' Row. AmBase Corporation's investment vehicle put more than $65 million into the joint venture that developed the tower, financed in part by a $325 million mezzanine loan from Apollo-affiliated lenders. When cost overruns pushed the loan out of balance in 2017, Apollo and the borrower restructured it into a $300 million senior tranche and a $25 million junior mezzanine loan secured by the joint venture's equity. According to the complaint, Apollo then shopped the junior loan to Spruce Capital Partners at its $25 million par value while privately circulating financial models showing the underlying equity was worth roughly $600 million — a sale price and equity value gap the plaintiff says only makes sense if the real transaction was a disguised equity grab. Two days after acquiring the loan, Spruce declared default and moved to strictly foreclose under UCC Article 9, wiping out the joint venture's equity, including the plaintiff's stake, while the project's sponsor allegedly received a side arrangement to stay on as developer with "carry-over" equity in exchange for not objecting.

The Appellate Division, First Department, had dismissed the implied-covenant claim, reasoning that because the loan documents gave Apollo absolute, "sole discretion" authority to assign the loan, the implied covenant could not override that express contractual right — a position with support in some prior First Department decisions. The Court of Appeals rejected that reasoning, siding instead with what it described as the prevailing view among the other Appellate Division departments and with Delaware precedent: a contractual grant of sole discretion authorizes a party to decide *whom* to deal with or *whether* to act, but does not license the exercise of that discretion in bad faith to destroy the other party's ability to realize the benefit of the bargain. Writing for the majority, Chief Judge Wilson emphasized that the implied covenant exists precisely to police conduct the parties did not — and could not practically — spell out in advance, and that reading an unqualified "sole discretion" clause as blanket immunity would let sophisticated parties contract around good faith altogether. Judge Garcia, dissenting in part, warned that the decision injects uncertainty into "billions of dollars of outstanding loan agreements" that rely on similar discretion clauses and could weaken New York's standing as the preferred governing law for complex commercial financings.

Importantly, the ruling arrives at the motion-to-dismiss stage: the Court held only that the plaintiff's allegations are legally sufficient to proceed, not that Apollo in fact breached its obligations. The case now returns to the trial court, where the plaintiff will have to prove the alleged "backroom deal" through discovery. Still, the pleading standard is the battleground that matters most for future cases, since it determines whether lenders and their counterparties can get bad-faith claims dismissed before ever facing discovery into internal communications, financial models, and forbearance negotiations.

For CRE lenders, sponsors, and REITs active in New York-governed mezzanine and preferred-equity structures, the decision changes the practical value of broad discretion language. Assignment, approval, and remedy-selection clauses drafted as ironclad protections should now be understood as subject to an unwritten good-faith overlay, particularly where a lender's counterparty in an assignment or UCC strict foreclosure stands to reap value far exceeding what it paid.

**Practical takeaways:**

Institutional lenders and mezzanine debt purchasers should audit internal communications and valuation models created around loan assignments, defaults, and strict foreclosures, since documents showing a gap between price paid and underlying collateral value are now squarely within the zone of discovery. Sponsors and joint-venture managers negotiating side arrangements with an incoming lienholder — such as retained management rights or carry-over equity — should recognize that such arrangements can themselves become evidence supporting an equity investor's bad-faith claim. Equity investors and mezzanine borrowers with New York-governed loan documents have a materially stronger pleading position going forward if they can point to internal projections or communications suggesting collusion around a loan sale or default declaration. Parties drafting new financing documents who want the pre-decision level of certainty should consider express, negotiated language addressing precisely how and to whom discretionary rights may be exercised, since generic "sole discretion" boilerplate will no longer foreclose an implied-covenant challenge on its own.
