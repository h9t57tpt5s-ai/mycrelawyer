Lampton Securities Partnership, LLP v. Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government (Reverse Condemnation Ripeness Ruling)

Kentucky's Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal of Lampton Securities' reverse condemnation suit over an Ohio River floodplain tract, holding the claim wasn't ripe because Lampton never sought a variance from the floodplain board.

Lampton Securities Partnership, LLP sued Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government and the Louisville and Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District for reverse (inverse) condemnation, alleging that 1997 floodplain ordinances stripped all economically beneficial use from a roughly two-acre Ohio River tract it bought for $800,000 in 1998. On October 30, 2026, the Kentucky Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for the government defendants, holding that Lampton's claim was not ripe because it never applied to the Metro Sewer District's Floodplain Board for a variance -- relief the ordinance made available and that the Board had granted to other landowners.

Reverse condemnation claim over a 2-acre tract Lampton bought for $800,000 in 1998; the opinion states no damages figure sought
Oct 30, 2026 Court of Appeals of Kentucky

New Jersey's Appellate Division affirmed a zoning board's approval letting a historic Point Pleasant Beach building be converted into a restaurant, spa, and boutique hotel, rejecting a neighboring owner's repeat variance challenge.

The Appellate Division of New Jersey's Superior Court affirmed a trial court's dismissal of a neighboring commercial property owner's prerogative-writs challenge to use variances the Point Pleasant Beach Zoning Board of Adjustment granted for a mixed-use redevelopment of the historic Gottlieb Building. RJ's International Trading, LLC v. Crown Castle South, LLC (Florida Supreme Court: Easement Attorney's-Fee Clauses Don't Run With the Land)

Answering a certified question from the Eleventh Circuit, Florida's Supreme Court held an easement's prevailing-party attorney's-fee clause is a personal covenant that doesn't bind successors like Crown Castle.

The Florida Supreme Court, answering a certified question from the Eleventh Circuit, held on October 29, 2026 that a prevailing-party attorney's-fee provision in an easement agreement is a personal covenant that does not run with the land. Crown Castle South, LLC therefore cannot be forced to pay RJ's International Trading, LLC's attorney's fees under a fee clause that Crown Castle's predecessors-in-interest, not Crown Castle itself, agreed to decades earlier, even though Crown Castle was bound by -- and lost on -- the underlying easement and trespass claims.

$40,001 jury verdict ($1 for breach of easement, $40,000 for trespass); the attorney's-fee amount actually in dispute is not stated in the opinion
Oct 29, 2026 Supreme Court of Florida

Seneca Ins. Co. Inc. v. Cobleskill Enter., LLP (Smoke-Detector Warranty Breach Voids Hotel Fire Coverage)

New York's Third Department ruled Seneca Insurance owes no coverage for a 2018 hotel and bowling-alley fire, finding the owner breached its policy's smoke-detector warranty as a matter of law.

New York's Appellate Division, Third Department, ruled on October 29, 2026 that Seneca Insurance Company Inc. owes no coverage for a December 2018 fire at a hotel, restaurant and bowling-alley complex owned by Cobleskill Enterprise, LLP, reversing a trial court and holding that the owner breached its policy's protective safeguards endorsement, a warranty requiring operational smoke detectors throughout the property, as a matter of law. The court found the breach materially increased the risk of loss under New York Insurance Law § 3106 given testimony that multiple areas of the complex, including the pool, bowling alley, basement and dining room, had no functioning smoke detectors at all.

Coverage dispute over fire damage to a hotel, restaurant and bowling-alley complex; no dollar figure for the loss is stated in the opinion
Oct 29, 2026 Appellate Division, Third Department, New York

Portage Township of St. Joseph County, Indiana v. City of South Bend (Eminent Domain in Redevelopment Areas Requires Council Authorization)

Indiana's Court of Appeals reversed a condemnation action against a developer's property, holding only a city's legislative body, not its mayor, can authorize eminent domain in a redevelopment project area.

The Indiana Court of Appeals ruled on October 29, 2026 that a city's legislative body, not its mayor or other officials, holds exclusive authority to exercise eminent domain over property inside a statutorily designated redevelopment project area, reversing a trial court that had allowed the City of South Bend to pursue a condemnation action against property owned by Argos 316, LLC without a vote of the South Bend Common Council. Objectors Portage Township, which had purchased the property after the condemnation suit was filed, and 350 Columbia Street, LLC, which held easement rights over it, successfully argued the City's suit was unauthorized under Indiana Code section 36-7-14-20(a).

Not stated; dispute concerns authority to condemn, not a damages or compensation figure
