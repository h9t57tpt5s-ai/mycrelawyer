# North Carolina Judge Trims Developer's Zoning Denial Lawsuit, But Core Claim Survives Against Hendersonville

### A Miami-based multifamily developer's suit over a rejected 180-unit rezoning loses two of three counts, leaving an "arbitrary and capricious" claim intact and offering a case study in how far courts will let municipalities go in denying rezoning requests

Commercial developers challenging a local government's rezoning denial got a mixed, but instructive, result out of North Carolina this month. On September 1, 2026, Henderson County Superior Court Judge Troy Stafford dismissed two of the three claims brought by Advenir Azora Development, LLC against the City of Hendersonville over the city council's January 2026 denial of a rezoning request for a 180-unit multifamily project on Haywood Road, while allowing the developer's core claim — that the denial was "arbitrary and capricious" — to proceed toward a hearing on the merits. The ruling matters well beyond Hendersonville: it illustrates both the narrow legal theories available to a rejected developer and the one theory, judicial review of the underlying zoning decision itself, that tends to survive a motion to dismiss even when statutory-authority and due-process theories do not.

Advenir Azora, a Miami-based builder known for duplex, triplex, and quadplex rental communities, had spent more than a year pursuing a zoning change for a 21-acre tract at 1741 Haywood Road, branded the LEO Hendersonville project. The proposal would have replaced the site's existing single-family subdivision entitlement with a denser mix of attached rental units. After the city council voted unanimously in January 2026 to deny the rezoning, Advenir Azora filed suit in March, alleging the council's denial exceeded its statutory zoning authority, violated the developer's due process rights, and was, independently, arbitrary and capricious given the evidence presented at the public hearing.

The city moved to dismiss all three counts. In his September 1 order, Judge Stafford agreed with the city on two of them, finding the complaint did not plead facts sufficient to show the council acted outside the scope of its statutory zoning power or that its process denied Advenir Azora constitutionally adequate process. But the court declined to dismiss the arbitrary-and-capricious claim, which functions in North Carolina zoning litigation as something close to a certiorari-style review of whether the council's stated reasons for denial are supported by competent, material, and substantial evidence in the record. That claim will now proceed to further briefing and, eventually, a hearing on whether the council's stated rationale for rejecting the LEO Hendersonville rezoning actually holds up.

For commercial real estate owners, developers, and their counsel, the ruling is a useful data point on how North Carolina trial courts are triaging this increasingly common category of dispute: a developer whose rezoning application is voted down and responds with a multi-count complaint layering procedural and constitutional theories on top of a more fact-intensive "was this decision supported by the record" claim. Statutory-authority and due-process claims, which require a developer to show something closer to bad faith or a fundamentally broken process, are proving difficult to plead past the motion-to-dismiss stage on generalized allegations of a "whimsical, willful and unreasonable" denial, the language Advenir Azora's complaint originally used. The arbitrary-and-capricious theory, by contrast, puts the burden back on the record built during the public hearing process, and Judge Stafford's order suggests that theory is enough, standing alone, to keep a rezoning-denial suit alive.

The case also carries a practical lesson about pacing: the city has now won two rounds in a motion practice that has stretched from the March filing through a September ruling, without resolving the underlying question of whether the LEO Hendersonville rezoning denial itself was defensible. Municipal defendants facing similar suits should expect that a well-pleaded arbitrary-and-capricious count is likely to survive even a partially successful motion to dismiss, meaning the fight typically shifts to the administrative record compiled at the public hearing rather than ending with the pleadings. For developers, the ruling reinforces that the administrative record made at the rezoning hearing — the traffic, density, and infrastructure evidence presented, and the specificity of the reasons a governing board gives for denial — is likely to be the single most consequential piece of evidence in any later court challenge, more so than allegations about the board's motives or process.

Practical takeaways: developers pursuing a rezoning application should build a hearing record methodically, assuming it may later become the central evidentiary battleground in litigation if the application is denied; complaints challenging a rezoning denial should lead with a well-supported arbitrary-and-capricious claim rather than relying primarily on statutory-authority or due-process theories, which North Carolina trial courts appear more willing to dismiss at the pleadings stage; and municipalities should ensure that denial votes are accompanied by specific, evidence-based findings in the public record, since generalized or conclusory reasons for denial are the likeliest vulnerability once a case reaches the arbitrary-and-capricious stage of review.
