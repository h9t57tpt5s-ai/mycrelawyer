# Wireless Developer Sues Maine Town Over Zoning Rule That Confines Cell Towers to a Narrow Road Corridor

### Blue Sky Towers' federal suit against Waldoboro tests how far a facially neutral dimensional standard can go before it becomes an unlawful barrier to wireless build-out under federal law

A wireless infrastructure developer has taken a Maine town to federal court over a zoning provision that, on its face, looks like an ordinary dimensional standard but that the developer says functions as a near-total ban on new cell towers across the vast majority of the town's land area. On September 11, 2026, Blue Sky Towers IV LLC filed suit against the Town of Waldoboro, its Board of Appeals, and its Planning Board in the U.S. District Court for the District of Maine, challenging the town's denial of a variance that would have allowed construction of a 194-foot self-support tower intended to close a documented Verizon coverage gap in South Waldoboro. The case is a useful illustration, for any commercial developer navigating local wireless-siting rules, of how the federal Telecommunications Act constrains even facially neutral local zoning provisions.

Waldoboro's land-use ordinance permits wireless telecommunications facilities in the town's Rural Zoning District with Planning Board site-plan approval, but a separate provision limits such facilities to locations within 300 yards of Route 1, the town's main highway corridor. Blue Sky's proposed site, at 2734 Friendship Road in South Waldoboro, sits roughly five miles from Route 1 — well outside the permitted zone. Blue Sky sought a dimensional variance from the 300-yard restriction, the standard mechanism by which a landowner asks a zoning board of appeals to excuse a specific numerical or locational requirement where strict application would cause undue hardship. The town's own attorney, however, concluded that the 300-yard rule is not truly a dimensional "setback" at all, but instead functions as a location requirement akin to an overlay zone — and that Maine law limits the categories of standards eligible for a dimensional variance in a way that left the Board of Appeals without jurisdiction to grant Blue Sky's request. Following that advice, the Board unanimously denied the variance on August 20, 2026.

Blue Sky's complaint invokes Section 332(c)(7) of the Telecommunications Act of 1996, the federal provision that has governed local wireless-siting disputes since it was enacted specifically to prevent state and local governments from using their traditional zoning authority to functionally block wireless carriers from filling coverage gaps. The complaint alleges that Waldoboro's denial violates the Act's prohibition on local decisions that have "the effect of prohibiting the provision of personal wireless services," a standard that courts have applied where a locality's rules leave a carrier with no feasible way to close a demonstrated coverage gap anywhere within a meaningful service area. Blue Sky also alleges the denial was not supported by "substantial evidence" in the written record, a separate and independently enforceable requirement under the same statutory section, and raises a state-law claim that the Board of Appeals abused its discretion and committed legal error by declining to even consider the effective-prohibition issue before denying the variance. Notably, the complaint asks the court for expedited review, a procedural track Section 332(c)(7)(B)(v) expressly contemplates for challenges to local wireless-siting decisions, reflecting Congress's judgment that these disputes should not linger on ordinary civil dockets while coverage gaps go unaddressed.

The dispute is not Blue Sky's first such fight in Maine's Midcoast region. A related entity, Blue Sky Towers III LLC, sued the neighboring town of Nobleboro earlier in 2026 after its Board of Appeals rejected a similar tower proposal, suggesting a broader pattern of friction between wireless build-out plans and small-town Maine zoning boards more accustomed to regulating setbacks for single-family homes than evaluating federal preemption standards. For commercial landowners and infrastructure developers generally, the case is a reminder that local governments retain substantial authority to regulate the siting, appearance, and location of wireless facilities and other infrastructure, but that authority has a hard federal floor: a local rule cannot, in practice, foreclose a wireless carrier's ability to close a demonstrated service gap, no matter how the rule is technically labeled under state zoning law.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A "setback" or "location requirement" that leaves only a narrow sliver of a municipality available for a use protected under federal law is vulnerable to a Section 332(c)(7) challenge even if it never mentions wireless facilities by name as a targeted prohibition.
- Local zoning boards that decline jurisdiction over a variance request without separately addressing an applicant's federal-preemption arguments risk a "failure to consider" claim distinct from, and in addition to, the underlying zoning dispute.
- Developers facing a wireless-siting denial should build a documented coverage-gap record before filing suit, since both the "effective prohibition" and "substantial evidence" standards under the Telecommunications Act turn heavily on the administrative record compiled below.
- Municipalities updating tower-siting ordinances should have zoning counsel stress-test any distance-based restriction against realistic coverage-gap scenarios, rather than assuming a numeric standard is automatically defensible simply because it does not single out wireless use by name.
