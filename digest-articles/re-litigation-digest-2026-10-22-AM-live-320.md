# Michigan Appeals Court Upholds $411K Award Against Tenant in "As Is" Commercial Lease Fight

### A tire retailer that walked away from a Detroit storefront over a sprinkler-system dispute loses its bid to undo a six-figure arbitration judgment

A commercial tenant that abandoned a leased Detroit storefront after the city flagged code violations has lost its appeal to undo a $411,131.50 arbitration award in favor of its landlord, after the Michigan Court of Appeals held the tenant—not the property owner—bore the risk of bringing the space into compliance under an "as is" commercial lease.

The case, *Trucks Rx, Inc. v. Padilla Investments, LLC* (Mich. Ct. App. No. 375909, decided October 21, 2026), is a reminder to commercial tenants that a lease's "as is" and compliance clauses can allocate the cost of unanticipated code requirements entirely to the tenant, even when the landlord played no role in creating the problem and even when curing it would be expensive.

### What happened

Padilla Investments, LLC owns a commercial building in Detroit that had previously operated as a hardware store. In February 2022, Trucks Rx, Inc. and an affiliated company, Jana Trucking Line, Inc., signed a three-year commercial lease for the space, intending to use it to sell tires. The tenants' principals personally guaranteed the lease. Under its terms, the tenants took the premises "as is," agreed to comply with all applicable laws and ordinances at their own expense, and accepted responsibility for insurance, utilities, and property taxes. The lease limited use of the property to selling new vehicle tires.

Trouble started almost immediately. Within two months of taking possession, the City of Detroit inspected the property and issued a correction order citing property-maintenance and zoning violations. A second inspection in June 2022 produced an emergency correction order, including a finding that the premises lacked a permit for its actual use. By July 2022, the tenants had stopped paying rent. An August 2022 inspection found the building was "operating illegally" as an auto-repair and truck-storage facility, when its only legal use was retail. The central defect was the absence of an automatic sprinkler system, required to store tires on the site—a fix the tenants estimated would cost more than $60,000 and take four to six months. The landlord's principal offered to accompany the tenant to a meeting with city officials to work out a path to compliance, but the tenant did not attend. In September 2022, the tenants abandoned the premises; two months later, the landlord obtained a certificate of compliance for ordinary retail use.

Padilla Investments sued for breach of contract in October 2022, and the tenants counterclaimed. The parties agreed to arbitrate. The arbitrator found the tenants breached the lease by stopping rent payments and vacating the premises, holding that the lease unambiguously placed the burden of code compliance on the tenants—who took the property "as is" and agreed to perform whatever work was necessary to operate legally. The arbitrator rejected the tenants' argument that the lease was void or unenforceable, awarded Padilla Investments $411,131.50 plus interest, and held the tenants' principals personally liable under their guaranties. The trial court confirmed the award over the tenants' objection, and the tenants appealed.

### The ruling

The Michigan Court of Appeals affirmed in an unpublished per curiam opinion, applying the deferential standard that governs judicial review of arbitration awards: a court may not revisit the merits, even if it would have decided the case differently, and can vacate an award only for fraud, partiality, or where the arbitrator exceeded the scope of his contractual authority. The panel held the arbitrator neither exceeded his authority nor committed a clear error of law in reading the lease to require the tenants, not the landlord, to obtain any permits and install any equipment needed for their intended use. The lease's "as is" clause and its general compliance-with-law provision were unambiguous, the court found, and the tenants' characterization of the sprinkler requirement as a mutual mistake did not change that allocation of risk.

### Practical takeaways

For commercial landlords, the decision reinforces that a well-drafted "as is" clause paired with a use-compliance covenant can shift the cost of an unanticipated code requirement onto the tenant, even where the landlord never represented the space was ready for the tenant's specific use and even when the fix is costly and time-consuming. For tenants and their counsel, the case is a caution against signing a narrow-use lease for raw or formerly differently-used space without first confirming, through due diligence or a landlord work letter, who pays if the intended use triggers new life-safety or zoning requirements—because once that risk is contractually assigned, an arbitrator's reading of the clause will get heavy deference on judicial review, leaving little room to revisit the allocation after the fact. Lenders and guarantors should also take note: the personal guaranties here converted what began as a corporate lease dispute into six-figure personal liability for the tenants' principals, underscoring how quickly a lease default can reach beyond the corporate tenant itself.
