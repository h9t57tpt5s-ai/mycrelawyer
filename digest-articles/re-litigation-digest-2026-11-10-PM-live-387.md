# A Grocery Chain's Gas Station, a Bottler's Spring, and a Lesson in Zoning Ordinance Drafting

### Pennsylvania's Commonwealth Court splits the difference in Bluetriton Brands v. Weis Markets, reversing on site-plan requirements but sending the special-exception fight back for another round

**Bangor, Pennsylvania — November 10, 2026.** Commercial landlords and retailers adding a new use to an existing shopping center often treat the zoning approval as the easy part, especially when the proposed use is permitted "by right" somewhere in the ordinance. A decision issued today by the Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania in *Bluetriton Brands, Inc. v. Washington Township Zoning Hearing Board and Weis Markets, Inc.* shows how quickly that assumption can unravel once a neighboring business with its own property interests at stake gets involved — and how an ambiguously drafted local ordinance can turn a routine site-plan approval into three years of litigation with no final answer yet.

Weis Markets, Inc., the regional grocery chain, operates a store under a lease at Bangor Plaza, a 20.3-acre planned development within Washington Township's Commercial District in Northampton County. In 2022, Weis applied to the Township's Zoning Hearing Board for a special exception to add a "Gas-N-Go" fuel station — six fueling positions under a canopy — on a pad previously approved for a now-vacant bank branch. The Board approved the application in February 2023, over the objection of Bluetriton Brands, Inc., which owns the Deer Park spring water brand and operates a commercial water-extraction business on fifteen parcels in the Township, several of which directly abut the Bangor Plaza site and lie downstream of it hydrologically.

Bluetriton's opposition was not the ordinary neighbor's land-use grievance. Through a licensed hydrogeologist and a petroleum-storage-systems expert, Bluetriton presented evidence that surface and groundwater flow southward from the Weis property toward the Greenwaltz Spring, the primary source for its Deer Park bottling operation, and that the proposed fuel station's planned effluent discharge into a stormwater basin forming part of that same flow path created a meaningful risk of gasoline contamination. Its expert also pointed to Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection records showing that roughly half of Weis's existing Gas-N-Go locations statewide had drawn DEP notices of violation for storage-tank compliance failures, including undetected releases and inadequate spill controls.

On appeal, the Court of Common Pleas of Northampton County sided with Bluetriton and reversed both the Board's site-plan approval and its grant of the special exception. Weis then appealed to the Commonwealth Court, arguing three things: that no special exception was required at all because a fuel station is permitted by right in the Commercial District; that, separately, no site plan was required given ambiguous language in the Township's zoning and subdivision ordinances; and that even if a special exception were required, the ordinance set no specific standards for a fuel station use, so nothing ever shifted the evidentiary burden to Weis in the first place.

Writing for a unanimous panel, President Judge Renée Cohn Jubelirer agreed with Weis on the narrower site-plan question, holding that the ordinance's language on when a site plan must be submitted was genuinely ambiguous and that Weis therefore did not need to submit one as a precondition to proceeding. But the court declined to resolve the special-exception question on the merits. Because the trial court's order had not addressed all of the arguments Bluetriton raised challenging the Board's grant of the special exception, the Commonwealth Court remanded the case for the Court of Common Pleas to issue a new opinion reaching those remaining arguments — leaving open whether Weis ultimately gets to build its fuel station, more than four years after it first applied.

**Practical takeaways**

For retailers and shopping-center owners planning to add a new use — a fuel station, a drive-through, a drive-up pharmacy lane — to an existing commercial development, this case is a reminder that "permitted by right" and "no approval needed" are not the same thing, and that an ordinance's site-plan and special-exception provisions should be checked independently rather than assumed to track each other. A neighboring business with a genuine, quantifiable property interest — here, a groundwater source feeding a national beverage brand — can mount an evidentiary record sophisticated enough to survive a Zoning Hearing Board approval and win at the trial-court level, so counsel should not assume a favorable board decision forecloses serious appellate risk. Compliance history matters beyond the four corners of the application: a pattern of regulatory violations at a chain's other locations became part of the evidentiary record on whether the proposed use posed a genuine risk at this site. And because the Commonwealth Court remanded rather than resolving the special-exception question outright, owners facing a similar multi-issue zoning appeal should expect that a partial win on one ground can still leave the central approval unresolved for years, with real carrying and opportunity costs for the vacant pad in the meantime.
