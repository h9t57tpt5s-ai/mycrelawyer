# Connecticut Appeals Court Affirms $16.8M Verdict Against City Over Reckless Permitting of Commercial Construction Project

### In Girolametti v. City of Danbury, the Connecticut Appellate Court held that a municipality and its building official can be held liable in recklessness for how they handled permitting and inspection of a private commercial build-out — a ruling with real exposure implications for cities nationwide.

For commercial property owners, the local building department can feel like just another box to check before a project breaks ground. A recent Connecticut appellate decision is a reminder that when a municipality's permitting and inspection process goes badly wrong, the city itself — not just the contractor — can end up on the hook for millions of dollars. On March 17, 2026, the Connecticut Appellate Court largely affirmed a $16.8 million jury verdict against the City of Danbury and one of its building officials, arising out of the permitting, construction, and inspection of a 30,000-square-foot commercial addition to a retail business called Party Depot.

The case, *Girolametti v. Larrabee*, 238 Conn. App. 1 (2026), traces back to a five-week jury trial in Connecticut Superior Court in Waterbury, which concluded in October 2023 with a verdict in favor of plaintiffs John and Cindy Girolametti and their company, Party Depot, Inc. The jury found that the City of Danbury and building official Edward Schullery acted with reckless disregard for the health and safety of the public in the course of permitting, constructing, and inspecting the addition to the Girolamettis' commercial building. The trial court entered final judgment on the verdict in March 2024, totaling $16,843,750, plus taxable costs of $37,648.88 and statutory post-judgment interest that has continued to accrue since the trial court's ruling — pushing the city's total exposure, by some estimates, toward $20 million.

Danbury appealed, and a three-judge panel of the Connecticut Appellate Court — Judges Seeley, Wilson, and Keller — largely rejected the city's arguments. The panel's one adjustment was a $280,000 remittitur tied to money the plaintiffs had already recovered from a settling party before trial; apart from that offset, the appellate court left the jury's recklessness finding and the resulting damages award intact, and remanded the case to the trial court to apply the remittitur and recalculate statutory interest. According to reporting on the decision, Danbury officials have said they intend to seek further review from the Connecticut Supreme Court, arguing the ruling exposes municipalities to outsized liability for the individual conduct of building department employees.

What makes the case significant for commercial property owners and developers — not just litigators who handle construction disputes — is the theory of liability the jury and the Appellate Court sustained. Connecticut, like most states, affords municipalities and their employees broad governmental immunity for discretionary acts such as building inspections, precisely because inspection and permitting decisions typically involve the exercise of judgment calls that courts are reluctant to second-guess after the fact. That immunity, however, has long had an exception for conduct that rises to the level of recklessness — a markedly higher bar than ordinary negligence, but one the jury in this case found the evidence satisfied over a five-week trial covering the entire permitting, construction, and inspection history of the project.

The practical lesson is that a commercial permitting dispute with a municipality is not always confined to administrative appeals or injunctive relief forcing a city to issue or honor a permit. Where a city's own conduct in the permitting and inspection process causes a property owner substantial, provable damages — and where that conduct can be framed as more than a good-faith discretionary misjudgment — governmental immunity may not be a complete shield, and a jury may be willing to award damages that rival or exceed the cost of the underlying project itself. For a modest, family-owned retail business, a verdict approaching $17 million (before interest) against a city represents an extraordinary outcome, and one that required years of litigation and a lengthy jury trial to achieve.

For municipalities and the officials who staff their building and planning departments, the decision is a caution that how a permitting and inspection process is documented and conducted matters well beyond whether a project ultimately gets built. Departments that deviate sharply from their own ordinances, codes, or established practice during a contentious or high-profile project create exposure that general discretionary-function immunity may not cover if a plaintiff can later show the deviation was not merely mistaken but reckless.

For commercial owners and developers navigating a difficult relationship with a local building department, the case offers encouragement that recklessness claims against a municipality are viable in the right circumstances, but it also underscores how much proof such a claim demands: years of litigation, a full trial record, and appellate review before the award becomes final — and even then, with further review still possible.

**Practical takeaways:**
- Governmental immunity for discretionary permitting and inspection decisions is strong but not absolute; a well-documented pattern of conduct that departs sharply from a municipality's own codes or practices can support a recklessness claim capable of defeating that immunity.
- Commercial owners who believe a city's building department is treating their project in bad faith should preserve a complete paper trail — permit applications, inspection reports, correspondence, and any statements by officials — since that record is what ultimately proved decisive at trial and on appeal.
- A verdict of this size against a municipality, even once affirmed on appeal, is not necessarily final; further review (here, a potential petition to the Connecticut Supreme Court) and post-judgment proceedings on remittitur and interest can extend the timeline and final dollar amount considerably.
- Municipal building departments should treat this decision as a reminder that discretionary-function immunity is not a blanket protection, particularly where a project has become politically or publicly contentious and documented departures from standard practice could later be characterized as reckless rather than merely mistaken.
