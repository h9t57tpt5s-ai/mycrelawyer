# Cleveland Steps Into Fannie Mae's Foreclosure Case to Force Repairs at Downtown's Largest Apartment Tower

### A municipal intervention in a federal CRE foreclosure shows how code-enforcement leverage is shifting from City Hall to the courthouse

The City of Cleveland has asked a federal judge for a seat at the table in a foreclosure case it is not a party to — a procedural move with real teeth for any commercial landlord whose property falls into receivership while carrying open code violations. In late September 2026, lawyers for Mayor Justin Bibb's administration filed a motion to intervene in *Federal National Mortgage Association v. Reserve Apartments, Ltd.*, the foreclosure action pending in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio over Reserve Square, downtown Cleveland's largest apartment complex. The city argues that the receiver appointed to run the 23-story, roughly 1-million-square-foot tower at 1701 E. 12th Street is not moving fast enough on dozens of outstanding violations, and wants the court to make sure that whatever happens to the property next does not leave those problems unresolved.

The underlying case is a straightforward commercial mortgage default. Fannie Mae sued in July 2025 after Willoughby-based owner K&D Group failed to pay off or refinance a mortgage that had matured carrying more than $78 million in principal, interest, and other costs. The default followed a steep collapse in occupancy, driven largely by the loss of international-student tenants: Reserve Square's occupancy fell from roughly 93% in 2024 to a small fraction of that as more than 400 units turned over in a matter of months, a decline K&D has linked to federal immigration-policy shifts affecting the foreign-student population that had been a mainstay of the building's tenant base. By August 2025, K&D had ceded day-to-day control to a court-appointed receiver, Andrew Hayman of the Southfield, Michigan-based Hayman Company, whose job is to stabilize the asset and shepherd it toward a sale. Judge Bridget Meehan Brennan is presiding. The property, now reported to be around 80% vacant, is currently listed for sale.

What makes this a case worth watching beyond Cleveland is the legal mechanism the city is using. Rather than pursuing housing-code enforcement through municipal court — the traditional venue, and one that can be slow and largely toothless against a property already in receivership — the city is asking to intervene directly in the federal foreclosure docket. City lawyers point to 19 open violation notices covering 52 distinct code issues, including elevator outages, water intrusion, and deterioration in the parking garage, and argue the receiver has not adequately addressed them. The ask is narrow but consequential: give the city standing to be heard before the court approves any transfer of the property, so a sale cannot simply pass the building — and its violations — to a new owner without the issues being confronted first.

For commercial property owners and their lenders, the filing is a reminder that a receivership does not insulate a distressed asset from municipal oversight, and that cities are increasingly willing to seek party status in federal proceedings rather than wait for separate state-court code enforcement to run its course. Receivers are typically tasked with preserving asset value for creditors, which is not always the same thing as the capital-intensive repairs a municipality wants prioritized; an intervention lets a city argue directly to the judge supervising the case that life-safety and habitability issues deserve priority alongside — or even ahead of — a clean sale process. If the court grants intervention, it could also shape how the eventual buyer underwrites the deal, since any purchaser would need to factor in enforceable repair obligations rather than simply inheriting a distressed building free of that overhang.

The case also illustrates a now-familiar pattern in CRE lending: a single-tenant-base concentration risk — here, international students — turning what looked like a stable, high-occupancy asset into a rapid default once that tenant pool evaporated. Lenders underwriting multifamily assets with concentrated institutional, university-adjacent, or otherwise narrow tenant bases should take note of how quickly occupancy, and with it debt-service coverage, can collapse when that single source dries up.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A receivership does not automatically resolve, or shield an owner from, outstanding municipal code violations; cities are increasingly seeking formal intervention in federal foreclosure cases to keep enforcement leverage alive through a sale.
- Prospective buyers of distressed multifamily assets in receivership should expect — and underwrite for — the possibility that a municipality will seek conditions on any court-approved transfer tied to unresolved code violations.
- Lenders and sponsors should stress-test occupancy assumptions for any asset with a concentrated, narrow tenant base (international students, a single corporate tenant, a university-affiliated population), since the loss of that base can produce an unusually fast default.
