# Grand Forks Bank's $7.86M Guaranty Suit Lands in Bankruptcy Court as Borrower Entities Seek Chapter 11 Protection

### First Western Bank & Trust says a personal guarantor's companies stopped paying on commercial real estate-secured loans more than a year ago — and now wants summary judgment before a group of affiliated borrowers' Chapter 11 cases can slow it down.

A commercial lending dispute out of Grand Forks, North Dakota, moved from state court to federal bankruptcy court this month, after the guarantor named in a $7.86 million loan-default suit removed the case following his affiliated companies' Chapter 11 filings. The underlying lawsuit, *First Western Bank & Trust v. Rabadi*, pending in Grand Forks County District Court before its removal, illustrates a recurring risk for commercial lenders: a guaranty suit against an individual can be derailed, at least procedurally, once the borrower entities standing behind the debt seek bankruptcy protection.

First Western Bank & Trust's complaint alleges that Dr. Khaled Rabadi personally guaranteed commercial loans made to three affiliated borrowers: Kidney and Hypertension Center, PLC; KDJ Investment Group, LLC; and KDJ Properties, LLC. The bank's complaint also names a fourth company, Washington Properties, LLC, as a defendant. According to the bank's filing, the guaranties Rabadi signed were unlimited and unconditional, meaning he agreed to stand behind the full amount of the borrowers' obligations without the kind of dollar caps or carve-outs that limited guaranties typically include.

The bank's complaint puts the total owed at approximately $7.86 million as of April 6, 2026, a figure that does not yet include late fees, additional interest, and unpaid property taxes that have continued to accrue. The default narrative described in the filing follows a familiar pattern for distressed commercial borrowers: the companies began missing scheduled loan payments starting in July 2025, then failed to make a final balloon-style payment that came due on December 25, 2025 on at least one of the loans, and separately fell behind on property tax obligations tied to the real estate securing the debt. The bank's asserted collateral package includes clinic equipment and assets along with mortgages on property held by the affiliated borrower entities — meaning the dispute is not simply an unsecured personal-guaranty claim, but one anchored in real property pledged by the operating companies themselves.

Rabadi did not default on the litigation itself. Court records show he filed an answer in May 2026 denying liability and asserting affirmative defenses including estoppel, waiver, and unclean hands. The bank has since moved for summary judgment, arguing that Rabadi's answer does not actually dispute that the underlying debt exists or that he personally signed the guaranties at issue — framing the defenses as insufficient to create a genuine factual dispute regardless of how they are ultimately decided.

The litigation's posture changed sharply on October 16, 2026, when seven affiliated entities tied to the Rabadi-controlled companies filed Chapter 11 petitions in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of North Dakota, listing Grand Forks as their principal place of business. Rabadi then filed a notice of removal on November 2, 2026, moving the bank's state-court guaranty suit into the bankruptcy forum. A judge overseeing the matter has given Rabadi until November 30, 2026 to respond to the bank's pending summary judgment motion, keeping the underlying guaranty question very much alive even as it now proceeds under the bankruptcy court's supervision rather than in the state district court where the bank originally filed.

The interplay between the personal guaranty suit and the borrower entities' Chapter 11 cases is the part of this matter most worth watching. An individual guarantor is not automatically protected by his companies' bankruptcy filing — the automatic stay generally applies to the debtor entities, not to a non-debtor guarantor sued in his individual capacity — but removal to the bankruptcy court can still change the practical dynamics of the case, including which judge decides the pending summary judgment motion and how that decision interacts with the broader restructuring of the borrower entities' secured debt.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Lenders holding unlimited, unconditional personal guaranties on commercial real estate-secured loans should expect a borrower-side bankruptcy filing to complicate, even if it does not legally stay, collection efforts against the individual guarantor; removal to the bankruptcy court can shift the forum and timeline even when the automatic stay itself does not reach the guarantor directly.
- A guaranty suit built on a clear admission of signature and loan terms, paired with an answer that raises only equitable defenses like estoppel or waiver without disputing the debt's existence, is a strong candidate for summary judgment — but lenders should be prepared for that motion to be decided in a different forum than the one where they filed.
- When a loan's collateral package spans both operating-company assets (here, clinic equipment) and real property held by affiliated holding entities, lenders should track the real estate collateral separately through any Chapter 11 case, since the bankruptcy court's treatment of the mortgaged property can diverge from how the personal guaranty claim against the individual is ultimately resolved.
- Borrowers and guarantors structuring commercial lending relationships through multiple affiliated entities should recognize that a coordinated Chapter 11 filing across the operating and property-holding entities does not, by itself, shield an individual guarantor from a parallel action on the guaranty — it principally affects where and how quickly that action proceeds.
