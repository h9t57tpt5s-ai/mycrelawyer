# Second Cincinnati Tower Admits Default as Rubenstein Partners' Twin Foreclosures Converge

### A Philadelphia sponsor's admission that it defaulted on a $46 million loan secured by a 26-story downtown Cincinnati office tower marks the second of two related foreclosure suits from the same lender — and the latest marker in a wave of court-ordered receiverships remaking the city's office skyline.

Philadelphia-based real estate investment firm Rubenstein Partners has admitted, in an August 12, 2026 answer filed in the Hamilton County Court of Common Pleas, that it defaulted on the $46.1 million mortgage loan secured by 312 Elm Street, a 26-story, 378,000-square-foot office tower in downtown Cincinnati. The admission comes just over two months after lender Wilmington Trust National Association — a Delaware-based affiliate of M&T Bank Corp. — filed suit on June 7, 2026, seeking to foreclose and recover $39.6 million in outstanding principal, interest, late charges, and fees. It is the second time in under two years that the same lender has sued the same sponsor over a Cincinnati office tower acquired in the same 2015 transaction, and it deepens a foreclosure wave that has now touched five downtown high-rises.

The 312 Elm Street tower, completed in 1992 and ranked as downtown Cincinnati's 15th-largest office building, is roughly 45.7% occupied — a vacancy level typical of the post-pandemic flight to quality that has hollowed out older, amenity-light Central Business District stock nationally. A court-appointed receiver, Paul Plattner of Colliers Real Estate Management Services, took over day-to-day management of the property last month, making 312 Elm the third downtown Cincinnati office building to pass into receivership this cycle, joining the historic Netherland Plaza Hotel and the tower at 600 Vine Street.

The Elm Street case does not stand alone. Wilmington Trust, holding a separate note originated the same year, sued Rubenstein Partners in the same Hamilton County court on December 30, 2024 over 312 Plum Street — a smaller, 12-story tower a block away that the Cincinnati Enquirer occupied as its headquarters until relocating to Plum Street itself in February 2023. That suit alleges Rubenstein owes more than $16 million on an original $18.4 million loan and separately claims the ownership entity fell delinquent on payments to vendors servicing the property. Rubenstein acquired both buildings in a single 2015 transaction, financed the purchases with loans that ultimately landed in the same lender's hands, and now faces materially identical default allegations on both.

For a lender, litigating two related foreclosures against the same sponsor in the same courthouse offers procedural efficiencies — overlapping counsel, similar loan documents, and a judge and local bar already familiar with the underlying facts — but it also signals something more significant to the market: a single sponsor relationship generating cascading defaults across a concentrated local portfolio. That pattern is often a leading indicator that the borrower's distress is structural rather than asset-specific, tied to portfolio-wide leverage or liquidity rather than the performance of any one building. Rubenstein's unqualified admission in its Elm Street answer — acknowledging "each and every" allegation, including the default itself — forecloses factual disputes and narrows the litigation to remedy, positioning the case for a comparatively fast path to judgment and eventual sheriff's sale, a trajectory the Plum Street matter, filed a year and a half earlier, is already further along.

The Cincinnati towers are not an isolated episode. Local commercial real estate reporting has tallied 312 Elm as the fifth downtown high-rise office building to face a foreclosure action in the current cycle, part of a broader pattern of special servicers and institutional lenders moving from forbearance to litigation more quickly as anchor-tenant departures, elevated interest rates on floating and maturing debt, and depressed appraisals continue to erode office collateral values in secondary and gateway markets alike.

**Practical takeaways.** Owners and lenders should treat parallel defaults by a single sponsor across multiple, otherwise-unrelated loans as a structural distress signal warranting portfolio-level diligence, not asset-by-asset triage. Sponsors weighing whether to contest or concede a foreclosure complaint should recognize that an unqualified admission — as Rubenstein filed here — accelerates the case toward judgment and receivership and forecloses later factual defenses, so the decision merits the same scrutiny as a settlement. And market participants tracking CBD office distress should watch receivership appointments as an early, and increasingly reliable, signal of which buildings are heading toward a lender-driven sale rather than a negotiated workout.
