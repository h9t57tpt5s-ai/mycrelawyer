# Alabama Supreme Court Shields Fairhope Planning Commission From Developer's Negligence Suit Over Denied Apartment Projects

### A writ of mandamus underscores how far municipal and volunteer immunity reach when a city's land-use decisions draw a damages claim rather than a direct appeal

The Supreme Court of Alabama granted a writ of mandamus on October 16, 2026, directing a Baldwin County circuit court to enter judgment for the City of Fairhope and its Planning Commission on a developer's negligence claim over the denial of two apartment-complex applications, holding that both the volunteer members of the Commission and the city employees who advised them are immune from suit under Alabama law.

The case, *Ex parte City of Fairhope Planning Commission and City of Fairhope (In re: 68V BTR Holdings, LLC v. City of Fairhope Planning Commission and City of Fairhope)*, SC-2025-0803, traces back to applications that 68V BTR Holdings, LLC filed with Fairhope's Planning Commission to develop two townhome apartment complexes on separate parcels, one named "Skyline Village" and the other "Gables on Lawrence." According to the Commission's own meeting minutes, it denied the Gables application for adverse drainage effects, unmet greenspace requirements, inconsistency with the city's Comprehensive Plan, and traffic problems, and denied the Skyline application for drainage issues and traffic and general health-and-safety concerns.

Dissatisfied, 68V first sued the City and the Planning Commission in federal district court rather than seeking mandamus review in state court to compel compliance with Alabama's land-use statutes. After the federal court resolved 68V's federal claims against it and declined to exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining state-law claims, 68V refiled in Baldwin Circuit Court. Its operative complaint there asserted three claims: damages for the City's and Commission's alleged negligence in considering and denying its applications; equitable relief invalidating the denials as arbitrary and capricious; and damages for an alleged deprivation of property without substantive due process under the Alabama Constitution.

The City and the Commission moved for summary judgment on all three claims, arguing the negligence claim was barred by a combination of municipal immunity under Alabama Code Section 11-47-190, volunteer immunity for the Commission's unpaid members under the Alabama Volunteer Service Act, and substantive immunity. The circuit court denied that motion, and the City and Commission petitioned the Supreme Court for a writ of mandamus — but only as to the negligence claim, leaving the arbitrary-and-capricious and substantive-due-process claims to proceed in the trial court regardless of the outcome.

Writing for the Court, Justice Will Parker explained that 68V's negligence theory rested on respondeat superior: holding the City and Commission liable for the acts of their agents. That theory fails, the Court held, if the agents themselves are immune. The nine volunteer members of the Planning Commission, who serve without compensation under Alabama Code Section 11-52-3(b), qualify as volunteers for a governmental entity under the Volunteer Service Act, and their consideration and denial of 68V's applications fell within the scope of their official duties under the statute authorizing planning commissions to approve or disapprove development proposals. Because 68V did not allege the commissioners acted willfully or wantonly — only that they were negligent, careless, or unskillful, and separately that they acted in bad faith — the Court held the commissioners immune, citing precedent that Alabama's municipal-immunity statute shields even bad-faith conduct by municipal agents from ordinary negligence claims. With the agents immune, the Court held, the City and the Planning Commission could not be held vicariously liable, and it granted the writ directing the circuit court to enter judgment for the City and Commission on that one claim.

The opinion notes, without resolving, 68V's earlier allegations — aired in a prior federal lawsuit against the City — that a planning commissioner wanted the city to "feel pain" for the developer's past project disputes, allegations that reflect the friction behind the litigation even though they did not save 68V's negligence theory from the immunity defense.

Practical takeaways for commercial developers: a negligence claim against a city or its planning commission for denying a development application is likely to run headlong into both municipal immunity and, where volunteer board members are involved, the Volunteer Service Act's additional layer of protection, regardless of how the denial is characterized. Because that immunity extends to bad-faith conduct short of willful or wanton misconduct, developers who believe a planning body acted with an improper motive should expect that a straightforward negligence or tort claim is not the vehicle to test that theory. Developers frustrated by a land-use denial are generally better served pursuing a direct mandamus action or an administrative appeal of the denial itself — the route 68V bypassed in favor of federal and then negligence-based litigation — since equitable and due-process theories, unlike a tort claim against the decisionmakers personally, remain unaffected by this immunity ruling and continue in the trial court. Finally, the ruling is a reminder that suing first in federal court on constitutional theories, only to refile state-law claims after losing there, can cost a litigant time without adding legal traction once the claims land back in state court against immune defendants.
