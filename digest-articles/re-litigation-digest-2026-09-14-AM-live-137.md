# Silverback Founder Sentenced to Four Years for $13 Million Real Estate Investor Fraud

### A Manhattan federal judge closes the book on a Ponzi-like scheme built on promises of equity in New York City development projects, offering a stark reminder of the exposure sponsors face when investor communications outrun the truth.

Joshua Schuster, founder of the now-defunct New York City development firm Silverback Development, was sentenced on August 11, 2026 to four years in federal prison and three years of supervised release after pleading guilty to securities fraud for defrauding investors of more than $13 million between approximately 2018 and 2022. U.S. District Judge Valerie E. Caproni of the Southern District of New York imposed the sentence and approved a $13 million forfeiture order intended to compensate Schuster's victims, though prosecutors acknowledged they have been unable to identify assets sufficient to make investors whole. For real estate sponsors, fund managers, and the counsel who paper their offering documents, the case is a compact and unusually well-documented illustration of how quickly an equity-raise built on inflated representations can convert from a financing strategy into a federal prison sentence.

## From Development Pitch to Ponzi Scheme

Schuster built Silverback Development into a visible presence in New York City real estate, pitching investors on equity stakes in ground-up and value-add projects across Gramercy Park, Long Island City, the South Bronx, and other neighborhoods. According to the criminal information and the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York, Schuster raised money from investors by making inaccurate statements about how their funds would be used and by exaggerating Silverback's business reputation and track record. Rather than deploying the capital as represented, prosecutors alleged, Schuster diverted portions of it to cover more than $1 million in personal credit card payments, private-school tuition, and hundreds of thousands of dollars in gambling losses, while using new investor funds to pay off earlier investors in the pattern courts and prosecutors describe as Ponzi-like.

The scheme first drew public attention in May 2025, when the SEC filed a civil fraud action against Schuster and his entity, Schuster Enterprises LLC (doing business as Silverback), and federal prosecutors separately charged him criminally with wire fraud and securities fraud, each carrying a statutory maximum of 20 years. Schuster pleaded guilty to the securities fraud count on February 27, 2026, telling Judge Caproni in open court that he was "ashamed" of his conduct. At his sentencing hearing roughly five months later, Schuster's counsel argued for leniency on the theory that he had been sober for four years and was, in his own telling, no longer the person who lied to and stole from his investors.

## A Skeptical Sentencing Judge

Judge Caproni was not persuaded that Schuster's post-offense conduct reflected genuine rehabilitation rather than continued image management. In imposing the four-year sentence, she pointed to Schuster's relocation to Boca Raton, Florida, where he rented an expensive house, purchased a Ford Bronco, and had his wife driving a Mercedes-Benz, remarking that she did not believe he had given up the desire to appear successful. That observation goes to a recurring theme in white-collar sentencing: courts increasingly scrutinize a defendant's lifestyle and spending in the period between guilty plea and sentencing as evidence of whether contrition is genuine or performative, and defense narratives built primarily around sobriety or personal transformation carry less weight when a defendant's outward financial conduct has not meaningfully changed.

The forfeiture order underscores a separate, often underappreciated dynamic in investor-fraud prosecutions: a forfeiture judgment establishes a legal entitlement to recovery, but it does not manufacture assets where none exist. Prosecutors told the court they had not identified sufficient assets to satisfy the $13 million figure, meaning Schuster's victims may hold a formally recognized claim with limited practical prospect of full repayment, a gap that is common where a fraud's proceeds were spent on lifestyle expenses and prior investor payouts rather than preserved in traceable form.

## Why This Matters Beyond One Developer

For sponsors raising equity from individual or family-office investors on New York City development deals, the Schuster prosecution is a reminder that the line between aggressive marketing and criminal securities fraud is drawn by the accuracy of specific representations about fund usage and track record, not by the size or sophistication of the deal. Silverback's projects were real, located in identifiable neighborhoods, and pitched to investors who presumably received offering materials describing intended uses of capital; the fraud arose not from the absence of a business, but from the gap between what investors were told their money would fund and what it actually funded. That distinction matters for how general counsel and outside securities counsel should think about diligence: verifying that a sponsor's track record claims and use-of-proceeds representations are accurate, and that capital calls are tracked against stated purposes, is not a formality but a control that separates an ordinary underperforming investment from an indictable fraud.

The case also illustrates the parallel-track exposure sponsors face once fraud allegations surface. The SEC's civil action and the criminal prosecution proceeded on separate but related tracks, each capable of independently producing significant consequences, from an SEC bar and disgorgement order to a felony conviction and custodial sentence. Investors and their counsel evaluating potential claims against a sponsor accused of misusing capital should recognize that a criminal forfeiture order, while it establishes the amount owed, offers no guarantee of recovery, and that civil claims — including direct actions against a sponsor's other assets or against any professionals who facilitated the offering — often remain the more realistic path to recouping losses.

## Practical Takeaways

Real estate sponsors and fund managers should treat use-of-proceeds representations to investors as binding operational commitments, not marketing language, and should maintain documentation showing capital was deployed as represented in offering materials. Investors and their counsel conducting diligence on a sponsor's track record should independently verify prior project performance and reputation claims rather than relying on the sponsor's own characterization, particularly for repeat capital raises from the same investor base. Once fraud allegations surface, affected investors should pursue civil remedies promptly and in parallel with any criminal or SEC proceeding, since a criminal forfeiture order — even one entered by the court — does not guarantee that recoverable assets actually exist. Finally, courts' increasing willingness to scrutinize a defendant's post-plea lifestyle as evidence bearing on genuine remorse is a signal that any sponsor facing fraud allegations, and their counsel, should think carefully about how ongoing personal conduct will read to a sentencing judge.
