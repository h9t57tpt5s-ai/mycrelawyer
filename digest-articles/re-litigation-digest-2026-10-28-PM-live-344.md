# Dorchester Joint Venture Implodes: Copper Mill Accuses Partners of Secretly Shopping a $67.5M Development Site

### A Boston developer says its equity partners tried to kill a 754-unit project deal behind its back, then sought a buyer of their own

Copper Mill, the Boston-based developer behind a planned 754-unit residential tower project at 75 Morrissey Boulevard in Dorchester, has sued its joint-venture partners, Chicago-based POB Capital and Virginia-based Pactolus Private Wealth Management, accusing them of fraud and breach of contract after the venture controlling the site collapsed. The dispute, filed in Suffolk Superior Court and in a companion action in Massachusetts Land Court, is a reminder that commercial real estate joint ventures are only as durable as the capital behind them — and that a partner's quiet shopping of a shared asset can turn a stalled deal into a courtroom fight.

According to the complaints, the relationship traces back to December 18, 2024, when Copper Mill agreed to buy the 2.24-acre Morrissey Boulevard site from POB and Pactolus for $67.5 million. Rather than closing a straight sale, the parties restructured the transaction into a joint venture formed on September 4, 2025, with POB and Pactolus holding a 64.35% stake and Copper Mill holding 35.65%. Copper Mill's role was to serve as developer and manage entitlements and construction financing for the 754-unit project, while the two investment firms were expected to help fund the deal.

Copper Mill alleges that instead of fulfilling their capital obligations, POB and Pactolus worked behind the scenes to unwind the venture. The complaint claims the two firms quietly marketed the property to other prospective buyers while still bound by the joint-venture agreement, including shopping a pitch that valued the site at $82.5 million — well above the $67.5 million price Copper Mill had locked in. On June 30, 2026, POB and Pactolus sent Copper Mill a notice terminating the joint-venture agreement. Copper Mill's suit characterizes the investors' professed inability to raise capital as pretextual, alleging it was a deliberate tactic to engineer a termination and free the site for a more lucrative sale to a third party.

The complaints seek specific performance — asking the courts to enforce the original purchase-and-sale and joint-venture agreements and confirm Copper Mill's right to control and develop the site — along with damages for the alleged fraud. Copper Mill has said publicly that it intends to recover both its rights to the property and compensation for what it describes as its former partners' misconduct. Representatives for Copper Mill, POB Capital and Pactolus did not immediately respond to media inquiries when the suits became public in reporting in late October 2026.

For commercial real estate owners, developers and capital partners, the dispute illustrates a structural risk inherent in development joint ventures: a majority investor with functional control over capital calls can use a capital shortfall — real or engineered — as leverage to exit a deal it no longer favors, especially once market pricing moves. Here, the roughly $15 million gap between the locked-in $67.5 million price and the $82.5 million figure Copper Mill says its partners pitched to third parties is itself the likely source of the falling-out: if land values in the corridor rose meaningfully between the 2024 purchase agreement and 2026, a majority partner holding nearly two-thirds of the entity may have had every financial incentive to look for an exit that captured the upside for itself rather than splitting it according to the original deal terms.

The case also underscores why developers negotiating joint-venture and purchase-and-sale agreements for large sites should pay close attention to exclusivity and non-solicitation provisions governing a partner's ability to market the underlying asset, and to what happens procedurally when a majority partner invokes a capital-call default. A termination notice tied to a funding shortfall can look, on its face, like routine deal friction; whether it holds up legally often depends on contemporaneous evidence — in this case, the alleged marketing pitch at a higher valuation — showing the shortfall was manufactured rather than genuine. Developers on the minority side of a CRE joint venture should also consider recording protective interests, such as notices of agreement or lis pendens, before a dispute becomes public, since the value of specific-performance relief depends heavily on whether the property can still be transferred to a third party while litigation is pending.
