# AmBase Vehicle Accuses Apollo's ACREFI of Improper No-Consideration Fund Transfer in Steinway Tower Mezzanine Fight

### A new Summons with Notice filed in Manhattan Supreme Court escalates the years-long dispute over the 111 West 57th Street supertall, alleging ACREFI Mortgage moved money to its parent, Apollo Commercial Real Estate Finance, without getting anything in return.

The long-running litigation over the 111 West 57th Street condominium tower — known to New Yorkers as the Steinway Tower, one of the city's thinnest and tallest residential buildings — has produced a new chapter. On October 19, 2026, 111 West 57th Investment LLC, suing on its own behalf and derivatively for 111 West 57th Partners LLC and 111 West 57th Mezz 1 LLC, filed a Summons with Notice in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, New York County, against ACREFI Mortgage LLC and its indirect parent, Apollo Commercial Real Estate Finance, Inc. (NYSE: ARI). The filing, docketed under Index No. 655919/2026, alleges that ACREFI improperly transferred funds to ARI "for no consideration or equivalent value." The disclosure comes from AmBase Corporation's quarterly report filed October 28, 2026; AmBase is the ultimate corporate parent of the plaintiff entities and has carried the Steinway Tower litigation on its books since the project's underlying equity investment was impaired in 2017.

The new claim is the latest front in a sprawling legal battle that has pitted AmBase's investment vehicles against Apollo-affiliated lenders and the project's sponsors for nearly a decade. The dispute traces back to a $25 million junior mezzanine loan secured by the plaintiffs' equity interest in the Steinway Tower development. In 2017, Apollo-affiliated ACREFI sold that loan to Spruce Capital Partners LLC at par value, notwithstanding internal valuations that reportedly put the underlying equity at roughly $600 million. Two days after acquiring the loan, Spruce initiated a UCC Article 9 "strict foreclosure," seizing the pledged collateral and wiping out the plaintiffs' equity position in the project entirely. AmBase's vehicles sued Spruce and the project sponsor that same year in what has become known as the "Lender Action" (Index No. 655031/2017), seeking to unwind the foreclosure and recover damages.

That earlier fight reached the New York Court of Appeals, which ruled on May 28, 2026 that Apollo's contractual right to assign the mezzanine loan at its "sole discretion" did not, standing alone, shield the lender from a claim that the discretion was exercised in bad faith as part of a scheme to strip the plaintiffs' equity. The Court of Appeals reinstated the plaintiffs' implied-covenant-of-good-faith claim and sent the case back to the trial court, while leaving dismissal of related tortious-interference claims in place. That litigation remains pending at the Supreme Court, New York County.

The newly filed action is a distinct case rather than a continuation of the Lender Action, naming ACREFI and ARI — rather than Spruce or the sponsor — as defendants, and asserting a different theory: that ACREFI moved money up to its parent without consideration. AmBase's disclosure does not specify the amount allegedly transferred, nor does it identify a presiding judge, consistent with the filing being an initial Summons with Notice rather than a verified complaint. A Summons with Notice is a procedural device under New York practice that commences an action and preserves a plaintiff's filing date while a fuller complaint is prepared or demanded by the defendant.

This is not AmBase's first collateral attack on the Apollo lending relationship. In 2024, the same plaintiff entities sued the law firm Kasowitz Benson Torres LLP and attorney Douglas B. Heitner, alleging the firm represented the 111 West 57th entities in the development's financing while simultaneously representing parties with adverse interests, including the project's principals Michael Stern and Kevin Maloney and entities they control.

For commercial mortgage lenders and mezzanine investors, the new filing is a reminder that a victory in a strict foreclosure does not necessarily close the book on a sponsor or equity investor's claims — collateral attacks on intercompany transfers, assignment practices, and professional representation can surface years after the underlying default. Lenders structuring mezzanine debt in New York should expect continued scrutiny of "sole discretion" assignment language following the Court of Appeals' May 2026 ruling, and should be prepared to document the business rationale for intercompany fund movements between a special-purpose lending subsidiary and its parent, particularly where those movements follow or coincide with a foreclosure that wipes out a junior equity holder. For sponsors and joint-venture partners who have lost equity in a strict foreclosure, this filing illustrates that fraudulent-transfer and no-consideration theories remain an available avenue even after a Lender Action has run its course, provided the facts support them.
