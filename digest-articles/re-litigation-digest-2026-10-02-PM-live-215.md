# Oklahoma Securities Regulator Sues Vesta Realty Over Unanswered Subpoena

**The state agency charged with policing Oklahoma's securities markets has gone to court to force Tulsa-based multifamily platform Vesta Realty to turn over subpoenaed investor records, adding a regulatory enforcement track to a company already facing dozens of private lawsuits over its collapse.**

The Oklahoma Department of Securities filed suit against Vesta Realty, LLC on August 24, 2026, alleging the company failed to comply with a subpoena seeking documents that include the identities of, and communications with, investors in a number of the limited liability companies operated under the Vesta umbrella. The case, reported October 1, 2026 by Oklahoma Watch, is a civil enforcement action rather than a criminal proceeding, though the department's governing statute allows it to refer findings to criminal authorities and share investigative material with other law enforcement agencies.

## What Happened

According to the department, Vesta — founded and controlled by CEO Marc Kulick — did not produce the records sought by the subpoena, prompting the department's trial attorney, Shaun Mullins, to tell the court the agency is now asking for a judicial order compelling production. The subpoenaed material centers on investor identity and communications tied to several of the LLCs that make up Vesta's roughly $1 billion, nearly 10,000-unit multifamily portfolio across Oklahoma, Kansas, and Arkansas — precisely the kind of investor-facing information a securities regulator needs to evaluate whether Vesta's capital-raising practices complied with state law.

This is only the latest front in a rapidly multiplying set of legal problems for Kulick and the Vesta platform. Kulick faces at least 28 lawsuits filed in 2026 alone, including a civil racketeering suit filed by Kansas investor John Upperman in federal court in the Northern District of Oklahoma alleging mail and wire fraud and money laundering, and a separate 199-page fraud suit by former business partner Josef Loeffler accusing Kulick of diverting more than $37 million in investor and company funds. Multiple Vesta-affiliated properties have already changed hands through foreclosure, receivership, and deed-in-lieu transactions as the platform's finances have come apart.

## Why a Regulatory Subpoena Enforcement Action Matters

A securities regulator's subpoena-enforcement suit is a narrower and procedurally simpler creature than the private fraud litigation already pending against Vesta and Kulick — it does not require the department to prove fraud, only that a validly issued subpoena was not honored. But that narrowness is precisely what makes it a useful early signal for anyone tracking a distressed sponsor's exposure: a state securities regulator does not typically invest the resources to pursue document-production litigation unless it believes the underlying investor-protection concerns are serious enough to warrant a formal investigative record, independent of whatever civil plaintiffs are separately alleging in parallel lawsuits.

For investors, lenders, and joint-venture partners evaluating exposure to a multifamily or other CRE sponsor under financial stress, a regulator's subpoena enforcement action is also a leading indicator of where the next wave of disclosure is likely to come from. Once a court orders production, the records a regulator obtains — investor lists, capital-raising communications, internal accounting — can surface facts that reshape the private litigation already underway, inform settlement leverage in the parallel fraud and receivership proceedings, and in some cases form the basis for a subsequent enforcement action or criminal referral.

## Practical Takeaways

Sponsors and syndicators raising capital through multiple affiliated LLCs should treat a state securities regulator's subpoena with the same urgency as a court-issued discovery request, since non-compliance converts a confidential regulatory inquiry into a public enforcement lawsuit that compounds reputational and litigation risk on top of whatever underlying issue triggered the subpoena in the first place. Investors and lenders already in litigation with a distressed sponsor should track parallel regulatory proceedings closely, since court-compelled production in a regulatory case can generate discovery useful well beyond the regulator's own case. And counsel advising multifamily or other CRE platforms under financial strain should recognize that a pattern of unanswered inquiries — from private litigants, lenders, and regulators alike — tends to accelerate rather than defer the consequences of a sponsor's distress, as this case and Vesta's broader litigation history both illustrate.
