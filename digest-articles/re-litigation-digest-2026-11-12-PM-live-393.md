# Florida Appeals Court Splits the Baby in Medical-Office Landlord's Default Judgment

### Fourth DCA affirms a Boynton Beach landlord's win over a defaulting tenant and guarantor, but throws out the $85,782.80 damages award for lack of a properly noticed trial

Commercial landlords who win a default judgment against a vanished tenant often assume the hard part is over once liability is established. A November 12, 2026 decision from Florida's Fourth District Court of Appeal is a reminder that the hard part may just be starting — at least when it comes to collecting unliquidated damages.

In *First Care Therapy, LLC and Loodjhinssy Lafaille-Paul v. Congress Ave Boynton, LLC*, the Fourth DCA affirmed a Palm Beach County circuit court's finding that tenant First Care Therapy, LLC and its guarantor, Loodjhinssy Lafaille-Paul, breached a commercial lease and guaranty covering medical office space in Boynton Beach, Florida. But the appellate panel reversed the $85,782.80 damages award the trial court entered against the defaulted tenant and guarantor, holding that the award of unliquidated damages without a properly noticed damages trial violated well-established Florida procedure. The case now returns to the circuit court for a do-over on damages alone.

## From a Routine Default to an $85,782.80 Judgment

According to the opinion, First Care Therapy originally leased the Boynton Beach premises from a predecessor landlord, SNH Medical Office Properties Trust, in September 2021, with Lafaille-Paul personally guaranteeing the tenant's obligations. Congress Ave Boynton, LLC acquired the property in May 2024, and the prior landlord assigned the lease and guaranty to it. Roughly nine months later, on February 21, 2025, Congress Ave Boynton sued First Care Therapy and Lafaille-Paul for breach of the lease and guaranty, alleging nonpayment of rent. The complaint did not plead a sum-certain damages figure; instead it sought base rent, additional rent, operating expenses, late fees, taxes, and reletting costs still to be determined, attaching a lease ledger showing $34,337.91 in charges already outstanding.

Neither defendant answered the complaint, and the clerk entered a default against both in March 2025. Congress Ave Boynton then moved for final summary judgment, relying on an "Affidavit of Indebtedness" from its managing agent that pegged total damages at $85,782.80 — more than double the ledger figure — broken down into $48,480 in rent, $4,222 in legal fees to negotiate a new lease, $10,557 in a reletting commission, and $22,523 in tenant-improvement costs for the replacement tenant. At a July 2025 hearing, Lafaille-Paul appeared without counsel and no attorney appeared for the corporate tenant; the trial court heard argument rather than evidence and entered judgment for the exact amount Congress Ave Boynton had requested.

## The Fourth DCA Affirms Liability, Reverses the Damages Award

On appeal, First Care Therapy and Lafaille-Paul argued the judgment was void for lack of standing, lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, and a denial of due process because the corporate tenant appeared without counsel. Writing for the panel, Judge Alan O. Forst rejected all three arguments. The court held that Congress Ave Boynton's failure to attach the written assignment documenting the transfer of the lease from the prior landlord did not defeat its standing, because Florida's pleading rules required only that the lease and guaranty themselves be attached — and because the defaulting defendants had, by defaulting, admitted the well-pled allegation that the assignment occurred. The panel likewise found no due-process violation in the corporate tenant's unrepresented appearance, since a corporation's inability to appear pro se does not create a due-process problem when the corporation simply fails to retain counsel.

The damages award fared differently. Citing its own recent decision in *Affinity Health Partners, LLC v. Hayes Locums, LLC*, the Fourth DCA held that a default — even an uncontested one — entitles a landlord only to liquidated damages, not unliquidated damages, without a properly noticed evidentiary trial under Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.440(d). The court found the $85,782.80 figure plainly unliquidated: the complaint pleaded no sum certain, the attached ledger totaled less than half the final award, and the trial court could only reach the final number by crediting a post-default affidavit covering reletting commissions, tenant-improvement costs, and legal fees — all of which required weighing evidence rather than simple arithmetic. Because the July 2025 hearing was a non-evidentiary summary judgment proceeding rather than a noticed damages trial, the panel reversed the award and remanded for a proper trial on damages alone, leaving the landlord's underlying victory on liability untouched.

## Practical Takeaways

A commercial landlord who obtains a clerk's default against a nonpaying tenant should not assume that a motion for summary judgment, however well-supported by affidavits, can substitute for a properly noticed damages trial once the damages sought go beyond what the lease ledger shows on its face. Treating reletting commissions, negotiation fees, and tenant-improvement costs as "liquidated" simply because they appear in a sworn affidavit of indebtedness invites exactly the reversal Congress Ave Boynton now faces, costing the landlord more than a year of additional litigation to collect the same debt. Landlords represented in these matters should build in the extra procedural step — noticing an evidentiary hearing under Rule 1.440(d) — any time the damages sought exceed what the complaint's own exhibits establish with exactness, even against a defendant who never appears. The decision is also a reminder that a corporate tenant cannot defend itself without counsel, and that a guarantor's informal appearance does not cure that defect for the company itself, so landlords facing an unrepresented corporate defendant should expect the default to stand even as the damages calculation gets a second look.
