# Oregon Appeals Court Upholds $1.2M Verdict Over a Billboard Lease Gone Sideways on a Transferred Permit

### A building owner's decades-old sign-lease arrangement unraveled when a government permit needed to keep advertising on the wall ended up solely in a departing tenant's name — and the Oregon Court of Appeals says the jury was right to make the tenant pay for it.

A commercial landlord's hard-won jury verdict over a Portland billboard lease survived appellate review on November 12, 2026, when the Oregon Court of Appeals affirmed a $1,237,191.92 judgment against Lamar Obie Company, LLC in *Atwood v. Lamar Obie Co., LLC*. The case turned not on the words of the lease itself, but on the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing that Oregon law reads into every contract — and on what happens when a tenant's assignee walks away with a regulatory permit the landlord needed to keep using his own wall.

The plaintiffs — Jim A. Atwood, individually and as trustee of the Jim A. Atwood Trust, together with his wholly owned J.A. Atwood Corporation — own a building on West Burnside in Portland with a large exterior wall that has displayed commercial advertising for decades. Rather than selling ad space directly, the Atwood interests leased the wall to advertising brokers, who in turn sold space to advertisers and split the revenue. That arrangement worked smoothly for years, moving from Onsite Advertising Services, LLC in 2009, to OnDisplay Advertising, LLC by assignment in 2010, each under a lease that let the plaintiffs re-let the wall to a new tenant once a given term ended.

The trouble began in 2012, when Oregon designated the relevant stretch of West Burnside a state highway, subjecting the wall sign to Oregon Department of Transportation permitting requirements for roadside commercial advertising, effective June 30, 2015. OnDisplay needed Atwood's written consent to apply for the ODOT permit, and Atwood gave it — but only after extracting a specific condition: the permit letter stated it should issue "in the name of: OnDisplay Advertising, J.A. Atwood, J.A. Atwood Corporation, their successors and assigns." Atwood's purpose, as he later testified, was to make sure he would own the permit once his lease with whoever held it eventually ended.

That protection failed in practice. ODOT issued the permit solely in OnDisplay's name, not the plaintiffs'. Around the same time, OnDisplay sold its lease and assets to Lamar, and ODOT transferred the permit to Lamar alone — again without naming the plaintiffs, and without anyone notifying them that the permit now belonged entirely to their tenant's successor. Atwood did not learn of any of this until years later. When he terminated the lease with Lamar in 2019, Lamar refused to sell or transfer the permit to the plaintiffs. Instead, Lamar surrendered it to ODOT for a relocation credit that Lamar itself could not use — a maneuver that had the practical effect of extinguishing the plaintiffs' ability to display commercial advertising on their own wall at all.

At trial, the jury found that Lamar's conduct breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing and awarded the plaintiffs more than $1.2 million, including an award for future lost revenue. Lamar appealed on nine separate grounds, arguing among other things that the plaintiffs' expectations about the permit were not objectively reasonable as a matter of law, that the future-damages claim was impermissibly speculative, and that the trial court erred in its jury instructions on both liability and damages.

The Court of Appeals, in an opinion by Presiding Judge Darleen Ortega, rejected every argument that was properly before it (two of Lamar's assignments, including the denial of summary judgment, were not even reviewable on appeal given the posture of the case). The panel held that a jury could reasonably find the parties shared an objectively reasonable expectation — rooted in the permit letter's own language and the parties' course of dealing — that whoever ultimately held the lease would not strip the landlord of the regulatory permit needed to keep the wall in commercial use. The court also found the future-damages evidence sufficiently grounded to go to the jury, and found no instructional error.

**Practical takeaways:**

- When a leased or licensed use of commercial property depends on a government permit, landlords should not assume informal consent letters or permit-naming conditions will survive an assignment; the permit itself, not just the lease, needs an enforceable transfer mechanism tied to any sale of the tenant's interest.
- A tenant's assignee inherits the practical power to deal with regulators as it sees fit unless the lease expressly restricts that discretion — which means landlords negotiating broker-style leases for signage, antennas, or similar permitted uses should bargain for contractual transfer rights up front, not rely on the implied covenant of good faith to fill the gap after the fact.
- Oregon's implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing can support a substantial verdict, including future lost revenue, where a party's conduct frustrates the objectively reasonable expectations embedded in the deal's own history — even without an explicit contract clause addressing the specific conduct at issue.
- Buyers of a leasehold interest in commercial real estate should audit any permits or governmental approvals tied to the premises before closing, since a permit issued or transferred in the buyer's name alone can create liability exposure to the landlord even if the buyer did nothing to cause the original naming error.
