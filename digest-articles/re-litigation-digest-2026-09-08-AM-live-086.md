# Supreme Court to Decide Whether Pipeline Condemnors Must Pay Landowners' Legal Bills

### Cert granted in a North Dakota eminent domain fight testing how far the Natural Gas Act preempts state fee-shifting statutes — with implications for every property owner facing federal condemnation

The U.S. Supreme Court agreed on June 29, 2026, to resolve a question that has quietly divided lower courts for years and that matters well beyond the pipeline industry: when a private company exercises the federal government's eminent domain power to condemn land, who pays the landowner's attorney's fees? In Hoffmann v. WBI Energy Transmission, Inc., No. 25-159, the Court will decide whether Section 7 of the Natural Gas Act displaces state fee-shifting statutes, like North Dakota's, that would otherwise let a successful condemnee recover the legal costs of fighting over just compensation. For any property owner, developer, or institutional landholder whose real estate sits along a proposed pipeline, transmission line, or other federally licensed infrastructure corridor, the outcome will help define the true cost of resisting a taking.

The case traces back to 2018, when WBI Energy Transmission, Inc. exercised federal eminent domain authority under NGA Section 7(h) to acquire pipeline easements across property owned by McKenzie County, North Dakota landowners Leonard and Denae Hoffmann, along with Randall Stevenson and Rocky Prestangen. The Natural Gas Act allows FERC-certificated pipeline companies to condemn private property for interstate gas transportation projects, but Section 7(h) itself does not directly address who bears the cost of litigating the condemnation, leaving that gap to be filled by state law under the federal condemnation procedure rules that ordinarily apply. North Dakota is among the states whose statutes entitle a landowner who successfully contests an eminent domain valuation to recover reasonable attorney's fees from the condemnor.

The Hoffmanns and their co-petitioners reached a confidential settlement with WBI over the underlying just-compensation figure in 2021, but the parties kept litigating over the landowners' legal fees. A federal district court sided with the landowners, ordering WBI to pay roughly $380,000 in attorney's fees and related costs under North Dakota's fee-shifting statute. WBI appealed, and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit reversed in the pipeline company's favor, holding that federal law displaces North Dakota's fee statute once a condemnation proceeds under the Natural Gas Act's federal eminent domain authority. That left the landowners on the hook for their own legal expenses despite having successfully pressed their compensation claim, and set up the circuit-level conflict the Supreme Court has now agreed to resolve.

The question presented turns on a distinctly technical but consequential point of federal preemption doctrine: whether Congress, in authorizing FERC-certificated pipelines to invoke federal eminent domain power under NGA Section 7, meant to displace the state-law rules that would otherwise govern ancillary aspects of a condemnation proceeding, including fee-shifting, or whether those state-law rules continue to apply by default because Section 7 itself is silent on the subject. Petitioners argue that federal condemnation procedure has long incorporated state law on exactly these kinds of questions absent a clear congressional directive to the contrary, and that reading silence as preemption effectively strips landowners of protections state legislatures deliberately built into their eminent domain regimes. WBI Energy, for its part, contends that a uniform federal rule is necessary to avoid a patchwork of fee obligations that would vary pipeline-by-pipeline depending on which state's easement happens to be at issue, and that Congress's choice to create a federal cause of action for interstate pipeline condemnation carries with it an implicit preference for federal, not state, remedial rules.

The case is expected to be argued during the Court's October 2026 Term, with a date to be set once briefing is complete. Because the question presented is framed narrowly around the Natural Gas Act, a ruling will speak directly to landowners facing pipeline condemnations, but the reasoning could reverberate further. Federal eminent domain authority is not unique to natural gas pipelines; it also underlies certain transmission-line, telecommunications, and other federally licensed infrastructure condemnations, and a broad preemption holding here could invite condemnors in those other contexts to argue that state fee-shifting and cost-recovery statutes no longer apply to them either.

For commercial and agricultural landowners, developers with land banked along known utility corridors, and the counsel who represent them in condemnation proceedings, the case is worth tracking closely regardless of which side ultimately prevails. A ruling for WBI Energy would mean that federally licensed pipeline condemnors need not reimburse a landowner's legal fees even when the landowner successfully increases the compensation award, which meaningfully raises the practical cost of contesting a lowball valuation and could push more owners to settle quickly rather than litigate. A ruling for the Hoffmanns would preserve state fee-shifting protections in this context and, in states with landowner-friendly statutes, keep real pressure on condemnors to make credible initial offers. Either way, property owners anywhere along a proposed interstate pipeline route should factor the current fee-shifting uncertainty into their negotiating posture now, document their litigation costs carefully if a dispute proceeds, and watch for the Court's decision, expected sometime before the end of the October 2026 Term, since it will likely settle how this fee question is litigated nationwide for years to come.
