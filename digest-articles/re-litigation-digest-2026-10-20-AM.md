# California Appeals Court Affirms Landlord's Arbitration Win in Westwood Restaurant Lease Fight Over a Grease Interceptor

### A commercial tenant's attempt to unwind a losing arbitration award over a stalled plumbing fixture falls flat on appeal, underscoring how far courts will defer to arbitrators in lease disputes

A California appeals court has affirmed a trial court judgment confirming an arbitration award in favor of a Los Angeles landlord in a years-long commercial lease dispute that began with a disagreement over a 3,000-gallon grease interceptor and ended in a full-blown fraud trial, a jury mistrial, and binding arbitration. The case, *The Waffle, LLC v. Tucker Investment Group, LLC*, decided October 19, 2026 by the California Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, Division Seven, is a reminder to commercial tenants that once they agree to arbitrate, California courts will scrutinize an adverse award only narrowly — and that a lease's own cure provisions can determine the outcome of a breach claim long before a dispute ever reaches a judge or arbitrator.

The dispute traces back to a March 30, 2017 lease between Tucker Investment Group, LLC (TIG), the master lessee of a commercial property in Westwood, and The Waffle, LLC, which planned to open a second restaurant location there. Under a construction work letter attached to the lease, TIG agreed to install a four-inch grease waste line connected to a 3,000-gallon grease interceptor. The parties never settled on a fixed deadline for that work, instead relying on a cure provision requiring TIG to act within a "reasonable time" after written notice of any default — no less than 30 days, or, if the obligation could not reasonably be completed within 30 days, commencement within that window followed by diligent pursuit to completion.

That ambiguity became the fuse. From late 2017 into early 2018, TIG's project manager and The Waffle's principal, restaurant owner Gavin Polone, went back and forth over whether a smaller "Schier" grease trap could substitute for the bulkier interceptor, which required costly excavation behind the premises. When The Waffle's side rejected the substitute as final in April 2018, it sent TIG a formal notice of breach. TIG responded that it was returning to engineers to plan installation of the original 3,000-gallon unit. Unsatisfied, The Waffle sued on May 2, 2018, alleging fraud and negligent misrepresentation against TIG and its project manager's firm, Coastline Construction Management, Inc., and seeking rescission and breach-of-contract relief against TIG.

The litigation proceeded in two phases. In a 2021 bench trial on the breach-of-contract claim, the trial court sided with TIG, reasoning that under the lease's cure language, a landlord is not in breach merely for missing an ambitious timeline — only for failing to commence a non-30-day obligation within 30 days and thereafter diligently pursue it. Because TIG's principal, William Tucker, had engaged engineers and communicated with his team about moving forward with the original interceptor, the court found no breach. A subsequent 2022 jury trial on the fraud and misrepresentation claims produced a verdict for Coastline on one claim but deadlocked on the remainder, resulting in a mistrial. Rather than retry those claims, the parties stipulated to submit everything — including any fee motions — to binding arbitration under the rules of ADR Services, Inc., expressly waiving any right to appeal except on narrow statutory grounds.

The arbitrator ultimately ruled for the Tucker defendants, and the trial court granted their motion to confirm the award while denying The Waffle's motion to vacate it. On appeal, The Waffle argued the arbitrator exceeded his authority by relying on findings from the earlier bench trial, failed to issue a "reasoned award" addressing its central argument, and erred in awarding fees for work performed on behalf of defendants who had already settled out. The Court of Appeal rejected each argument and affirmed across the board.

For commercial landlords and tenants alike, the case illustrates two durable lessons about lease drafting and dispute strategy. First, a lease's cure-period language can do more work than any later factual dispute: here, the operative question was never really whether TIG delayed, but whether the lease's own 30-day commence-and-diligently-pursue standard was satisfied, and that contractual framework effectively decided the breach claim at the trial-court level well before arbitration ever began. Second, once parties stipulate to binding arbitration with a waiver of appellate rights, California courts will not relitigate the merits on review; an arbitrator's reliance on prior court findings from the same litigation, a terse award, and fee awards touching settled co-defendants are all well within the narrow discretion appellate courts afford arbitrators under the state's arbitration statute.

Commercial parties negotiating construction work letters or tenant-improvement obligations should take away three practical points: build a specific, unambiguous deadline into any obligation tied to a major capital item like a grease interceptor or similar utility infrastructure, rather than relying on open-ended "reasonable time" cure language that can be litigated for years; recognize that agreeing to binding arbitration — especially with an appellate-rights waiver — effectively forecloses a second bite at the apple on the merits, so that decision should be made with full awareness of the stakes; and understand that a prevailing party's fee award in arbitration can extend to work connected to co-defendants who settled earlier in the case, so fee exposure should be evaluated across the full scope of the litigation, not just against the arbitrating parties.
