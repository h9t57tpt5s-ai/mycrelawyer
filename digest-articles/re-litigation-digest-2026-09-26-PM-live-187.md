# From $97.3 Million Default to Rebrand: Inside the Foreclosure That Remade Salt Lake City's Industry SLC

### A San Francisco lender's enforcement of a defaulted construction loan against a celebrated Granary District office campus has concluded with a full repositioning — a real, finished example of how commercial foreclosure increasingly doubles as an asset-recovery strategy, not just a liquidation.

Foundry43, the rebranded successor to Salt Lake City's Industry SLC office campus, relaunched this month under new ownership after lender ACORE Capital completed foreclosure on a defaulted $97.3 million loan and, together with Lincoln Property Company, repositioned the 230,000-square-foot Granary District property under a new name and expanded program. The relaunch, reported this week by Building Salt Lake, Hoodline, and Connect CRE, closes out a saga that began with the collapse of the project's original developer in 2023, and gives commercial lenders and adaptive-reuse sponsors a concluded, fully documented example of how a construction-loan default on a marquee project actually gets resolved from complaint to repositioned asset.

Industry SLC opened in 2020 inside a converted 1943 foundry at 513 and 517 W. 600 South, and was widely credited as a catalyst for the surrounding Granary District's redevelopment, leasing up quickly as a flagship adaptive-reuse project. The campus's fortunes turned when its construction arm, Makers Line — an affiliate of developer Q Factor — collapsed in 2023 amid at least fifteen lawsuits from unpaid subcontractors seeking roughly $2.8 million combined, a wave of mechanic's-lien and breach-of-contract litigation that is common when a general contractor's own capital structure fails mid-project. That construction-side distress proved to be an early signal of deeper financial trouble at the ownership level.

The ownership entity, referred to in litigation and reporting as Catalyst, had financed the campus with a $97.3 million loan from ACORE Capital, a San Francisco-based commercial real estate lender. When Catalyst defaulted, ACORE Capital Mortgage filed suit in Utah's Business and Chancery Court in November 2025, alleging that principal, accrued interest, and fees had grown the amount owed to roughly $103.7 million. The Business and Chancery Court is a relatively young, specialized division of Utah's Third Judicial District created specifically to handle complex commercial disputes with a judge experienced in business litigation — a venue choice that reflects how large, multi-tranche commercial loan defaults increasingly get routed to specialized commercial dockets rather than general civil calendars, mirroring similar business courts that have emerged in Delaware, New York, and elsewhere.

A foreclosure sale was scheduled for early March 2026, but rather than proceeding to a contested public auction, ACORE Capital took title by special warranty deed on March 9, 2026, with the property conveyed to Marwood Investment Trust. That resolution — a negotiated deed transfer rather than a fought-over courthouse-steps sale — is itself an instructive data point: lenders holding underwater construction loans on distinctive, hard-to-value adaptive-reuse assets frequently prefer a controlled transfer of title over an auction process that risks an unpredictable clearing price and a protracted receivership fight. It let ACORE take control of the asset on a known timeline and immediately begin planning its repositioning, rather than remaining a mortgagee waiting on a sale outcome it could not fully control.

That repositioning is now public. ACORE partnered with Lincoln Property Company to stabilize and relaunch the campus, bringing on JLL to lead leasing under the new Foundry43 name. The plans go beyond simply re-tenanting the existing 230,000 square feet: the ownership group intends to add 64,000 square feet of additional creative office space, a 34,000-square-foot fitness center, 12,000 square feet of new retail, and a 1,000-stall parking garage — a program that treats the foreclosed asset as a platform for further capital investment rather than a distressed holding to be sold off quickly. For a life-cycle case study of a construction-loan default, that is the significant twist: the lender's post-foreclosure playbook here looks less like liquidation and more like the sponsor role Catalyst originally occupied, executed by an institutional owner with the balance sheet to fund the buildout Catalyst apparently could not sustain.

For commercial lenders, borrowers, and adaptive-reuse sponsors nationally, the Foundry43 story is a complete arc worth studying end to end: a promising conversion project, a general contractor's mid-project collapse, an ownership-level loan default, a specialized commercial court's role in adjudicating the debt, a negotiated deed transfer in lieu of a contested auction, and a lender-led repositioning funded well beyond the original capital stack. Each stage carries its own lessons for parties managing similar exposure elsewhere.

## Practical takeaways

The case is a reminder that a construction lender's remedies do not end at loan enforcement; increasingly, sophisticated CRE lenders are prepared to take title and fund a repositioning themselves when they conclude that is a better recovery path than a forced sale, and both borrowers and junior creditors negotiating a workout should assume the senior lender has that option available. It also underscores that a general contractor's insolvency and an ownership entity's loan default are legally separate events but are often causally linked, so lenders underwriting adaptive-reuse and ground-up construction should track the financial health of the construction arm as closely as the borrowing entity itself. Utah's Business and Chancery Court, along with similar specialized commercial dockets nationally, is becoming a more common venue for large CRE loan-enforcement litigation, and out-of-state lenders and their counsel should factor that venue's procedures and timelines into their default-enforcement planning. Finally, a negotiated deed transfer rather than a contested foreclosure auction remains a live option even on large, high-profile defaults, and can materially compress the time between default and a lender's ability to control and reposition the asset.
