# $1.5 Billion Water Rights Trial Could Redraw the Line Between Nevada's Regulatory Power and Developers' Vested Property Interests

### A Clark County bench trial pitting a stalled master-planned community against the state's water regulator is testing how far Nevada can go in curtailing groundwater rights without paying for what it takes

A trial now underway in the Eighth Judicial District Court in Las Vegas may do more than decide the fate of a long-dormant desert development — it could reset the boundaries of Nevada's authority to regulate groundwater across the state, with direct consequences for any developer, lender, or investor whose real estate underwriting depends on water availability. Coyote Springs Investment, LLC, the entity controlled by California's Seeno family, is seeking more than $1.5 billion, and potentially upward of $2 billion once damages are calculated, from the State of Nevada over the Nevada State Engineer's curtailment of groundwater pumping rights that the developer says destroyed the value of a planned 43,000-home city roughly 50 miles north of Las Vegas.

The case, which originated in 2020 and was later remanded from federal court to Clark County District Court, went to trial on July 8, 2026, before Judge Mark R. Denton, one of five judges assigned to the court's specialized Business Court docket. The trial's first phase is a bench trial — judge only, no jury — expected to run for months given the volume of expert hydrological and valuation testimony involved. If Judge Denton finds that the state's actions amounted to an unlawful taking or breach of the developer's water rights, a second phase before a jury would then determine the dollar amount of damages, a sum plaintiffs' counsel and independent analysts alike have suggested could run into the billions and fall on Nevada taxpayers.

## What Happened

Coyote Springs was conceived in the early 2000s as a self-contained master-planned community spanning roughly 43,000 acres of desert land straddling Clark and Lincoln counties, with plans for tens of thousands of homes, golf courses, and commercial development anchored by rights to pump groundwater from the Coyote Springs Valley and neighboring hydrographic basins. The project's economics depended entirely on the developer's ability to certificate and use those water rights at a scale sufficient to serve a city-sized population.

Over the following two decades, the Nevada State Engineer — the state official charged with administering water law — moved to limit and ultimately curtail pumping in the basin, citing evidence that groundwater withdrawals at the levels contemplated by Coyote Springs and other users would draw down the aquifer beyond sustainable yield and threaten senior water rights, including those tied to environmental flows protecting the endangered Moapa dace. Coyote Springs Investment contends the State Engineer's actions went well beyond routine water-law administration and instead amounted to a regulatory taking: by cutting off the water supply the project's entire value depended on, the state effectively destroyed hundreds of millions of dollars the developer had invested in infrastructure, entitlements, and land acquisition, without paying just compensation.

Nevada's defense rests on the State Engineer's core statutory mandate to manage a scarce and over-appropriated resource for the long-term benefit of all water users in the basin, arguing that curtailment was a lawful exercise of the state's police power over water administration rather than a compensable taking of property.

## Why It Matters for Commercial Real Estate

Water availability is normally treated as a due-diligence checkbox in Southwestern land deals rather than a live litigation exposure, but this case argues otherwise. A ruling for the developer would validate a legal theory that a state water regulator's exercise of its ordinary curtailment authority can itself trigger takings liability when it forecloses a project's economic use — a precedent that would reverberate well beyond Nevada in every arid Western jurisdiction managing over-appropriated basins amid worsening drought and rising demand from residential growth, agriculture, and increasingly, water-intensive data centers. Conversely, a ruling for the state would reaffirm that developers bear the risk of regulatory tightening on water rights much as they do zoning changes, reinforcing that even substantial up-front investment does not insulate a project's underlying entitlements from a regulator's evolving assessment of resource sustainability.

Lenders financing land-banking and master-planned community deals contingent on water certificates, title insurers pricing water-rights exceptions, and REITs or private equity sponsors underwriting large-scale Western land plays should all be watching how Judge Denton frames the line between permissible regulation and a compensable taking, since that framework — not just the ultimate damages figure — is what will govern the next dispute.

## Practical Takeaways

- Developers and lenders underwriting land deals in water-scarce Western states should treat water-rights curtailment risk as a live legal exposure, not a static entitlement, and stress-test project valuations against the possibility of future regulatory tightening.
- A ruling establishing that lawful water-curtailment action can constitute a compensable taking would materially raise the litigation and settlement leverage available to developers facing similar regulatory setbacks in over-appropriated basins nationwide.
- Because the case's first phase is a bench trial with no fixed end date, parties to comparable water-dependent transactions should monitor its outcome before finalizing long-term underwriting assumptions tied to Nevada groundwater availability.
- Title insurers and diligence counsel on Southwestern land transactions should revisit how water-rights curtailment risk is disclosed and allocated in purchase agreements and loan documents in light of the theories being tested here.
