# A Dismissed Foreclosure Doesn't Erase a Mortgage: Ohio Appeals Court Revives Lender's Claim to Condemnation Proceeds

### Wiltshire Capital Partners wins a reversal after a trial court held its mortgage interest was wiped out by a foreclosure case it never managed to serve

A commercial mortgage lender whose foreclosure suit was dismissed for failure to prosecute has not thereby lost its mortgage, the Tenth District Court of Appeals of Ohio held on November 5, 2026, reversing a Franklin County trial court that had barred the lender from any share of a eminent domain payment on the same property. The decision in *Columbus v. Ohio Wholesale Auto Sales, L.L.C.*, 2026-Ohio-4386, is a reminder to commercial real estate lenders and municipal condemnation practitioners alike that a procedural dismissal is not the same thing as losing on the merits — and that the distinction can determine who gets paid when the government takes a mortgaged property.

The case began in August 2021, when the City of Columbus filed a petition to appropriate, by eminent domain, a small fee interest and construction easements across a used-car lot owned by Ohio Wholesale Auto Sales, LLC, as part of the Hudson Street Sidewalks Project. Eminent domain law requires a condemning authority to join everyone with a recorded interest in the property, so Columbus named not only the fee owner but every lienholder of record, including mortgagee Wiltshire Capital Partners, LP. By 2022, after various dismissals and default judgments against the other named parties, only three respondents remained: Wiltshire, Ohio Wholesale Auto Sales, and the Franklin County Treasurer.

The twist was that Wiltshire's mortgage interest had already been the subject of litigation. In August 2017, Wiltshire had filed a separate foreclosure action on the same mortgage, but that case was dismissed in 2018 under Ohio Civil Rule 41(B)(1) for failure to prosecute — a dismissal the record suggests followed from Wiltshire never obtaining service of process on the defendants. When the condemnation case reached the question of how to divide the appropriation proceeds, the trial court seized on that dismissal: because Wiltshire's foreclosure claim had been dismissed under Rule 41(B)(1), the court reasoned, res judicata barred Wiltshire from asserting any mortgage interest in the condemnation case at all, and Wiltshire therefore lacked "standing" to participate. On that basis, the court approved a settlement between the city and Ohio Wholesale Auto Sales — vesting title in Columbus upon a payment of just $4,014 — over Wiltshire's objection, and barred Wiltshire from presenting any claim to a share of that payment.

Wiltshire's first trip to the Tenth District, in 2024, ended without a merits ruling: the appellate court held the trial court's order wasn't yet final and appealable, though it flagged in a footnote that treating the issue as one of "standing" was analytically off, since Columbus itself had named Wiltshire as a respondent in the appropriation action. That observation proved decisive once the case came back up on its second appeal. Writing for the panel, Judge Dingus held that a dismissal for failure to prosecute under Civil Rule 41(B)(1) is not an adjudication on the merits of the underlying claim, and therefore cannot support res judicata preclusion of a later, different claim to the same mortgage interest. Because the foreclosure dismissal never decided whether Wiltshire's mortgage was valid, the condemnation court was wrong to treat that dismissal as conclusively extinguishing the mortgage — and wrong, in turn, to treat Wiltshire as lacking any claim to participate in dividing the appropriation award. The court reversed and remanded for further proceedings.

The dollar amount at stake in this particular case is modest — the underlying condemnation payment was just over four thousand dollars — but the legal principle reaches well beyond it. Commercial mortgages are frequently subject to multiple rounds of enforcement litigation over the life of a troubled loan: a lender may file, pause, refile, or lose track of service in a foreclosure action for reasons that have nothing to do with the strength of its lien. If a later court — in a condemnation proceeding, a second foreclosure, a bankruptcy claims dispute, or otherwise — were free to treat any dismissed foreclosure as conclusive proof the mortgage no longer exists, lenders would face a trap that has nothing to do with the merits of their security interest. The Tenth District's ruling forecloses that shortcut, at least within Ohio's Tenth Appellate District, by insisting that preclusion doctrines track what was actually decided, not merely what was dismissed.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A dismissal for failure to prosecute (the Ohio Rule 41(B)(1) analogue, or similar rules in other states) does not resolve the merits of the dismissed claim, so it should not be treated as extinguishing a mortgage or other property interest in later litigation absent a court actually deciding that question.
- Lenders named as condemnation respondents should not assume a prior, unrelated dismissal — especially one entered for a procedural failure such as lack of service — forfeits their right to share in an appropriation award; that question deserves its own briefing rather than summary resolution at a "status conference."
- Municipalities and private condemning authorities should expect that every record lienholder they name as a respondent retains a live claim to appropriation proceeds unless and until a court actually adjudicates that lienholder's interest, and should budget litigation time accordingly rather than assuming a tangled title history resolves itself.
- The ruling is a useful citation for CRE lenders anywhere a court conflates "the foreclosure case went away" with "the mortgage went away" — the two are legally distinct, and only a decision on the latter question can support res judicata.
