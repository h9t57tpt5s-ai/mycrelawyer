# Old Second National Bank Sues to Foreclose River North Building, Targets Investor Personally Over $2.9M Guaranty

### A $9 million foreclosure suit over a River North building approved for a $3 million restaurant buildout shows how lenders are pairing property foreclosure with personal guaranty claims against the investors behind defaulted commercial loans.

Old Second National Bank filed a foreclosure lawsuit in the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, against Great Street LLC over a commercial building at 400 N. State Street in Chicago's River North neighborhood, according to an October 29, 2026 report from The Real Deal that was the first to disclose the filing, with Crain's Chicago Business and Hoodline following with additional coverage. The bank's complaint, filed October 26, 2026, says Great Street LLC owed roughly $9 million on the underlying loan as of October 21, 2026, with interest continuing to accrue. Beyond seeking foreclosure and a court-ordered sale of the property, the bank is separately pursuing $2.9 million from the investor behind the entity personally under a loan guaranty, and has also named MAB Capital Management and WFB Gift as defendants.

Great Street LLC is an entity registered to a Chicago address tied to a hospitality investment platform. The building itself has an active redevelopment angle: city permits have already been approved for a roughly $3 million conversion of the property into a restaurant, meaning the foreclosure complaint lands in the middle of what was intended to be a value-add repositioning rather than a simple vacant or distressed holding. Reporting indicates the guarantor is contesting the action and intends to fight to keep the building, though the specifics of the defense were not detailed in available coverage.

The dual-track structure of the complaint — a foreclosure claim against the real property paired with a separate breach-of-guaranty claim against the individual investor — reflects a now-standard lender strategy in commercial mortgage defaults. Foreclosing on the collateral alone often leaves a lender exposed to a shortfall if the property's value, or its eventual sale price, does not cover the full loan balance plus accrued interest, fees, and costs. A personal guaranty, where one exists, gives the lender a second source of recovery independent of the property's ultimate sale price, and pursuing both remedies simultaneously — rather than waiting to see how the foreclosure plays out — preserves the lender's position and puts pressure on the guarantor to negotiate a resolution before the litigation advances further.

For River North specifically, the filing adds to a string of recent commercial foreclosure actions in the neighborhood, as lenders work through a broader wave of maturing and underperforming loans on smaller commercial buildings in Chicago's downtown-adjacent submarkets. Unlike the large, institutionally owned towers that dominate headlines about Chicago office distress, buildings like 400 N. State Street tend to be owned by smaller private investment vehicles and individual sponsors, who are more likely to have signed personal guaranties when the original loan was underwritten — and are therefore more exposed when a lender decides to enforce the full range of its remedies rather than negotiate a modification.

The approved restaurant redevelopment plan adds a wrinkle: a foreclosure can disrupt or delay a permitted redevelopment project regardless of how close it was to breaking ground, since a receiver or foreclosing lender typically has little incentive to advance a borrower's redevelopment plans during active litigation, and a subsequent buyer at a foreclosure sale may or may not choose to pursue the same permitted use. Tenants, contractors, or prospective operators with a stake in a building's planned redevelopment should treat an active foreclosure filing as a signal to reassess timelines, since litigation can stall permitted projects for months or years depending on how aggressively the parties litigate and whether a receiver is appointed to manage the property in the interim.

Practically, the case is also a reminder of how quickly a lender can move from informal workout discussions to a formal two-front litigation strategy once it concludes a borrower cannot or will not cure a default. Property owners and sponsors facing similar pressure should review their guaranty obligations carefully before a loan matures or goes into default, since a personal guaranty materially changes the calculus of how a default plays out — an owner can no longer treat a struggling property as a contained, non-recourse problem once a guaranty is in the mix, and should budget for the possibility that a lender will pursue the guarantor's personal assets in parallel with foreclosing on the property itself. Investors structuring commercial acquisitions through single-purpose entities should also recognize that a guaranty, by design, pierces that liability shield, and should negotiate guaranty caps, burn-off provisions, or carve-outs limiting personal exposure at the time the loan is originated rather than after a default has already occurred.
