# Kentucky Appeals Court Rejects Landowner's Floodplain Takings Claim — For Skipping a Required Step

### A Louisville business partnership's eight-year fight over an undevelopable Ohio River tract ends not on the merits, but on ripeness: it never asked the floodplain board for a variance.

Commercial landowners who believe a zoning or floodplain regulation has wiped out the value of their property often rush to the courthouse. A decision issued October 30, 2026 by the Kentucky Court of Appeals is a reminder that doing so too early can be fatal to the claim, whatever its merits might otherwise have been.

In *Lampton Securities Partnership, LLP v. Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government*, No. 2025-CA-1380-MR, the court affirmed summary judgment against a landowner that sued the city and its sewer district for inverse condemnation after floodplain ordinances allegedly destroyed the value of a roughly two-acre tract along the Ohio River. The panel — Judges Combs, Easton, and Lisa Payne Jones, who authored the opinion — held that the claim was not ripe because the landowner never sought the variance the local floodplain board was authorized to grant.

### How the dispute arose

The property's history stretches back more than three decades. In the early 1990s, the prior owners of a roughly four-acre tract secured approval from the Louisville and Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District to build condominiums in two phases. Phase One was completed on about two acres. The remaining two acres — the "Tract" at the center of this case — were earmarked for Phase Two, but nothing beyond an electrical equipment building and some utility lines was ever built there.

In 1997, following catastrophic regional flooding, the Louisville-Jefferson County Metro Government enacted floodplain ordinances barring most new construction within a designated "conveyance zone." The Tract sits within that zone. The ordinances carved out two exceptions: construction that had already begun before the rules took effect could continue, and an affected landowner could apply to the Metro Sewer District's Floodplain Board for a variance from the restrictions. The record showed the Board had granted variances to other landowners in the area.

Lampton Securities Partnership bought the Tract in 1998 for $800,000, after the floodplain ordinances were already on the books. The property sat idle until around 2019, when Lampton listed it for sale, marketing it as commercially developable. Metro Sewer District staff learned of the listing, and at a meeting with Lampton's representatives, the district's counsel advised that the floodplain ordinances foreclosed development of the site.

Rather than apply for a variance or attempt to resume the never-finished Phase Two construction, Lampton filed a reverse condemnation action in Jefferson Circuit Court in May 2020, arguing that the floodplain ordinances had deprived it of "all economically beneficial uses" of the property. The trial court granted summary judgment to the Metro Government and Metro Sewer District in July 2025 on two grounds: Lampton had failed to exhaust its administrative remedies, and its suit was untimely.

### The Court of Appeals' ripeness analysis

The Court of Appeals affirmed, but on a threshold ground the trial court had not needed to reach: ripeness. Drawing on a line of U.S. Supreme Court regulatory-takings cases — *Palazzolo v. Rhode Island*, *MacDonald, Sommer & Frates v. Yolo County*, *Suitum v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency*, and *Pakdel v. City and County of San Francisco* — the panel explained that a court cannot determine whether a regulation has gone "too far," and therefore effected a taking, until the government's land-use authorities have rendered a final decision on how the regulation applies to the specific parcel. Where "avenues still remain for the government to clarify or change its decision," the claim is not yet ripe for judicial review.

The court found Kentucky law applies the same principle, citing its own 2002 decision in *Spanish Cove Sanitation, Inc. v. Louisville-Jefferson County Metropolitan Sewer District*, which held that a reverse condemnation suit filed before any actual taking had occurred could not proceed. Because Lampton never applied to the Floodplain Board for the variance the ordinance expressly made available — and the Board had a demonstrated practice of granting such relief to other landowners — the court could not know how far the regulation actually restricted use of the Tract. "At the time this action was filed, nothing had even arguably been taken," the court held, affirming dismissal of Lampton's claim.

### Practical takeaways

For commercial owners and developers who believe a floodplain, zoning, or other land-use regulation has eliminated the economic value of a parcel, this decision underscores that a regulatory-takings or inverse-condemnation claim generally cannot be filed until the available administrative relief — a variance application, a special-use permit, or an appeal to the relevant board — has been pursued to a final decision. Filing suit first and skipping that step risks dismissal on ripeness grounds without the court ever reaching the substance of the takings claim, no matter how strong the underlying argument might be. Owners holding land that has sat undeveloped for years under a post-acquisition regulatory restriction should document every step taken (or not taken) to seek relief from the regulating authority, since the timing and content of that administrative record can determine whether a later takings claim is even heard. And because ripeness here hinged on the fact that other landowners had successfully obtained variances from the same board, a well-documented pattern of administrative discretion can cut either way — for a landowner who exhausts the process, or against one who short-circuits it.
