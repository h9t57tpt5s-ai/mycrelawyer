# Hawaii Appeals Court Hands Developers a Cautionary Tale on Suing Over Zoning Complaints

### RCA Trade Center, Inc. v. Hu affirms that a Hawaii landowner's letter opposing a neighboring commercial development — even one that torpedoed a federal loan — is protected speech, and that developers who sue over such letters risk paying the other side's attorney fees.

A Hawaii appeals court has affirmed the dismissal of a real estate developer's lawsuit against neighboring landowners who wrote a letter opposing the developer's agricultural warehouse project, reinforcing the reach of the state's newly adopted anti-SLAPP statute and signaling that developers who respond to zoning or permitting criticism with litigation do so at real financial risk. In RCA Trade Center, Inc. v. Hu, 2026 WL 1802987 (Haw. App. June 23, 2026), the Intermediate Court of Appeals upheld a trial court's dismissal of claims brought by RCA Trade Center, Inc. and an affiliated entity, MP Unit 21, LLC, against Wayne and Tara Hu, along with the trial court's award of attorney fees and costs against the developer.

The underlying dispute centers on a cluster of large agricultural warehouses built within the Marconi Point Condominiums, a roughly 100-acre agricultural condominium project on Oahu's North Shore near Kahuku, developed under the related names Makai Ranch, Marconi Farms, and RCA Trade Center. The Hus, who own a lot within the same agricultural condominium, sent a detailed letter to attorneys representing the project's developer, raising concerns that the warehouses violated agricultural zoning restrictions and permitting requirements and were being put to unauthorized commercial uses. The Hus copied the letter to city, state, and federal agencies. Notably, the letter's circulation to federal regulators coincided with the U.S. Department of Agriculture halting a roughly $7.8 million loan that had backed construction of the warehouses — a consequence RCA Trade Center evidently viewed as serious enough to warrant a lawsuit against the Hus themselves.

RCA Trade Center and MP Unit 21 sued the Hus in October 2025, and the Hus responded by invoking Hawaii's Uniform Public Expression Protection Act, a relatively new anti-SLAPP statute designed to allow defendants to obtain early dismissal of lawsuits that target speech on matters of public concern. Although the Hus' special motion to dismiss was filed after the statute's normal deadline, the trial court accepted it for good cause related to an insurance-coverage issue and proceeded to the merits. On May 21, 2026, Circuit Court Judge John M. Tonaki entered final judgment granting the motion, finding that the Hus' letter constituted protected speech on a matter of public concern — commercial activity's compliance with agricultural zoning restrictions in a community bordering a historic site — and dismissing RCA's complaint in its entirety. The Intermediate Court of Appeals affirmed that judgment on June 23, 2026, agreeing that the letter was protected activity under UPEPA and upholding the fee award against the developer.

For commercial developers, the case is a pointed illustration of how a state anti-SLAPP statute can reshape the calculus around responding to neighbor or community opposition. Zoning and permitting fights routinely generate pointed correspondence from neighboring property owners to regulators, and it is not unusual for a developer, frustrated by the practical consequences of that correspondence — here, the loss of federal financing — to consider a defamation, tortious-interference, or similar claim against the author. UPEPA and the roughly three dozen similar state anti-SLAPP statutes now on the books are specifically designed to make that response costly: a defendant who successfully invokes the statute is typically entitled not just to dismissal but to an award of the attorney fees and costs incurred defending the suit, precisely the outcome RCA Trade Center now faces.

The decision also underscores a broader point about the source of the developer's real exposure here, which was regulatory rather than litigious. It was the Hus' letter to the USDA and other agencies — not any court filing — that triggered the loan suspension that evidently prompted RCA's lawsuit in the first place. Suing the letter's authors did nothing to restore the financing and instead added a second, ultimately unsuccessful, front of litigation and a fee award to the developer's problems.

**Practical takeaways:** Developers on the receiving end of a neighbor's letter to regulators about zoning or permitting compliance should treat litigation against the letter's author as a last resort, not a first response, particularly in any of the roughly three dozen states with an anti-SLAPP or UPEPA-style statute, where an unsuccessful suit can result in an award of the opposing side's attorney fees on top of the underlying loss. Before suing over community opposition, developers and their counsel should assess whether the complained-of speech addresses a genuine matter of public concern — zoning compliance and permitted land use squarely qualify in most jurisdictions — since that characterization, not the truth or falsity of the underlying claims, often decides an anti-SLAPP motion. And because regulatory consequences (like a suspended loan) typically flow from the agency's own independent review rather than from the letter itself, developers facing that kind of setback are usually better served addressing the underlying compliance question directly with the regulator than pursuing the messenger.
