# New York Appeals Court Revives Damages Fight in Chelsea Redevelopment Lien Case

### A supplier's $1.4 million mechanic's-lien judgment against a Chelsea condo developer survives on liability but gets sent back for a damages recount, after the First Department found the supplier's own evidence fell short on part of its claim.

New York's Appellate Division, First Department, on October 15, 2026, largely upheld a mechanic's-lien and breach-of-contract judgment a bathroom-fixtures supplier won against the owner of a Chelsea redevelopment project, but vacated the roughly $1.4 million damages award and sent the case back to the trial court to recalculate how much the owner actually owes. The decision, *Waterworks Operating Co., LLC v. 514 W. 24th Owner LLC*, is a reminder to commercial developers and their contractors alike that a party can prevail on the core liability question and still lose on appeal when its own summary-judgment proof does not fully support the dollar figure a trial court awarded.

**What happened**

Waterworks Operating Company, a manufacturer and supplier of high-end bathroom and cabinet fixtures, sued 514 West 24th Owner LLC and its surety in New York County Supreme Court over unpaid bills for materials delivered to the Owner's redevelopment of a building in Chelsea. Under a series of purchase orders and change orders, the Owner had agreed to pay the full contract price of $2,577,166.99. The parties agreed the Owner had paid $1,608,827.37 for goods actually delivered and accepted, leaving Waterworks to file a mechanic's lien for the remaining $968,339.62 it says it was owed.

The trial court, Justice Joel M. Cohen presiding, granted Waterworks summary judgment on its breach-of-contract and lien-foreclosure claims in June 2024 and entered judgment in December 2024 awarding the company $1,396,058.74 — the outstanding balance plus pre-judgment interest, costs, and disbursements. The Owner appealed both the underlying order and the judgment.

**The two arguments on appeal**

The Owner raised two separate challenges. First, it argued it could not be held liable for $390,605.51 in disputed change orders because the project's original construction manager, Michael Ohman of Sunrise Construction, LLC, lacked authority under the parties' construction management agreement to approve change orders without the Owner's prior written consent. The appellate panel — Justices Webber, Friedman, Kapnick, and Pitt-Burke — rejected that argument outright, holding that the Owner's acceptance of the materials delivered under those change orders, without any objection at the time, amounted to an implied ratification of the agreements regardless of Ohman's actual authority to sign off on them.

The Owner's second argument fared better. It contended there were still triable issues of fact on the total damages owed because Waterworks had not adequately proven it delivered everything the contract called for. On this point, the court drew a careful line. Waterworks had submitted invoices documenting $2,340,098 in materials actually delivered, and the Owner never identified any specific invoiced item it did not receive — so the panel held Waterworks had met its initial burden on that portion of the claim. But Waterworks fell short on the remaining $237,069 in disputed materials: its proof of full delivery came from an accounts-receivable manager's affidavit submitted only with its reply papers in the original summary-judgment motion, after the Owner had already filed its opposition. Because the Owner never had a chance to rebut that late-arriving evidence, the court held the affidavit could not be considered, leaving that slice of the damages claim unsupported.

**The result:** the panel modified the judgment to vacate it and remit the case to Supreme Court solely to recalculate damages consistent with its decision, while otherwise affirming the summary judgment on liability for breach of contract and lien foreclosure. The separate appeal from the underlying order was dismissed as subsumed within the appeal from the judgment.

**Why it matters**

Mechanic's-lien and construction-payment disputes on commercial redevelopment projects routinely turn on exactly this kind of evidentiary sequencing. A party that wins outright on liability can still see its damages number unravel on appeal if any piece of its proof was submitted at the wrong procedural moment, or if it cannot point to specific documentation tying every dollar claimed to an actual delivery or performance. The change-order ruling is equally instructive for owners: silently accepting delivered materials under a change order, even one a project manager arguably had no authority to approve, can be read by a court as ratifying the deal after the fact.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial owners and developers should put written change-order approval limits in actual practice, not just in the construction management agreement, since silent acceptance of delivered materials can ratify an unauthorized change order regardless of what the contract says about signing authority. Suppliers and contractors moving for summary judgment on a payment or lien claim should front-load every piece of proof — invoices, delivery records, and any supporting affidavits — in their original moving papers rather than holding evidence in reserve for a reply, since courts will disregard reply-only proof the other side never had a chance to rebut. And both sides should expect that a split liability-versus-damages result on appeal can send a case back for a second round of proceedings limited only to recalculating the dollar figure, extending the timeline and cost of what looked like a resolved dispute.
