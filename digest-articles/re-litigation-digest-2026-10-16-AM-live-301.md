# Parents of Teen Killed in Mall of Louisiana Shooting Sue Mall Owner, Operator, and Security Contractor

### A wrongful-death and negligence suit against Mall of Louisiana LLC, Brookfield Properties Retail Inc., and Marksman Security LLC tests how far a commercial landlord's security obligations extend when a property has a documented history of violence.

The parents of a 17-year-old killed in an April mass shooting at the Mall of Louisiana in Baton Rouge filed a wrongful-death and negligence lawsuit on October 7, 2026, in the 19th Judicial District Court for East Baton Rouge Parish, naming the mall's owner, Mall of Louisiana LLC; its operator, Brookfield Properties Retail Inc.; and its security contractor, Marksman Security LLC, alongside the accused shooter. The suit is a reminder to commercial landlords, mall operators, and the security firms they hire that a documented pattern of violent incidents at a property can become the central evidence in a premises-liability case once another act of violence follows.

**What happened**

On April 23, 2026, gunfire broke out in the food court at the Mall of Louisiana on Bluebonnet Boulevard, killing 17-year-old Martha Odom and wounding several others. Markel Lee, who was 17 at the time, has since been indicted as an adult on one count of second-degree murder, six counts of attempted second-degree murder, and one count of illegal use of a weapon during a crime of violence; he has pleaded not guilty to the criminal charges, which remain pending separately from the civil suit.

Odom's parents, joined by four other students who were present during the shooting, two of whom were wounded, filed the civil suit roughly five and a half months later. They are represented by the Lafayette firm Broussard, David & Moroux and attorney Christopher L. Trahan. The complaint names Mall of Louisiana LLC, Brookfield Properties Retail Inc., and Marksman Security LLC as defendants alongside Lee.

**The theory of liability**

The complaint does not rest on a single lapse on the day of the shooting. It alleges that the mall and its security provider failed to take reasonable steps to protect patrons from what it characterizes as reasonably foreseeable violence, pointing specifically to the absence of any security personnel stationed in the food court at the time of the shooting and to screening and access-control measures the plaintiffs say did not effectively keep firearms out of the facility. To support the foreseeability element, which is often the central battleground in a premises-liability suit against a commercial property, the complaint cites the property's own history: a 2013 disturbance in the food court serious enough to prompt an evacuation and temporary closure of the mall, and a 2022 shooting near the property that killed two people.

That pattern-of-violence theory is a familiar one in mall and shopping-center shooting litigation nationally, and it places significant weight on how a landlord and its security vendor responded, or failed to respond, to earlier warning signs. Plaintiffs in these cases typically must show not just that a crime occurred, but that the property owner and its security contractor had actual or constructive notice of a heightened risk and nonetheless failed to adjust staffing, screening, or access control in response.

**Why it matters for commercial landlords and security vendors**

Shopping centers, mixed-use retail properties, and other high-traffic commercial venues face a distinct and growing category of premises-liability exposure built around mass-violence incidents, and courts increasingly treat a property's own incident history as the most probative evidence of foreseeability. A landlord that experiences a serious violent incident and does not meaningfully change its security posture afterward, whether by adding officers, improving screening, or revising post orders, risks that the earlier incident becomes Exhibit A in the next lawsuit rather than a closed chapter. The claims here also target the security contractor directly rather than only the property owner, reflecting a broader litigation trend of naming the vendor actually responsible for day-to-day security staffing and protocols as a defendant in its own right, separate from any indemnification or contractual dispute that may follow between the landlord and the vendor.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial landlords and mall operators should treat any serious violent incident at a property as a documented triggering event that, if not followed by a demonstrable change in security staffing or protocols, can later be used to establish foreseeability in litigation over a subsequent incident. Security contracts for high-traffic retail and mixed-use properties should be reviewed for how staffing levels, post assignments, and screening protocols are documented and revised over time, since the adequacy of security "on paper" matters less than what a plaintiff can show was actually staffed and enforced on the date of an incident. And property owners relying on third-party security vendors should examine their indemnification and insurance provisions now, before a mass-violence claim arrives, since suits of this kind name the security vendor as a direct defendant and can quickly raise questions about which party bears primary responsibility for a judgment or settlement.
