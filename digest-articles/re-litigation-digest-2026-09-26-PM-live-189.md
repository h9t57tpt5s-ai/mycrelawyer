# Kansas City Loses Anti-SLAPP Fight After Suing Its Own Residents Over a Data Center Petition

### A Johnson County judge's ruling against Edgerton, Kansas hands data-center opponents nationally a rare precedent: a municipality itself can be the defendant in an anti-SLAPP motion when it sues citizens over a zoning petition.

A Johnson County, Kansas district judge has dismissed a lawsuit the City of Edgerton filed against two of its own residents over a citizen petition seeking to ban "high-impact" data centers, ruling on September 3, 2026 that the city's suit violated the residents' rights under the Kansas Public Speech Protection Act — the state's anti-SLAPP law. The decision, issued by Judge Stephanie Goodenow, is a notable data point for the fast-growing body of litigation surrounding hyperscale data center development: it applies an anti-SLAPP statute, typically invoked by private citizens or media defendants against corporate or individual plaintiffs, against a municipal government itself.

The dispute began when Edgerton residents Kimberly Twente and Carrie Schmidt, working with the nonprofit Public Trust Collective Inc., circulated a citizen petition calling for a local ban on high-impact data center development and gathered 208 signatures — enough, under Kansas law, to force the question toward a public vote. In August 2026, rather than allow the petition to proceed through the normal certification process, the City of Edgerton filed suit against Twente and Schmidt, characterizing the petition as "void and invalid" and asking the court to block the data-center ban from reaching an election. The city's theory was that the petition was administrative rather than legislative in character — a legally significant distinction in Kansas, since citizen initiative and referendum rights generally attach only to legislative acts, not to administrative or ministerial ones.

Judge Goodenow rejected that framing. In granting the defendants' special motion to strike under the Kansas Public Speech Protection Act, she found that the zoning ordinance the petition targeted met the standard of a legislative act, meaning the citizens' initiative rights validly attached and the city's suit was, in substance, an attempt to punish or chill Twente and Schmidt's protected petitioning activity — precisely the harm anti-SLAPP statutes exist to prevent. The court further found the city's evidentiary showing conclusory, writing that the city's proof "to the extent they bring it is conclusory, verified pleading, and therefore insufficient at this stage" to overcome the defendants' motion. Because Kansas's anti-SLAPP statute, like similar laws in other states, shifts attorney's fees to a prevailing defendant, the ruling also means Edgerton must cover the legal expenses Twente and Schmidt incurred defending the suit.

The ruling's significance extends well past Edgerton's town limits. Anti-SLAPP statutes were drafted primarily with an eye toward deterring wealthy private plaintiffs from using litigation to silence individual critics or petitioners; applying the same framework against a city government that sued its own citizens is a comparatively novel maneuver, and one that other data-center-adjacent municipalities weighing similar suits against petition organizers should now take seriously as a live litigation risk. It also reinforces, at the trial-court level, that citizen initiative and referendum rights around zoning and land-use ordinances are not easily defeated by recharacterizing a genuinely legislative ordinance as merely administrative — a strategy municipal defendants facing unwanted ballot measures may otherwise be tempted to try.

The underlying policy fight is not over: the ruling clears the path for the data-center-ban petition to proceed toward a public vote, rather than resolving whether Edgerton will ultimately restrict high-impact data center development. But for developers, landowners, and municipalities involved in the current wave of data center siting disputes — including comparable fights already underway in Joliet, Illinois and Westlake, Texas — the case is an early, concrete signal that courts are willing to protect citizen-led zoning petitions against a municipality's own institutional resistance, and that a city suing its critics carries real financial and legal exposure of its own.

## Practical takeaways

Municipalities considering litigation against citizen petition organizers over a zoning or land-use ballot measure should recognize that anti-SLAPP exposure now runs against government plaintiffs too, not just private ones, at least in Kansas and states with similarly broad anti-SLAPP statutes. Developers relying on a friendly city council to resist a citizen-led zoning backlash should not assume the city can simply block a qualifying petition through litigation; courts are showing a willingness to let the initiative process run its course even over a municipality's strong objection. The "administrative versus legislative" characterization of a challenged ordinance is likely to remain a recurring, outcome-determinative fight in these disputes, and parties on both sides should build a substantial factual record on that question well before litigation, rather than relying on conclusory characterizations. Finally, developers and their counsel should watch the eventual public vote in Edgerton as a real-world test of how citizen sentiment translates into binding zoning restrictions once courts clear procedural obstacles to a vote.
