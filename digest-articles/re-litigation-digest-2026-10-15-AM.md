# New York Appeals Court Rebuffs Property Owner's Bid to Undo a Mechanic's Lien Bond

### The Second Department holds that Lien Law §59's personal-service requirement is not optional, denying a Queens property owner's motion to vacate a lien-discharge bond and dismiss a contractor's foreclosure claim.

A property owner's attempt to escape a mechanic's lien claim on procedural grounds failed on October 14, 2026, when the Appellate Division, Second Department, affirmed a Queens County trial court's refusal to let CG & J Realty, LLC off the hook for a construction lien filed against its commercial property. The decision, *CFS Enterprises, Inc. v. CG & J Realty, LLC*, is a compact but instructive reminder that New York's Lien Law enforces its procedural requirements to the letter — on both lienors and the property owners trying to shake them loose.

The dispute traces back to November 2022, when CFS Enterprises filed a notice of mechanic's lien against commercial property in Queens owned by CG & J Realty, claiming it was owed money for work connected to improvements on the premises. In February 2023, CFS Enterprises sued to foreclose on that lien. CG & J Realty responded the way many commercial owners do when a lien clouds title or threatens a construction loan draw: in April 2023 it posted a bond under Lien Law §19 to discharge the lien from the property records, freeing the title while the underlying payment dispute continued in litigation. Three months later, a separate contractor on the same project, Century Construction Group Corp., posted its own bond under Lien Law §37, a broader instrument meant to discharge all liens and claims against the property. The trial court agreed in October 2023 that the Section 37 bond would supersede the earlier Section 19 bond.

Having been superseded, CG & J Realty tried to extract itself from the dispute entirely. By order to show cause, it moved under Lien Law §59 to vacate and cancel its own Section 19 bond and to dismiss CFS Enterprises' foreclosure claim outright. The Supreme Court, Queens County, denied the motion in October 2024, and CG & J Realty appealed.

The Second Department's affirmance turns on a narrow but absolute procedural point. Lien Law §59 permits a court to vacate a lien or discharge bond, but only after the lienor has been given formal notice: the statute requires that notice be served on the lienor either personally or by leaving it at the lienor's last known residence with someone of suitable age and discretion. CG & J Realty, the court found, never did that. It simply filed its order to show cause on the New York State courts' electronic filing system (NYSCEF) and treated that as sufficient notice to CFS Enterprises. The Appellate Division held that electronic filing alone does not satisfy Lien Law §59's service requirement, citing the long-settled principle that "[s]trict compliance with the statutory requirements is mandated and the court does not have discretion to excuse noncompliance" in mechanic's lien proceedings. Because mechanic's liens exist purely by statute — with no roots in the common law or in equity — courts have consistently refused to relax their procedural conditions even where the underlying dispute might otherwise be resolved on the merits.

The court also rejected CFS Enterprises' request for an award of appellate attorneys' fees under New York court rule 22 NYCRR 130-1.1(c), which allows fee-shifting only for truly frivolous conduct. The panel found CG & J Realty's appeal, while unsuccessful, was not frivolous within the meaning of the rule — a position with at least a colorable argument does not become sanctionable merely because it loses.

For commercial property owners, lenders, and the contractors and suppliers who work on their buildings, the case is a clean illustration of how mechanic's lien practice in New York punishes procedural shortcuts on both sides of the transaction. Lienors must perfect and foreclose their liens by the statutory playbook or lose them; owners seeking to discharge or vacate a lien, or a bond that secures one, face exactly the same rigidity. A party that wants to use Lien Law §59 to unwind a bond cannot treat electronic notice on NYSCEF as a substitute for the statute's specific personal-service mechanism, however routine e-filing has become in ordinary civil practice. The decision also underscores the layered nature of lien security on active commercial construction projects: a Section 19 bond discharges a single lien, while a Section 37 bond can supersede multiple bonds and claims across a project, and disputes about which instrument controls can keep litigation alive long after the underlying lien has nominally been "bonded off."

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial owners and general contractors who intend to use a Lien Law §19 or §37 bond to clear title should build in — and document — strict compliance with every procedural predicate for later relief, including personal service requirements under Lien Law §59, rather than relying on NYSCEF filing as constructive notice. Owners negotiating project financing should expect that a bonded-off lien does not end the underlying foreclosure claim; it only substitutes security for the real property itself, and the litigation over the debt continues. Finally, parties should not assume that losing a procedurally rigorous motion exposes them to fee-shifting sanctions: New York's frivolous-conduct standard under 22 NYCRR 130-1.1(c) remains a high bar, reserved for positions with no colorable legal support at all.
