# Former Owners Accuse Arbor Realty Trust of Engineering Their Own Foreclosure in $175M Georgia Apartment Suit

### A bridge-loan-to-foreclosure sequence at a 474-unit College Park complex draws fraud claims that Arbor strung borrowers along before an affiliate bought the property at its own auction

Brothers Yisroel and Hanoch Cimerring, the former owners of a 474-unit apartment complex in College Park, Georgia, filed suit against Arbor Realty Trust in late March 2026 in New York state court, alleging the publicly traded mortgage REIT fraudulently induced them into a high-interest bridge loan on the promise of a future permanent refinancing, then used the loan's structure to force the property into foreclosure and acquire it through an affiliated entity at a steep discount. The Cimerrings are seeking $175 million in damages, and the case adds to a growing docket of borrower-side litigation against Arbor amid separate federal scrutiny of the lender's broader loan book.

According to the complaint, the Cimerring brothers purchased the College Park complex for approximately $45 million and financed the acquisition with a two-year bridge loan from Arbor. Bridge loans of this kind are typically structured as short-term, higher-interest financing intended to be paid off through a subsequent permanent loan once a property stabilizes; here, the brothers allege Arbor represented that it would help arrange a takeout refinancing through the Fannie Mae multifamily lending program once the property met the agency's underwriting benchmarks. The suit alleges that Arbor instead strung the borrowers along, allowing the punitive interest rate on the bridge loan to keep accruing while never delivering the promised refinancing path, until the loan matured without a permanent replacement in place and the property fell into default.

Once the loan defaulted, Arbor moved to foreclose on the complex. The Cimerrings allege that an Arbor-affiliated entity then purchased the property at the foreclosure auction for approximately $40 million, roughly $5 million less than the brothers' original purchase price years earlier, despite the substantial capital and carrying costs the Cimerrings say they invested in the interim. The allegation that a lender's own affiliate ends up acquiring a distressed borrower's collateral at foreclosure is not new in commercial mortgage litigation, but the Cimerrings' complaint frames the entire bridge-to-refinance arrangement as a fraudulent scheme from the outset, rather than a good-faith financing relationship that later soured, a distinction that matters considerably for what claims can survive a motion to dismiss and what damages theories remain available.

The lawsuit lands amid a broader wave of scrutiny facing Arbor Realty Trust, a real estate investment trust that specializes in bridge and mezzanine lending to multifamily owners nationally. Separate reporting has indicated that the Department of Justice and the FBI's New York office have examined Arbor's lending practices and loan portfolio following short-seller research alleging the company obscured losses tied to certain distressed loan positions through a network of affiliated holding entities. While that regulatory inquiry involves different underlying allegations than the Cimerrings' individual fraud claim, both threads point to the same underlying vulnerability for bridge lenders operating at scale: when a lender's business model depends on originating short-term loans that convert into permanent takeout financing, borrowers and regulators alike will scrutinize closely what happens when that conversion doesn't occur and the lender, or an affiliate, ends up owning the collateral instead.

For sponsors and owners who rely on bridge financing to acquire value-add multifamily assets, the Cimerring litigation is a pointed reminder that a lender's informal assurances about future refinancing are not a substitute for a binding forward commitment, and that the gap between the two can determine who ultimately owns the asset once market conditions tighten. Borrowers entering bridge loan agreements should treat any refinancing "plan" that is not documented as a conditional forward commitment, with defined underwriting benchmarks and timing, as aspirational rather than contractual, and should independently track their own qualification for a permanent takeout loan rather than relying on the bridge lender's representations about progress. Owners facing a maturing bridge loan without a secured takeout should also be alert to the conflict of interest inherent whenever the same lender or its affiliate stands to benefit from a foreclosure sale, and should consider engaging independent counsel and, where feasible, marketing the refinancing or a voluntary sale to third parties before a default forecloses those options. Finally, this case is a reminder that allegations against a single lender's individual transaction can gain outsized significance when they surface alongside broader portfolio-wide scrutiny, since a pattern across multiple borrowers can shift how courts, regulators, and future counterparties evaluate any one dispute in isolation.
