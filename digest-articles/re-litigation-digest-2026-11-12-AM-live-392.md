# Alabama Appeals Court Voids Judgment After Commercial Eviction Case Strayed Into Circuit Court

### A district court's well-meaning transfer of a tangled lease-or-sale dispute wiped out more than a year of litigation, leaving a Montgomery landlord's $53,000 claim back at square one

The Alabama Court of Civil Appeals on November 6, 2026 voided a circuit court judgment in a Montgomery commercial-property dispute, ruling that the case had no business being in circuit court in the first place — a jurisdictional misstep that erased more than a year of litigation and sent the matter all the way back to where it started. The decision in *Devosky Hood v. Barbara L. Smith* is a pointed reminder that Alabama's summary unlawful-detainer remedy is jurisdictionally rigid, and that a trial judge cannot simply hand off a messy case to a different court once it starts looking complicated.

### A lease, or a sale, or both

Barbara L. Smith commenced an unlawful-detainer action against Devosky Hood in the Montgomery District Court on December 12, 2025, alleging that the two had entered into a "commercial lease" in July 2022 covering a parcel of commercial property in Montgomery. According to the filing, Smith notified Hood in August 2025 that he owed roughly $53,500 in unpaid rent and gave him seven days to pay or vacate, as Alabama's unlawful-detainer statute requires.

Hood answered that he was not Smith's tenant at all. He produced what he described as a purchase agreement, also dated July 2022, under which he was buying the same property from Smith, plus a 2024 addendum providing that he would receive a deed once he finished paying Smith $53,000 in $500 monthly installments. In other words, the same underlying arrangement that Smith read as a landlord-tenant lease, Hood read as an unfinished land sale with him already in equitable possession as a purchaser.

That tension came to a head mid-trial. The district court, hearing live testimony, concluded that the dispute had strayed into territory — the validity of a real estate sales contract — beyond a district court's authority, and transferred the unlawful-detainer action to the Montgomery Circuit Court. Once there, Hood filed a counterclaim arguing the matter was a contract dispute over the alleged purchase agreement, not a landlord-tenant case, and asked the circuit court to order Smith to complete the sale or pay damages.

The circuit court held its own trial in April 2026 and, on May 6, 2026, entered judgment finding that Smith held legal title to the property and that Hood had defaulted under the lease — while explicitly declining to rule on whether the alleged purchase agreement was valid, whether Hood had a right to buy the property, or whether he was owed a refund of any payments. Hood appealed.

### A transfer nobody had the power to make

Writing for the Court of Civil Appeals, Judge Christy O. Edwards raised a jurisdictional problem neither party had argued: Alabama law gives district courts exclusive original jurisdiction over unlawful-detainer actions, and a circuit court can only acquire jurisdiction over one after the district court has actually adjudicated it and a party has appealed that adjudication — not through a mid-case transfer ordered by the district court itself. Leaning on its own 2008 precedent in *Darby v. Schley*, the court held that because the Montgomery District Court never ruled on Smith's unlawful-detainer claim before shipping it to circuit court, the circuit court never acquired jurisdiction over the case at all. Its May 2026 judgment — the one that found Hood in default and Smith the rightful titleholder — was therefore void from the start.

The court dismissed Hood's appeal but ordered the circuit court to vacate its void judgment and transfer the unlawful-detainer action back down to the district court where it began. In a footnote with real practical bite, the panel flagged that Smith's $53,000 claim exceeds the district court's $20,000 jurisdictional cap on damages, meaning that on remand she will either have to waive the excess or file a separate circuit court action to recover the balance.

### Practical takeaways

Commercial landlords using Alabama's unlawful-detainer statute to recover possession should treat it as a narrow, summary remedy that a trial court cannot broaden by simply moving the case somewhere more comfortable — any transfer to circuit court has to come through a completed district court adjudication and a timely appeal, not an informal handoff once the facts get complicated. Parties and courts facing a hybrid dispute that blends a lease with an alleged sale contract should consider litigating the two theories in separate, properly filed actions from the outset rather than hoping one court can resolve both at once. And any landlord whose unpaid-rent or damages claim could plausibly exceed a district court's statutory jurisdictional ceiling — $20,000 in Alabama — should weigh filing in circuit court directly, or be prepared to cap the claim, rather than risk a judgment that later proves to have been entered without jurisdiction at all.
