# Illinois Appeals Court Reinstates Chicago's Denial of Southeast Side Scrap Recycling Permit

### The city's rejection of a large metal-recycling facility on Chicago's Southeast Side survives appeal, closing off (for now) RMG's bid to force the permit through after a favorable administrative ruling

In a decision filed November 6, 2026, the Appellate Court of Illinois, First District, affirmed a Cook County circuit court order that reinstated the City of Chicago Department of Public Health's denial of a permit to operate a large metal-recycling facility on the city's Southeast Side, reversing a hearing officer's earlier decision that had cleared the way for the facility to open. The ruling is the latest turn in a years-long fight over the relocation of a scrap-metal operation from Chicago's Lincoln Park neighborhood to a 175-acre industrial site at 116th Street and Burley Avenue, a dispute that has become a flashpoint in Chicago's environmental-justice politics and a cautionary tale for industrial operators relying on administrative appeals to overturn a municipal permit denial.

The applicant, General III, LLC, is an affiliate of Reserve Management Group (RMG), which already owns and operates four recycling facilities at the Burley site. General III is 80% owned by RMG entities, a relationship neither side disputed in the litigation. The facility traces back to a 2018 deal in which General Iron Industries, which had run a metal-scrap business in Lincoln Park for decades, agreed to sell assets to RMG as the city pushed to end recycling operations in that neighborhood. In September 2019, the city, General Iron, and RMG signed a term sheet under which RMG agreed to shut down the Lincoln Park site and transition operations to the Burley site, with the city agreeing to cooperate on permitting while reserving its right to enforce its ordinances to protect public health and safety.

That reservation became the crux of the case. General III obtained a special-use zoning permit from the Chicago Zoning Board of Appeals in 2019, but the Department of Public Health denied its separate "Large Recycling Facility" operating permit in February 2022, a decision that followed sustained community opposition and a city-commissioned health study finding the Southeast Side bears the highest cumulative air-pollution burden of any Chicago neighborhood. General III appealed to the city's Department of Administrative Hearings, and in 2023 an administrative law judge vacated the permit denial, finding General III had met the applicable requirements. The city, under Mayor Brandon Johnson, said it would appeal that ruling, and the circuit court subsequently reversed the administrative law judge and reinstated the Department of Public Health's denial.

On further appeal, the First District held that the administrative law judge had committed legal error in vacating the city's denial, and it affirmed the circuit court's reversal, reinstating the permit denial outright. The court's opinion walks through the regulatory structure in detail: the Department of Public Health's permitting authority under the Chicago Environmental Protection and Control Ordinance, the "compliance history evaluation" required before a large recycling facility permit may issue, and the department's 2020 "Rules for Large Recycling Facilities," which govern operations meeting the volume and equipment thresholds the Burley site facility concededly meets. Because General III's application and compliance history did not satisfy those standards in the department's judgment, and because the hearing officer had misapplied the legal standard in second-guessing that judgment, the appellate panel concluded the circuit court was right to put the department's original denial back in place.

For industrial and commercial property operators generally, the case is a reminder that an administrative hearing officer's ruling in a permit applicant's favor is not the end of the road, and the city's own term-sheet commitment to "cooperate" on a permitting process does not estop it from exercising substantive permitting discretion later if an application does not satisfy applicable public-health or environmental standards. The dispute also illustrates how industrial relocation decisions tied to one neighborhood's environmental concerns can become legally entangled with the receiving community's environmental-justice objections for years afterward, with the courts, not just the permitting agency, ultimately deciding who prevails. Operators planning a similarly contested industrial relocation should expect that a favorable administrative ruling can be reversed on judicial review, that a municipality's compliance-history and public-health permitting criteria carry real teeth even after zoning approval has already been secured, and that multi-year permitting fights of this kind typically generate a long public record (environmental studies, community comment, prior citations) that can be used against an applicant well after the underlying zoning question has been resolved.
