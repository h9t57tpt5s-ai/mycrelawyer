# Receivership Fight Over The Greenbrier Tests How Far a Lender Can Go Against a Flagship Commercial Borrower

### A $289 million debt sale turned a historic West Virginia resort into a federal-court battleground over who gets to run it while a refinancing drags past deadline after deadline.

A legal fight over control of The Greenbrier, the storied luxury resort in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia, has moved from a routine loan dispute into a full-blown test of a secured lender's remedies against a marquee commercial borrower. The case turns on roughly $289 million in debt that Carter Bank & Trust sold earlier this year to White Sulphur Springs Holdings LLC ("WSSH"), an entity backed by TRT Holdings Inc., the parent company of Omni Hotels. After a forbearance agreement with the resort's owner, Greenbrier Hotel Corporation, lapsed, WSSH went to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of West Virginia asking Judge Frank Volk to install a receiver — hospitality executive Michelle Russo was proposed for the role — with authority to take operational control of the property and, if necessary, pursue a sale or bankruptcy filing.

Greenbrier Hotel Corporation did not simply answer the federal complaint. Within days, it filed its own suit against WSSH in Greenbrier County Circuit Court, and its attorneys separately asked Judge Volk to stay the federal receivership case while the state-court action proceeds first. The state complaint, according to West Virginia Watch and other outlets covering the dispute, accuses the bank and the hotel company of conspiring to "snatch" the resort by acquiring the discounted debt rather than negotiating a workout in good faith. West Virginia's state tax department has also moved to intervene in the federal case, seeking to recover roughly $4.4 million in unpaid consumer sales and service taxes tied to the property — a reminder that a receivership dispute over a large hospitality asset tends to pull in creditors well beyond the named lender.

The procedural posture is itself instructive for commercial lenders and borrowers alike. Rather than litigating the merits of default in one forum, Greenbrier Hotel Corporation has tried to control the sequence of litigation by filing first in a court it may view as more favorable, then asking the federal judge to pause his own case in deference to it. Dueling state and federal filings over the same underlying debt are a familiar tactic when a borrower wants to slow a receivership motion long enough to arrange a refinancing, and that is exactly what played out here: as the receivership litigation continued, the Justice family-controlled ownership announced a proposed $500 million refinancing, reportedly from New York-based lender Kennedy Lewis, intended to retire the WSSH debt outright and moot the receivership fight entirely.

That refinancing has not gone smoothly. An initial July 16, 2026 target for closing passed without a deal, prompting Judge Volk to order the borrower to show cause by July 21 why the receivership litigation should not simply resume. Court filings around that deadline indicated the $500 million facility — secured by Greenbrier assets and by equity in the borrowing entity — was close but not yet finalized, with an August 7 outside date floated by the ownership side. The resort's casino license added another layer of exposure: the West Virginia Lottery Commission's ongoing review of the ownership change led the Justice family to warn in mid-August that the casino might have to close and lay off roughly 90 employees if the Commission did not act, before regulators confirmed the casino could keep operating during the review provided incoming board members were walled off from day-to-day control.

For commercial real estate lenders, the case is a live illustration of how quickly a performing forbearance arrangement can escalate into contested receivership litigation once a maturity default is not cured on schedule, and of how much leverage a secured creditor can gain simply by buying distressed debt at a discount rather than originating it. For borrowers, it shows the limits of forum-shopping as a delay tactic: a state-court filing and a motion to stay can buy time, but they do not substitute for an actual closed refinancing, and a court can and will set its own deadlines for showing that a deal is real rather than aspirational. The dispute remains active, with the receivership question still unresolved as the refinancing's closing conditions play out.

**Practical takeaways:** Owners facing a maturity default on a trophy asset should assume that any forbearance extension has a hard deadline a court will enforce, and that a lender who has purchased the debt at a discount may be more willing to pursue receivership than the original originating bank would have been. Lenders and special servicers evaluating a loan sale into a distressed situation should factor in that a borrower with political and financial resources will often fight in multiple forums simultaneously, and should build realistic timelines — not aspirational ones — into any forbearance-to-refinancing bridge. Because a hospitality asset's gaming or liquor licenses, state tax obligations, and employment levels are all collateral consequences of a receivership fight, lenders and borrowers negotiating around a licensed hospitality property should loop in the relevant state regulators early rather than litigating around them. Finally, counsel on both sides should recognize that a parallel state-court filing aimed at staying a federal receivership motion is a recognized defensive tactic, but its success still depends on showing the court a credible, time-bound path to resolving the underlying default.
