# Neighbors Fight to Keep Crypto-Mine Noise Suit Against Data Center Operator Out of Federal Court

### A proposed class action over "unrelenting" noise from a Cherokee County, North Carolina bitcoin-mining and AI data center tests how far commercial operators can rely on federal removal to control the venue — and the exposure — of a nuisance claim

By the time Judith Stines and William Huffman filed suit against PrimeBlock Operations, LLC and its affiliates this summer, residents near the company's cryptocurrency-mining and artificial-intelligence data center outside Murphy, North Carolina had been living with what one neighbor called "a small jet that never leaves" for nearly five years. Their case, *Stines et al. v. PrimeBlock Operations, LLC et al.*, is now before the U.S. District Court for the Western District of North Carolina, where the plaintiffs are fighting to send it back to state court — a jurisdictional skirmish that will shape how much leverage a commercial data-center operator can exert over where, and on what terms, a neighboring-nuisance claim gets litigated.

The underlying facts are straightforward. PrimeBlock, a California-based bitcoin-mining company, operates a roughly three-acre facility near Harshaw Road in Murphy that has run since September 2021, housing high-powered servers, generators, and industrial cooling systems. Stines and Huffman allege the facility's operators — PrimeBlock, together with affiliated entities Exponential Digital LLC and Innotech Z Inc. — have declined to invest in basic noise-abatement measures despite years of complaints, leaving nearby property owners to contend with a continuous mechanical drone that sound meters have measured at 55 to 85 decibels depending on weather conditions. The plaintiffs are seeking to represent a class of roughly 1,000 property owners and residents within a two-mile radius of the facility, framing the case as a common-law nuisance claim rather than a regulatory enforcement action.

The procedural fight now underway is almost as consequential as the underlying nuisance allegations. After the case was removed to federal court, the plaintiffs moved to send it back to North Carolina state court, arguing that the damages actually at stake fall short of the amount-in-controversy threshold required to keep a case in federal court under diversity or Class Action Fairness Act jurisdiction. That argument matters because venue is rarely neutral in nuisance litigation: state courts in rural counties are often seen as more receptive to local nuisance and property-value claims, while federal courts can offer a defendant more predictable procedural rules, a different jury pool, and — in a CAFA-eligible class action — a forum where aggregate damages calculations get more rigorous scrutiny before a class can proceed. A defendant's decision to remove, and a plaintiff's decision to fight for remand, is frequently a bet about which forum's tendencies favor which side, made well before anyone litigates the merits of whether a data center's cooling fans and generators actually constitute an actionable nuisance.

For the broader commercial real estate and infrastructure community, the case lands at a moment when data centers — the physical backbone of both cryptocurrency mining and the current AI buildout — are being sited at a pace and scale that increasingly puts them in tension with existing residential and rural land uses. Municipalities including Murphy itself have already responded to complaints about mining-facility noise by passing new local noise ordinances, but ordinance compliance and common-law nuisance liability are legally distinct questions: a facility can satisfy a decibel limit at the property line and still face a viable nuisance claim if a court finds its ongoing operation substantially and unreasonably interferes with neighbors' use and enjoyment of their property. That gap is precisely where cases like this one live, and it is a gap that ordinance compliance alone does not close.

The remand dispute also has a practical lesson for how these disputes get valued from the outset. Plaintiffs' counsel structuring a nuisance class action around a data center, crypto mine, or similar industrial facility increasingly calibrate their damages theory — often centered on diminished property value and loss of use and enjoyment rather than a fixed statutory penalty — with an eye toward the forum question as much as the merits, since a claim pled to stay under CAFA's $5 million aggregate threshold keeps the case in what plaintiffs view as a more favorable state venue.

**Practical takeaways.** Developers, operators, and financiers of data centers, cryptocurrency-mining facilities, and other high-density industrial infrastructure should treat sustained neighbor complaints about noise as a maturing legal exposure, not merely a community-relations issue, particularly once a facility has operated long enough for neighbors to document a pattern of ongoing interference. Compliance with a local noise ordinance should not be mistaken for a defense to common-law nuisance liability, since the two claims turn on different legal standards and a facility can be lawful under one while still exposed under the other. Counsel evaluating whether to remove a nuisance class action to federal court should model the plaintiffs' likely damages theory carefully before removing, since a forum fight that consumes months of litigation before the merits are even reached can itself become a meaningful cost and delay, and a remand loss can leave a defendant back in the venue it removal was designed to avoid.
