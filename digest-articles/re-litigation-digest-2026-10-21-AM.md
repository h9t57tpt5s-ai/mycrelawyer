# New York's First Department Upholds NYC's Pandemic-Era Commercial Guaranty Law

### A six-year-old Chelsea gallery lease dispute settles a question that has divided state and federal courts: can the City bar landlords from enforcing personal guaranties signed before COVID-19?

New York's Appellate Division, First Department, ruled on October 20, 2026 that New York City's so-called "guaranty law" -- which barred landlords from enforcing personal guaranties on certain commercial leases for a 16-month window during the COVID-19 pandemic -- does not violate the Contracts Clause of the U.S. Constitution. The decision, in *513 West 26th Realty LLC v. George Billis Galleries, Inc.*, reverses a trial court ruling that had struck the law down, and it lands squarely in the middle of a yearslong, unsettled fight between state and federal courts over the measure's constitutionality.

The case arose from an ordinary pandemic-era rent dispute. Landlord 513 West 26th Realty LLC leased space in Chelsea to George Billis Galleries, Inc., an art exhibition business, with gallery principal George Billis personally guarantying the tenant's obligations. When the gallery stopped paying rent during the pandemic, the landlord sued the tenant for breach of the lease and the guarantor for breach of the guaranty. The guarantor moved to dismiss, invoking New York City Administrative Code § 22-1005 -- the guaranty law, enacted by the City Council in April 2020 as part of a package of bills meant to help small businesses avoid personal bankruptcy during the pandemic's economic collapse. The law precluded enforcement of personal guaranties tied to certain commercial leases for defaults occurring during a defined COVID-19 period.

Supreme Court, New York County initially sided with the guarantor, dismissing the claims against him. The First Department reversed that first ruling in 2023, not to decide the constitutional question outright but to send the case back for a fuller record, in light of a Southern District of New York decision, *Melendez v. City of New York*, that had found the guaranty law unconstitutional. That federal ruling was itself later vacated by the Second Circuit in *Bochner v. City of New York*, leaving the question open again. On remand, Supreme Court this time declared the guaranty law unconstitutional under the Contracts Clause, reinstated the landlord's claims against the guarantor, and denied motions to dismiss filed by both the tenant and the City of New York, which had intervened to defend its own ordinance.

The First Department's decision, authored by Associate Justice Llinét M. Rosado, reverses that ruling and resolves the case in the City's favor. Applying the U.S. Supreme Court's three-part test for Contracts Clause challenges from *Energy Reserves Group, Inc. v. Kansas Power & Light Co.*, the panel held that the guaranty law survives scrutiny. It emphasized the "strong presumption of constitutionality" attached to legislative enactments and the substantial deference courts owe to a city council's judgment about the necessity of emergency measures -- deference the court found "particularly appropriate" given the declared public health emergency in New York State at the time the law passed. The panel was careful to note that its ruling takes no position on the law's wisdom as policy, only on its constitutionality.

For a city law passed six years ago as emergency pandemic relief, the stakes of this ruling are not simply historical. A substantial population of pre-2020 personal guaranties tied to commercial leases remain the subject of active collection litigation in New York courts, and landlords and guarantors alike have been litigating in the shadow of conflicting signals from state and federal courts -- first a federal district court finding the law unconstitutional, then a federal appellate vacatur that left the question unresolved, and now a state appellate ruling going the other way. The First Department's decision does not bind federal courts, and the Second Circuit has not yet ruled on the law's merits following the *Bochner* vacatur, so the conflict is not fully resolved. But it is now the controlling word for guaranty disputes litigated in New York's state courts, where the overwhelming majority of these cases are filed.

The practical effect for landlords pursuing legacy pandemic-era guaranty claims in New York state court is that the guaranty law remains a live defense: a personal guarantor whose tenant defaulted during the statutorily defined COVID-19 period cannot be held personally liable for that default, regardless of the Southern District's now-vacated contrary holding. Landlords who have been pressing or considering guaranty claims tied to 2020 and 2021 defaults should factor this ruling into settlement posture rather than assume federal precedent will rescue a guaranty claim in state court. Guarantors facing collection suits on legacy pandemic defaults gain a now-reaffirmed shield, at least in the First Department, though the same claim could still face a different result if re-litigated in federal court given the unsettled state of *Bochner*. Counsel on both sides of pending guaranty litigation should also watch for further appellate activity, since a ruling this significant -- reversing a trial court that had itself reversed course after remand -- is a natural candidate for further review, whether by the City seeking to cement the win elsewhere or by guarantors testing the ruling's reach in other departments.
