# Arizona Supreme Court Revives Contractor's $6.5M Claim Against Landowners Who Never Signed a Contract

### In Markham Contracting Co. v. Cahava Springs Phase I, Inc., a unanimous court holds that landowners who accept the benefit of infrastructure work can be liable for unjust enrichment even without privity to the contractor who built it

Commercial developers and landowners who rely on special improvement districts to finance roads, utilities, and other infrastructure got an unwelcome reminder on June 17, 2026: standing behind a public financing vehicle does not automatically insulate an owner from a contractor's unpaid bill. In Markham Contracting Co., Inc. v. Cahava Springs Phase I, Inc., et al., No. CV-25-0036-PR, the Arizona Supreme Court unanimously held that a contractor may pursue an unjust enrichment claim directly against landowners who benefited from public infrastructure improvements, even though the contractor's actual agreement ran only to the improvement district — not to the landowners themselves. Chief Justice Ann A. Scott Timmer authored the opinion for the full court.

The case arose out of a planned community development in Cave Creek, Arizona. Markham Contracting agreed with a public improvement district to build roads, water lines, and related infrastructure for the project. The landowners behind the development had helped create the district in the first place and agreed that special assessments levied against their parcels would fund the district's payment obligations to Markham. Partway through construction, a dispute arose and the district stopped paying. Markham took its claims against the district to arbitration and won, securing an award of roughly $6.5 million. But an award against a special-purpose improvement district is only as good as the district's ability to pay it, and Markham found itself unable to collect the full amount from the district alone.

Markham then turned to the landowners directly, suing them for unjust enrichment. Its theory was straightforward: the landowners had authorized creation of the district, agreed to fund it through assessments, and received a tangible, uncompensated benefit — completed roads, water lines, and other infrastructure serving their land — without Markham having been fully paid for the work that produced it. The landowners moved to dismiss, arguing they had no contract with Markham at all; their only relationship was with the district, a separate legal entity, and unjust enrichment could not be used to manufacture a payment obligation where no direct contractual relationship existed. The trial court agreed and dismissed Markham's claims, and the court of appeals affirmed in an unpublished decision.

The Arizona Supreme Court reversed. Writing for a unanimous court, Chief Justice Timmer rejected the landowners' central premise — that the absence of a direct contract between Markham and the landowners was fatal to an unjust enrichment claim. Unjust enrichment, the court explained, is an equitable doctrine that exists precisely to address situations where no adequate remedy at law, including a contract claim, is available. An owner who requests, authorizes, or accepts improvements performed by a contractor or subcontractor, and who fails to compensate the party that actually provided the labor and materials, can be held liable for the value of that benefit regardless of whether a formal contract connects the two parties. The court's opinion, according to legal commentary tracking the decision, effectively forecloses a "not my contract" defense that landowners in similar financing structures have used to avoid liability when an intermediate financing entity defaults.

For commercial real estate developers, the ruling lands squarely on a financing structure that is common well beyond Arizona: using a public improvement district, community facilities district, or similar special-purpose entity to fund and contract for infrastructure work, with landowners agreeing to repay the district through assessments. That structure is popular precisely because it lets developers push infrastructure costs onto a bond-like financing mechanism rather than carrying the debt on their own balance sheets, while still controlling — through their role in creating and funding the district — how the work gets done. Markham confirms that this arrangement does not fully insulate the landowners if the district itself cannot pay. A contractor left holding an uncollectible arbitration award or judgment against an underfunded district now has a viable equitable path to the landowners who actually benefit from the finished infrastructure.

The decision is also notable for what it signals about Arizona courts' broader posture toward unpaid contractors and subcontractors on complex, multi-party development projects. Coming amid a wave of construction-cost inflation and tighter project financing nationally, Markham gives contractors added leverage in disputes where a financing intermediary — whether a special district, a single-purpose entity, or an undercapitalized general contractor — proves unable to pay in full. Owners can expect more contractors and subcontractors to plead unjust enrichment as a fallback theory whenever the party they contracted with turns out to be judgment-proof.

Practical takeaways: Developers and landowners using special improvement districts or similar financing vehicles to fund infrastructure work should not assume that structure alone shields them from direct liability to contractors if the district defaults. Owners should build reserves, guarantees, or completion bonds sufficient to cover district payment obligations rather than relying on assessment revenue alone, since courts may look past the financing structure to the party that actually benefited from the completed work. Contractors and subcontractors on similar projects should preserve unjust enrichment as an alternative theory from the outset, documenting landowners' knowledge of and acquiescence in the work being performed. And counsel structuring these financing arrangements should revisit indemnification and assessment-shortfall provisions in light of Markham's clear signal that Arizona courts will not treat the absence of contractual privity as dispositive.
