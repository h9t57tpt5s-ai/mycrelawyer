# Third Circuit Lets Atlantic City Price-Fixing Case Against Casino-Hotels Stand, Denying Rehearing

### A rare appellate win for an algorithmic-pricing antitrust theory against the hospitality industry survives a bid for full-court review, keeping five Atlantic City casino-hotels and their shared pricing vendor in federal court

Atlantic City's largest casino-hotel operators have lost their bid to have a full panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit reconsider a decision that revived a price-fixing lawsuit over how they set hotel room rates. On October 2, 2026, the Third Circuit denied a petition for rehearing en banc in *Cornish-Adebiyi v. Caesars Entertainment, Inc.*, leaving intact a July 29, 2026 panel ruling that reversed dismissal of the case and sent it back to the district court for further proceedings. The denial is a significant marker in a wave of litigation testing whether shared, software-driven pricing recommendations can support an antitrust conspiracy claim even without any direct agreement among competitors to fix prices.

The case, filed in May 2023 in the U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey, accuses Caesars Entertainment, Inc.; Boardwalk Regency LLC; Harrah's Atlantic City Operating Company, LLC; Tropicana Atlantic City Corporation; MGM Resorts International; Marina District Development Company, LLC (operator of the Borgata Hotel Casino & Spa); Hard Rock International, Inc.; Seminole Hard Rock Support Services, LLC; and Boardwalk 1000, LLC of conspiring to fix hotel room rates in violation of Section 1 of the Sherman Act. The alleged mechanism is Cendyn Group, LLC's "Rainmaker" revenue-management platform, which the complaint says collected non-public pricing and occupancy data from the participating casino-hotels and used it to generate room-rate recommendations that the properties then adopted roughly 90% of the time, departing from Rainmaker's suggested price only through a documented internal "override" process.

A putative class of hotel guests who rented rooms at the defendant properties originally brought the suit, alleging the arrangement functioned as a "hub-and-spoke" conspiracy, with Cendyn's software as the hub coordinating pricing among the casino-hotel "spokes" that would otherwise compete independently. U.S. District Judge Karen M. Williams dismissed the complaint with prejudice on September 30, 2024, holding that the plaintiffs had not plausibly alleged the kind of parallel pricing conduct or pooled proprietary data necessary to distinguish lawful, independent use of a common software vendor from an unlawful agreement to restrain trade.

On appeal, a Third Circuit panel of Circuit Judges Restrepo, McKee, and Smith disagreed. In a precedential opinion authored by Judge McKee, the panel held that the complaint's allegations, taken as true, plausibly supported a horizontal price-fixing conspiracy facilitated by the shared pricing algorithm. The court pointed in particular to the allegation that the casino-hotels accepted Rainmaker's recommended prices approximately 90% of the time, reasoning that such a high rate of adoption, combined with the exchange of competitively sensitive data through a common platform, was enough to move the claim past the pleading stage. Commentators have noted the ruling places the Third Circuit at odds with a Ninth Circuit decision reaching a different result in a comparable Las Vegas algorithmic-pricing case, deepening a circuit split over how courts should treat shared revenue-management software under antitrust law.

With rehearing now denied, the case returns to the District of New Jersey for further proceedings on the merits, including discovery into how widely Rainmaker's recommendations were followed and what data flowed into the algorithm from each defendant.

## Why It Matters for Commercial Real Estate

The *Cornish-Adebiyi* litigation extends a legal theory that has already reshaped the multifamily housing sector — the RealPage-style "hub-and-spoke" algorithmic pricing claim — into hospitality real estate. Hotel and resort owners who rely on shared revenue-management software to set room rates now face the same basic exposure that apartment landlords using RealPage have confronted in dozens of suits and government enforcement actions over the past two years: an allegation that high adherence to a vendor's pricing recommendations, paired with the exchange of non-public competitor data through that vendor, is itself enough to plead an antitrust conspiracy. The Third Circuit's willingness to let that theory proceed past the pleading stage, and the fact that the full court declined to revisit the panel's reasoning, gives plaintiffs' firms a precedential win they can cite well beyond Atlantic City and well beyond the hotel industry.

For owners and operators of any commercial property that prices space, rooms, or units using a shared or third-party algorithmic tool — hotels, self-storage facilities, short-term rental portfolios, and multifamily and industrial landlords alike — the case is a reminder that the legal risk in these arrangements turns less on whether the software itself is lawful and more on how much non-public, competitively sensitive data flows into it and how mechanically an operator follows its output.

## Practical Takeaways

Commercial property owners and operators using shared or vendor-provided pricing or revenue-management software should treat this ruling as confirmation that the hub-and-spoke theory is not confined to multifamily housing and can reach any sector where competitors rely on a common pricing platform. Operators should audit what data — particularly non-public occupancy, rate, or booking information — flows into any third-party pricing tool, and should document the business rationale whenever they deviate from or depart significantly from a software recommendation, since a high rate of mechanical adherence is precisely what plaintiffs will point to as evidence of coordination. Given the emerging circuit split between the Third and Ninth Circuits, operators with properties in multiple jurisdictions should expect materially different litigation risk depending on where a case is filed, and general counsel should treat any revenue-management vendor relationship as warranting the same antitrust diligence now routinely applied to RealPage-adjacent software in the residential rental industry.
