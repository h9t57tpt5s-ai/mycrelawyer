# Michigan Appeals Court: Township Could Amend Zoning Mid-Litigation to Defeat Gravel Mine Developer's Permit Bid

### Schell Road, LLC's mandamus action against Grant Township shows how far local governments can go in revising an ordinance while a land-use application is pending

The Michigan Court of Appeals on October 13, 2026 affirmed a trial court ruling that handed a Grand Traverse County township a decisive win over a commercial landowner that had sought to force a public hearing on its special land use application. In *Schell Road, LLC v. Steve Vanderford*, the unpublished per curiam opinion rejected developer Schell Road, LLC's bid for a writ of mandamus against Grant Township's zoning administrator and planning commission, and upheld the township's decision to amend its zoning ordinance for mining operations while the litigation was pending. The decision is a cautionary tale for any commercial property owner banking on a fast-tracked entitlement process once local officials start raising concerns about an application's completeness.

Schell Road, LLC purchased 80 acres on Schell Road in Grant Township in 2022, intending to develop and operate a gravel mine. The company submitted an initial special land use application to the township that September, accompanied by site plans from a civil engineer. According to the opinion, it was the first mining-operation application the township had received since 2003, and it prompted officials to question whether the township's zoning ordinance, unchanged on mining regulations since 1998, properly accounted for state law that had been amended in 2011. While the township studied the issue, Schell Road submitted supplemental materials in December 2022 and January 2023 to address what officials described as deficiencies in the original filing. Township officials then told the company they intended to delay consideration of the application until the ordinance could be amended.

After an exchange of letters in which Schell Road's counsel accused the township of "playing a dangerous game" of deliberate delay, the company filed a circuit court complaint in February 2023 seeking a writ of mandamus to compel a public hearing on its application. While that case was pending, the Grant Township Planning Commission recommended, and the township board adopted, amendments to the mining provisions of the zoning ordinance, effective March 30, 2023. Schell Road amended its complaint to add claims that the ordinance amendments were enacted in bad faith specifically to block its gravel-mine application, along with due process, injunctive and declaratory-relief theories.

The Grand Traverse Circuit Court sided with the township on cross-motions for summary disposition, finding that Schell Road's original application was incomplete under the township's own pre-amendment ordinance and that the company therefore never had a "clear, legal right" to a hearing, the threshold requirement for mandamus relief in Michigan. The trial court also rejected the bad-faith theory, concluding the amended ordinance complied with the state statute governing township zoning, MCL 125.3205.

On appeal, the Court of Appeals panel of Judges Korobkin, Rick and Maldonado affirmed across the board. The court held that whether an application is "complete" under a zoning ordinance can be a question of law resolved on summary disposition rather than a disputed fact issue for trial, and that the trial court was entitled to weigh the extensive documentary record, including the parties' correspondence and the civil engineer's site plans, in reaching that conclusion. Because Schell Road could not establish the clear legal right that mandamus requires, the panel did not need to reach, and did not disturb, the trial court's separate finding that the ordinance amendment itself was not adopted in bad faith.

For commercial developers and their counsel, the case underscores two practical risks in entitlement litigation against a local government. First, a mandamus claim is an extraordinary remedy that lives or dies on the completeness of the underlying application measured against the ordinance as it existed when the application was submitted, and courts will treat that as a legal question they can decide without a trial if the record is developed enough. An applicant who believes its filing was complete needs a clean, well-documented record establishing exactly that, since ambiguity will be resolved against the extraordinary relief sought. Second, a municipality retains real latitude to amend its own zoning ordinance while an applicant's case is pending in court, particularly where the municipality can point to an independent, long-standing policy rationale, here, an ordinance that had gone unrevised for roughly a quarter-century, against a statutory backdrop that had already changed. Absent a strong, record-supported showing that the amendment was adopted for the specific purpose of defeating one applicant's project, courts are unlikely to second-guess the timing.

The decision does not foreclose further mining-permit disputes in Michigan townships, but it does confirm that a developer cannot assume the pre-amendment version of an ordinance will govern its application indefinitely simply because it filed first and sued when the delay began.
