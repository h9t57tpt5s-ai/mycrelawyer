# Michigan Appeals Court Upholds $525,000 Valuation in Retail Conversion Tax Fight

### The Court of Appeals affirms a Tax Tribunal ruling that credited the township's appraisal over the property owner's, finding the owner's below-market purchase price and renovation evidence unpersuasive.

A Michigan property owner's attempt to overturn an unfavorable commercial property tax assessment failed on October 14, 2026, when the Michigan Court of Appeals affirmed the Michigan Tax Tribunal's determination that a Redford Township retail property had a true cash value of $525,000 for the 2023 tax year. The decision, *Zecorra Enterprises, LLC v. Redford Township*, is a useful illustration of how fact-intensive and evidence-driven property tax valuation appeals remain in Michigan, even in a transaction that on its face looks like a straightforward arm's-length purchase.

Zecorra Enterprises bought the property, a long-vacant former bowling alley that had closed in 2019, in October 2022 for a total acquisition cost of $450,000, split between a $200,000 contract sales price and $250,000 in other consideration tied to personal property and fixtures. Before the sale even closed, Zecorra had already signed a construction contract to convert the building into a "Turn Key Box" retail location for a proposed Dollar General store. In December 2022, it signed a lease with Dolgencorp, LLC (Dollar General) obligating it to complete the buildout by April 1, 2023, and Dollar General took possession of the finished store in May 2023.

When Zecorra challenged Redford Township's resulting 2023 tax assessment, the fight came down to two sharply conflicting appraisals of the property's value as of the December 31, 2022 tax day. Zecorra's appraiser treated the $200,000 contract price as an arm's-length sale and, after deducting a "Build-Out/Repair Allowance," valued the property at just $180,000. The township's appraiser countered that the $200,000 price was a 62% discount from the property's $525,000 list price only eighteen months earlier, making it an unreliable indicator of market value, and valued the property at $570,000 using an income-capitalization approach with deductions for the renovation and lease-up period.

The Tax Tribunal, after a three-day evidentiary hearing, largely sided with the township. It found Zecorra's photographic evidence of the property's physical condition as of the tax date incomplete, undated, and in places unauthenticated, and found Zecorra's own appraiser's renovation-deduction testimony too vague to apply to the property. The tribunal then conducted its own independent valuation analysis, arriving at $529,000 under the sales-comparison approach and, after applying the township's own proposed non-stabilized adjustment, $521,000 under the income-capitalization approach. Reconciling the two, the tribunal set the property's final true cash value at $525,000.

On appeal, Zecorra argued that the tribunal erred by relying on the township appraiser's renovation deduction, which Zecorra said captured only a portion of its actual renovation costs of $797,783.70. The Court of Appeals, in a per curiam opinion, found no basis in the record to disturb the tribunal's weighing of the competing evidence and affirmed in full, emphasizing the tribunal's broad discretion to choose among competing, record-supported valuation methodologies.

For commercial property owners and the tax counsel who represent them, the case underscores several recurring themes in true-cash-value litigation. A recent purchase price is not a safe harbor in a valuation dispute if the taxing authority can show the price diverged sharply from a documented prior asking price or comparable sales, particularly where the seller's financial distress (here, a shuttered, deteriorating bowling alley) might explain a below-market sale. Tribunals and reviewing courts will credit a well-documented, methodologically disciplined appraisal over one built on incomplete or undated supporting evidence, regardless of which party bears the burden of persuasion on the overall assessment. And for owners pursuing adaptive-reuse or repositioning projects, like this conversion of a defunct big-box recreational use into a net-leased national retail tenant, the full renovation and lease-up timeline, not just the acquisition price, will be scrutinized closely in fixing value as of the relevant tax day.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial owners planning to challenge a tax assessment after a below-market acquisition should be prepared to affirmatively explain why the purchase price does not reflect market value, rather than simply asserting the transaction was at arm's length. Owners undertaking a renovation or repositioning project should maintain dated, authenticated documentation, photographs, invoices, and ledgers tied contemporaneously to the relevant valuation date, rather than reconstructing the record later from general ledgers compiled well after the fact. Finally, parties litigating valuation methodology before a state tax tribunal should recognize that appellate review of the tribunal's chosen approach and deduction amounts is highly deferential, so the real fight over value is won or lost at the tribunal hearing itself, not on appeal.
