# A Decade-Old Lead-Poisoning Judgment Becomes the Legal Tool That Ousted a Notorious Brooklyn Landlord

### A sheriff's execution sale on a 2017 tort judgment — not a housing-court order — is what finally forced three chronically distressed Crown Heights buildings out of the Dukler family's hands, offering a rare playbook for displacing an unresponsive owner.

Three rent-stabilized walk-ups on Eastern Parkway and Sterling Place in Crown Heights, Brooklyn changed hands on September 4, 2026, ending nearly a decade of tenant organizing against a landlord family that has repeatedly ranked among New York City's worst. The transfer is legally notable less for the outcome than for the mechanism: it was accomplished not through a housing-court eviction of the owner or a negotiated sale, but through a New York City Sheriff's execution sale on a nine-year-old tort judgment, illustrating an underused route for tenants and municipalities confronting entrenched, judgment-proof-seeming landlords.

The three buildings — 1018 Eastern Parkway, 1074 Eastern Parkway, and 1392 Sterling Place, totaling 88 units — were owned by Rikud Realty, an entity affiliated with, and half-owned by, Sara Dukler of the Dukler family. Rubin Dukler and entities connected to him have appeared for years on the New York City Public Advocate's worst-landlords list. By the time of the sale, the three properties had accumulated nearly 1,000 open Department of Housing Preservation and Development violations against just 88 units, including roughly 400 classified as immediately hazardous, covering conditions such as lead paint, rodent and roach infestation, and chronic leaks. The buildings had been placed in HPD's Alternative Enforcement Program, reserved for the city's most distressed housing stock, and the agency brought Rikud into housing court in 2020 to compel repairs after two earlier tenant lawsuits, filed in February 2019 and January 2020, cited 561 open violations and roughly $2 million in alleged illegal rent overcharges, respectively.

None of that housing-court activity produced the ownership change tenants had spent years seeking. What did was a separate, older piece of litigation entirely unconnected to the conditions fight: a 2017 judgment of approximately $2.2 million entered against Rikud in a lead-poisoning case brought by a tenant injured by the buildings' own deteriorated paint. That judgment sat largely dormant for years before an investor connected to Iris Holdings Group, the property's management company, acquired the creditor's rights to it. This September, that judgment holder used it to force a sheriff's sale of the three properties — a judgment-execution auction run by the city Sheriff's Office rather than a foreclosure or bankruptcy proceeding. The judgment creditor won the auction with a credit bid, effectively purchasing the properties by canceling debt rather than paying cash, but then declined to take on ownership and operation of severely distressed, rent-stabilized housing. Real estate investor Mark Schwartz, who also serves as mayor of Teaneck, New Jersey, and his firm Brooklyn Affordable Housing Associates stepped into that gap, paying roughly $7.6 million in total consideration to take the buildings instead.

The resolution tenants and the city are now pursuing runs through a nonprofit ownership structure: the buildings will be held by a housing development fund corporation, a vehicle commonly used in New York to convert distressed rental housing into resident-controlled cooperatives. HPD has indicated it expects to provide roughly $10 million in rehabilitation financing, subject to a financial needs assessment, and the Dukler Tenant Union — formed in 2025 after tenants began organizing in 2018 and later staged a rent strike — has reached a separate agreement with Schwartz committing to tenant-overseen rehabilitation and an eventual path to limited-equity cooperative ownership.

For landlords and asset managers, the case is a pointed reminder that a personal-injury or premises-liability judgment entered against a property-owning entity does not simply disappear if left unpaid; it remains a live, transferable lien that a patient or opportunistic creditor can enforce years later through an execution sale, potentially forcing a change of control the owner never negotiated. For tenant advocates, municipal housing agencies, and community land trusts, the episode demonstrates that judgment liens tied to a building's own history of neglect — the very conditions litigation typically seeks to remedy — can, in the right circumstances, become a more effective lever for displacing a chronically noncompliant owner than repair-focused housing-court proceedings alone, particularly where an owner is prepared to absorb violations and fines indefinitely rather than sell voluntarily.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Property owners should treat unsatisfied tort or premises-liability judgments against ownership entities as an ongoing solvency and control risk, not a closed matter, since judgment creditors can revive and enforce them years later through an execution sale that bypasses the owner's own decision to sell.
- Tenant organizations and legal-services providers pursuing chronically noncompliant landlords should audit outstanding judgments against the ownership entity — not just open code violations — since an existing lien may offer a faster path to a change of ownership than incremental housing-court remedies.
- Buyers stepping in after a judgment-execution sale, as Schwartz did here, should budget for both the acquisition price and a substantial near-term capital-improvement obligation, since these properties typically carry deferred maintenance well beyond what a conventional purchase would reflect.
- Municipal housing agencies structuring HDFC or similar nonprofit transfers following a distressed sale should build in financing commitments and tenant-oversight mechanisms at the time of transfer, since the transfer itself resolves ownership but not the underlying capital needs that produced the violations in the first place.
