# Utah's "Preliminary Municipality" Law Faces Constitutional Test Over Moab-Area Resort Project

### A suit challenging the state's novel developer-governed municipality statute puts SB258's zoning and land-use delegation squarely before a Utah district court

A pair of Utah nonprofits has asked a state district court to strike down the mechanism behind one of the more unusual land-use experiments in the country: a 2024 Utah statute that lets qualifying developers petition to form their own transitional local government, complete with zoning and infrastructure authority, before a single resident lives there. Kane Creek Development Watch and Living Rivers filed suit on February 10, 2026 in Utah's Seventh District Court in Grand County against the Lieutenant Governor of Utah, in her capacity as the official who administers the certification process, and against the developer behind Echo Canyon, the project that became the law's first real-world test case. The complaint asks the court to invalidate both Senate Bill 258 and the certification it conferred on Echo Canyon, a proposed 176-acre mixed-use resort development in the Kane Creek canyon corridor southwest of Moab, along the Colorado River.

SB258, enacted in 2024, created a new category of governmental entity called a "preliminary municipality." Under the statute, landowners who control a qualifying parcel can petition the Lieutenant Governor's office to certify a transitional governing board with authority to adopt zoning ordinances, approve infrastructure, and otherwise exercise land-use powers ordinarily reserved to elected county or city government, all while the entity pursues a path toward formal incorporation as a town. Echo Canyon became the first project in Utah to receive that certification, in June 2025. Current plans for the site call for roughly 478 housing units, 102 overnight accommodations, and about 67,000 square feet of commercial space in a canyon corridor that has otherwise been governed by Grand County's land-use code.

The plaintiffs' central claim is a constitutional one: that SB258 unlawfully transfers zoning and land-use authority, a core function of elected, publicly accountable government, to a board that is effectively selected by and answerable to the very landowners seeking to develop the property, without an election, a public vote, or the oversight mechanisms that ordinarily constrain a municipality's exercise of police power. The complaint further argues that the statute amounts to an impermissible "private or special" law under the Utah Constitution's prohibition on legislation that confers a benefit or exemption on a specific, identifiable private party rather than establishing a generally applicable rule, since SB258's qualifying criteria functioned, in practice, to fit only the Echo Canyon parcel at the time of passage.

The dispute does not exist in isolation. A related suit filed by the same two plaintiffs in mid-2025 challenges the water rights Echo Canyon's developer has claimed for the project, arguing that several of the underlying water rights, including one the plaintiffs say lapsed in 2019 when the developer missed a state filing deadline, have been forfeited through decades of nonuse and cannot lawfully support the scale of development now proposed. A hearing on motions to dismiss in that companion case was calendared for March 2026. Together, the two matters put both halves of Echo Canyon's viability, its governing authority and its water supply, in front of Utah courts at the same time.

For commercial developers and their counsel, the case is worth tracking well beyond Grand County. A small but growing number of states have experimented with statutory pathways that let large-scale developers exercise quasi-governmental land-use authority over their own projects, marketed as a way to accelerate housing and infrastructure delivery without waiting on traditional municipal approval processes. Utah's preliminary municipality law is among the most aggressive versions of that model now on the books, and a ruling on its constitutionality, whichever way it comes out, will shape how other states calibrate similar proposals and how aggressively developers elsewhere can rely on self-governance structures to bypass friction with existing local government.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Developers evaluating land-use structures that delegate zoning or infrastructure authority to a project-controlled entity should assume that structure will draw a constitutional challenge in states without a long track record of such statutes, and should build litigation risk and timeline delay into underwriting rather than treating certification as a settled matter.
- Because the Echo Canyon suit pairs a governance challenge with a separate water-rights forfeiture claim, counsel structuring large resort or mixed-use projects in water-constrained jurisdictions should confirm that all underlying water rights have been affirmatively put to beneficial use and are well documented, since a forfeiture theory can undercut a project's viability independent of any zoning fight.
- Owners and lenders underwriting projects that rely on novel or first-of-their-kind statutory authority should monitor the litigation closely, since a successful constitutional challenge could retroactively cloud title, permits, or entitlements issued under the certification while the case was pending.
