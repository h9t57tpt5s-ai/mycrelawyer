# Boston Landlord's Tax-Retaliation Suit Clears a Motion to Dismiss — and Opens a New Front for Commercial Owners Everywhere

### A Suffolk Superior Court judge lets a class claim that the city punished commercial landlords for appealing their assessments move into discovery, testing a First Amendment theory that could travel well beyond Massachusetts

A lawsuit accusing the City of Boston of quietly punishing commercial property owners for exercising their legal right to appeal tax assessments has survived the city's motion to dismiss and is now moving into discovery — a development that commercial real estate owners and their tax counsel nationally should be watching closely, even if they never set foot in Suffolk County.

The case, brought in Suffolk Superior Court by HS148SST LLC, owner of the 11-story office building at 148 State Street near Boston's Financial District, accuses the city's Assessor's Office of running what the complaint calls an "Add-Back Policy." According to the filing, prepared by the Pioneer New England Legal Foundation together with Sullivan & Worcester LLP, assessors began in 2024 flagging commercial properties with open appeals before the state's Appellate Tax Board (ATB) and then overriding their own determinations of fair market value for those properties, effectively restoring the prior year's higher assessment rather than applying the figure the city's own valuation process would otherwise have produced. The complaint alleges the practice touched at least 60 commercial properties and, for 148 State Street alone, resulted in a $41,351 overcharge in a single fiscal year.

What makes the case more than a routine assessment dispute is the theory HS148SST is advancing: that conditioning a property's assessed value on whether its owner challenged a prior valuation amounts to retaliation against the exercise of a legal right, and specifically implicates the First Amendment's protection against government retaliation for petitioning activity — here, petitioning the ATB. Judge Peter Krupp, in denying the city's motion to dismiss, did not resolve whether the city actually did what the complaint describes, or whether retaliation can be proven. But he found the allegations serious enough to warrant discovery, writing that limited fact-finding was needed to determine the existence and extent of the alleged Add-Back Policy before any final determination about equitable relief. That ruling alone does not decide the case, but it keeps a novel constitutional theory alive against a municipal assessor's office, with class claims attached, which is itself unusual in property tax litigation.

For commercial owners, the stakes extend well past the roughly $41,000 at issue for this one building. If the plaintiffs can show a systemic practice affecting dozens of properties, the remedy sought is injunctive and declaratory: a court order barring the city from continuing the practice, a declaration that it is unlawful, and repayment of excess taxes collected for fiscal years 2024 and 2025 across the class. That would be a meaningful signal to assessors in any jurisdiction that treat an ATB or similar appeal as a reason to hold a property's valuation at its prior, higher level rather than apply their own current assessment methodology. Commercial landlords regularly weigh whether an abatement appeal is worth the administrative cost and the risk of souring a relationship with local tax officials; a theory that an adverse assessment practice tied directly to the act of appealing is itself actionable gives owners a second lever beyond the ATB process itself.

The case also illustrates a broader pattern property owners and REIT asset managers are increasingly willing to pursue: using litigation discovery to force municipalities to produce internal assessment records and communications that are otherwise difficult to obtain through public records requests alone, particularly where a practice is alleged to be informal or undocumented in any public policy statement. Discovery in this case will likely probe assessor work papers, internal notes referencing the ATB appeal status of individual parcels, and communications about the "Add-Back" methodology, which could either substantiate or undercut the class-wide allegations.

Boston is not the only city where assessors face scrutiny for how they treat appealed properties, and a ruling on the merits here, whichever way it goes, will likely be cited elsewhere. Commercial owners and their counsel should treat the discovery phase as the real test of this case: if internal city records corroborate a deliberate add-back practice, the exposure could extend to repayment obligations and policy changes well beyond the original plaintiff.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Commercial owners who have successfully appealed an assessment should watch for unexplained increases in a subsequent year's valuation that track back toward a prior, higher figure, and should preserve correspondence with assessors that might later support or rebut a retaliation claim.
- A First Amendment retaliation theory aimed at a municipal assessor's practices is a novel vehicle for challenging systemic valuation conduct that falls short of an explicit written policy, and its survival past a motion to dismiss — even without a merits ruling — is itself useful precedent for owners elsewhere facing similar patterns.
- Asset managers overseeing multi-property commercial portfolios in jurisdictions with active ATB-style appeal processes should consider whether a pattern of post-appeal valuation increases across their own holdings would support a similar claim, since this case's discovery record may become a template for proving systemic retaliation.
