# JCPenney's Real Estate Trust Countersues Onyx Partners Over Collapsed $947M Portfolio Deal

### A yearlong effort to unwind JCPenney's bankruptcy real estate has curdled into dueling breach-of-contract suits in Manhattan, with the seller now accusing its would-be buyer of dangling a "sham" offer it never had the money to close.

Copper Property CTL Pass Through Trust, the special-purpose entity created out of J.C. Penney's 2020 Chapter 11 reorganization to hold and eventually monetize the retailer's owned real estate, filed suit against Boston-based private equity firm Onyx Partners and its founder, Anton Melchionda, on August 25 in New York Supreme Court, New York County. The complaint alleges that Onyx fraudulently induced Copper to sign a $947 million agreement to sell 119 former J.C. Penney store properties, spanning more than 15 million square feet across 35 states, by misrepresenting the state of its financing. Copper is seeking at least $150 million in damages.

The filing is the latest turn in a deal that began with considerable promise. Onyx agreed roughly a year ago to acquire the portfolio outright, a transaction that would have allowed Copper's beneficiaries, largely the unsecured creditors left holding claims from J.C. Penney's bankruptcy, to realize value from real estate that had been slowly leased back and sold off in the years since the retailer's reorganization. The deal was expected to close in the fall of 2025 but slipped repeatedly, and by late December Onyx had missed what Copper treated as a final deadline. Copper walked away from the agreement and, within days, Onyx sued first, filing its own breach-of-contract action in Manhattan Supreme Court in early January. That complaint accused Copper of acting in bad faith by quietly shopping the portfolio to other prospective buyers while negotiations with Onyx were still ongoing, and of blocking the closing by refusing to deliver a tenant estoppel certificate Onyx says the purchase agreement required. Onyx is seeking specific performance of the sale or, in the alternative, damages.

Copper's new complaint answers that suit with an aggressive counter-narrative. Rather than characterizing itself as the party that sabotaged the deal, Copper alleges that Onyx never had a realistic path to funding the purchase and that Melchionda and his firm "repeatedly misrepresented the status of funding discussions with major financial institutions" to make Onyx's financing look more certain than it actually was. In Copper's telling, the entire bid was a sham designed to lock up the portfolio and buy time, using leaked media coverage of the pending sale to its own advantage while quietly failing to line up the capital needed to perform. The complaint further alleges that the resulting uncertainty depressed the portfolio's value and left Copper unable to pursue alternative buyers on comparable terms once the Onyx deal fell apart.

A separate, related skirmish is playing out over the $5 million deposit Onyx posted when the agreement was signed. Copper is holding $2 million of that deposit and is now seeking to claim the remaining $3 million held in escrow, a demand Onyx is contesting in the same litigation.

For an audience of general counsel, asset managers, and institutional sellers, the dispute is a useful case study in how large, complex portfolio sales can unravel even after extensive due diligence and a signed agreement. Purchase-and-sale agreements involving distressed or bankruptcy-derived real estate frequently include closing conditions, such as tenant estoppel deliveries, financing contingencies, and deadline extensions, that create leverage for both sides once a deal begins to wobble; here, each party is now pointing to the other's conduct around those very mechanisms as the true cause of the collapse. Sellers negotiating large portfolio dispositions, particularly through litigation trusts or other creditor-recovery vehicles with fiduciary obligations to beneficiaries, should take note of how quickly a buyer's financing representations can become the central battleground once a deal falls through, and how naming a fund's principal individually, as Copper has done with Melchionda, can raise the stakes and complicate a defendant's settlement calculus. Buyers, in turn, should recognize that aggressive use of media narratives during a contested closing can later be cited as evidence of bad faith rather than legitimate deal pressure. With both sides now litigating in the same New York courthouse and roughly $947 million in real estate value in the balance, the case is likely to generate substantial discovery into Onyx's financing efforts and Copper's marketing conduct before either side sees a ruling on the merits.
