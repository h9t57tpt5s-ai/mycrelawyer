# Kansas Fraud Suit Accuses Tulsa Multifamily Sponsor of Looting Investor Funds as $1B Apartment Empire Collapses

### A business partner's 199-page complaint, a religious-arbitration defense, and a hostile takeover by a bankrupt private lender are converging on one of the Heartland's largest apartment portfolios — with lessons for anyone who invests alongside a sponsor with unchecked control of the books.

A fraud lawsuit filed in Johnson County, Kansas has become the legal centerpiece of what is shaping up as one of 2026's more dramatic commercial real estate collapses: the unraveling of Vesta Realty and Vesta Capital, a Tulsa-based multifamily platform that at its peak controlled roughly 39 apartment properties and nearly 10,000 units across Oklahoma, Kansas, and Arkansas, valued at close to $1 billion. The case, captioned Vesta Capital, LLC, et al. v. Marc Kulick, et al. and pending before Judge Joann Woltman in the Tenth Judicial District, accuses Kulick, the platform's founder and chief executive, of diverting more than $37 million in investor and company funds for personal use while concealing the portfolio's deteriorating finances from the very investors who capitalized it.

The suit was brought by Josef Loeffler, a longtime business partner who says he was responsible for raising capital for the venture while Kulick, as sole manager, retained exclusive control over the properties' books, bank accounts, and cash management. According to the complaint, Kulick cultivated a public image as a philanthropist and savvy operator even as the portfolio slid toward insolvency, allegedly moving cash between affiliated entities to mask shortfalls, sidestepping the accounting controls investors had been promised, and deflecting scrutiny with what the pleading colorfully describes as "blandishments, bluster and bullying." The complaint further alleges Kulick liquidated at least one property without distributing proceeds to investors, took out additional loans without authorization, and directed company funds toward more than $37 million in personal credit card payments, car loans, and gambling debts.

The litigation carries an unusual procedural wrinkle. Loeffler and Kulick had structured at least part of their arrangement as a "heter iska," a rabbinical financing instrument that recasts what would otherwise be an interest-bearing loan as a profit-sharing investment so as to comply with Jewish law's prohibition on charging interest between coreligionists. When Loeffler moved to enforce his rights in Kansas state court, Kulick argued the dispute belonged before a beis din, a rabbinical tribunal, rather than a secular judge. Loeffler's counsel countered that Kulick had already been summoned before religious authorities, refused to appear, and was subsequently issued a heter arko'os — effectively a religious-law permission slip authorizing recourse to civil court — making his belated invocation of the arbitration clause, in the plaintiffs' words, "remarkable for its audacity." The motion to dismiss remains pending.

While the Kansas fraud suit works through Judge Woltman's docket, Vesta's broader collapse has spilled into courts and receiverships across two more states. In Oklahoma County, lenders on the 272-unit Drexel Flats complex in southwest Oklahoma City moved to foreclose after Vesta-affiliated borrowers failed to turn over rents on a matured loan carrying roughly $28 million to $30 million in outstanding debt; a state court judge ordered Vesta removed as property manager and a receiver installed after tenants endured months of unpaid utility bills and overflowing trash service, though Vesta briefly regained control of the property on appeal before the receivership dispute returned to the docket. Separately, Efraim Diveroli — the disgraced former arms dealer whose exploits inspired the film "War Dogs" — extended Kulick a series of loans carrying reported interest rates compounding at extraordinary annualized levels, then moved through his firm, YSA Investments 1, LLC, to seize roughly two dozen Vesta-affiliated properties via deeds in lieu of foreclosure after Kulick defaulted. That takeover fight took its own dramatic turn on July 28, 2026, when YSA itself filed for Chapter 11 protection in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, with a Chapter 11 trustee appointed within minutes of the filing to sort out competing claims to the disputed properties.

For commercial real estate investors, lenders, and joint-venture partners, the Vesta saga is a case study in governance risk that has nothing to do with market cycles or interest rates. A sponsor with sole, unchecked authority over cash management — reinforced by financing structures, like the heter iska, that operate outside conventional secured-lending documentation — can obscure a portfolio's true condition from capital partners for a surprisingly long time, particularly when growth is funded through complex intercompany transfers rather than transparent, audited distributions. The receivership actions playing out in Oklahoma also illustrate how quickly deferred maintenance and unpaid municipal bills can escalate into a full change of control once a court concludes tenants are being harmed. And the entrance of a high-cost private lender willing to extend usurious-seeming credit to a distressed sponsor — only to end up in bankruptcy itself — is a reminder that rescue capital arriving after a sponsor is already underwater rarely resolves a distressed situation cleanly; it just adds another layer of creditors and litigation to unwind. Investors negotiating LLC operating agreements should insist on independent audit rights, distribution waterfalls with real teeth, and forum-selection clauses that cannot later be weaponized to delay accountability, while lenders evaluating a distressed sponsor's rescue financing should scrutinize not just the collateral but the sponsor's demonstrated pattern of financial transparency before extending fresh credit into an already-troubled capital stack.
