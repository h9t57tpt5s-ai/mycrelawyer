# Massachusetts Appeals Court: A Landlord's Fixed Notice Defect Doesn't Bar a Second Eviction Suit

### In *Eng v. Chen's Food Enterprise*, the state's intermediate appellate court holds that curing a defective termination notice resets the clock on claim preclusion — reviving a commercial landlord's $198,000 judgment against a Roxbury restaurant tenant.

A Massachusetts landlord who stumbled on the first attempt to evict a nonpaying commercial tenant, then fixed the defect and tried again, did not forfeit the right to collect nearly $200,000 in back rent, the Massachusetts Appeals Court ruled on August 24. The decision in *Eng v. Chen's Food Enterprise, Inc.*, No. 25-P-1222, reverses an Appellate Division ruling that had thrown out the landlord's judgment on claim-preclusion grounds, and it settles a question that recurs constantly in commercial landlord-tenant practice: what happens when a summary process case is dismissed for a fixable procedural error, and the landlord comes back and does it right the second time.

The case began in the Roxbury Division of the Boston Municipal Court, where trustee Paul Eng filed a summary process action against Chen's Food Enterprise, Inc. for nonpayment of rent. That first case was dismissed because Eng had not served the tenant with the written notice to quit that the underlying lease required before litigation could commence — a common and entirely curable defect, but a jurisdictional one under Massachusetts summary process practice. Eng served a corrected notice of lease termination and filed a second summary process action. Following a three-day bench trial before Judge Kenneth J. Fiandaca, the trial court rejected the tenant's argument that the first dismissal barred the second suit, and entered judgment for Eng on both possession and $198,000 in unpaid rent.

Chen's Food Enterprise appealed to the Appellate Division of the District Court/Boston Municipal Court departments, and won a reversal. The Appellate Division held that the first case's dismissal operated as an adjudication on the merits for purposes of claim preclusion (res judicata), meaning the second suit — even armed with a corrected notice — should never have been allowed to proceed. That holding, had it stood, would have handed commercial tenants a powerful tool: any landlord who missed a notice requirement in an initial filing could be permanently barred from ever recovering the rent through summary process, regardless of how promptly or completely the defect was cured.

Writing for a unanimous panel that also included Justices Ditkoff and Tan, Justice Sookyoung Shin rejected that reading. The court held that the Appellate Division's application of claim preclusion "could not be reconciled with the plain language" of G. L. c. 239, § 7, the statute governing summary process for nonpayment of rent, and concluded that the statute affirmatively authorizes a landlord to bring a second action once the underlying notice defect has been fixed. In the panel's view, a dismissal grounded purely in a landlord's failure to satisfy a notice precondition — as opposed to a ruling on the substantive question of whether rent was actually owed — is not the kind of merits determination that claim-preclusion doctrine is designed to protect. The unpaid-rent question had never actually been tried or decided in the first case; it was tried, for the first time, in the second.

For commercial landlords and their counsel throughout Massachusetts, the ruling closes off what had become, after the Appellate Division's decision, a genuinely dangerous trap: a landlord who got a notice-to-quit technicality wrong could have faced not just delay, but the permanent loss of any judicial remedy for rent that a tenant plainly owed. The Appeals Court's opinion restores the more conventional and more workable rule — that procedural dismissals for curable notice defects leave the underlying rent claim intact, so long as the landlord actually fixes the problem before refiling. Tenants' counsel, for their part, lose a defense that would have functioned less like a merits argument and more like a windfall keyed to a landlord's paperwork error, but the decision leaves untouched their ability to litigate the merits of nonpayment, notice compliance, and lease-termination disputes in the second action itself, as Chen's Food Enterprise did across three days of trial.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Commercial landlords in Massachusetts who have a summary process action dismissed for a defective or missing notice to quit should treat that dismissal as correctable, not fatal — serve a proper termination notice and refile rather than assuming the claim is lost.
- Property managers and in-house counsel should audit lease and statutory notice requirements before initiating any nonpayment summary process action; the underlying defect in this case was avoidable and cost the landlord more than two years of delay before recovering the judgment.
- Tenants' counsel should not count on an early notice-based dismissal as a permanent shield against a rent claim; the decision confirms that a landlord gets a real second chance once the procedural defect is cured, so litigation strategy should focus on the merits of the rent dispute rather than banking on preclusion.
- The decision is specific to notice-based dismissals under G. L. c. 239, § 7, and does not disturb claim-preclusion principles generally — a dismissal that actually resolves the merits of a rent dispute will still likely carry preclusive effect in a later action.
