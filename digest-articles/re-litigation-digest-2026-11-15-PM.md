# McCormick County Rezoning for 678-Acre Data Center Challenged in Court

### South Carolina residents say the county's industrial rezoning ignored its own comprehensive plan and zoning procedures, in the latest of a wave of suits testing local governments' power to approve AI-driven data center development

A group of McCormick County, South Carolina residents filed suit on November 6, 2026 in the McCormick County Court of Common Pleas, challenging the county council's September 10 vote to rezone roughly 678 acres of agricultural land to industrial use for a proposed data center. The defendants are McCormick County, the McCormick County Council, and the two Pollard entities tied to the site, Pollard Land Company, Inc. and Pollard Lumber Co., Inc. The suit is the latest entry in a fast-growing docket of litigation nationwide over the land-use approvals that AI-driven data center demand is forcing onto rural and suburban county governments, following similar suits this year in Georgia, New Jersey, Ohio and Michigan.

The rezoning at issue, Ordinance 25-11, cleared the McCormick County Council on a 3-2 vote after a monthslong public fight. The county's own planning commission had recommended against the rezoning in July, following a series of contentious public meetings over the summer in which residents raised concerns about noise, heavy water consumption, and the strain a large-scale data center campus would place on rural infrastructure. The council had also considered, and rejected, a proposed nine-month moratorium on new data center development before proceeding to approve the Pollard-site rezoning instead. The project has been linked in local reporting to a companion natural gas pipeline intended to serve the facility's power needs, and at least one area family has separately described losing land to eminent domain tied to the pipeline corridor.

The complaint lists three causes of action, all aimed at the county's process rather than at the wisdom of hosting a data center. First, the plaintiffs allege the county failed to follow the zoning-amendment procedures required under South Carolina law and its own ordinances before adopting Ordinance 25-11. Second, they argue the rezoning is inconsistent with McCormick County's adopted comprehensive plan, which plaintiffs say designates the Pollard tract for uses other than heavy industrial development. Third, the complaint alleges the county failed to adequately evaluate the project's impact on surrounding infrastructure and neighboring properties before approving the change. Those theories track the procedural and comprehensive-plan-consistency claims that have become the standard playbook in data center zoning litigation this year, echoing suits over data center rezonings in Stokes County, North Carolina, and Coweta and Effingham counties in Georgia.

For commercial real estate owners, developers and lenders, the case is a reminder that a favorable rezoning vote is rarely the end of the entitlement story for a large industrial or data center project. A county council majority can approve a rezoning only to find the approval itself becomes the next battlefield, with opponents using the same comprehensive-plan-consistency and procedural-compliance doctrines that have long applied to conventional commercial and industrial rezonings. That exposure is squarely the developer's and the landowner's problem: a successful challenge can unwind the zoning a deal was underwritten on, stall construction financing, and delay or kill the lease-up or build-out timeline a data center operator or its construction lender is counting on.

The litigation also illustrates how comprehensive-plan consistency requirements, often treated as a formality in routine rezonings, are becoming a primary line of attack against large energy-intensive developments. Developers acquiring or optioning land for data centers, logistics campuses, or other large industrial users should not treat a locally adopted comprehensive plan as background noise; where a plan's land-use designations do not clearly support the intended industrial use, a rezoning that departs from it is a built-in litigation risk that counsel should flag and that purchase and financing contingencies should account for before closing.

No hearing date had been reported as of this writing, and McCormick County has not yet filed a public response to the complaint.

**Practical takeaways:**
- A rezoning vote is not the end of entitlement risk for a large industrial or data center project; challengers are increasingly attacking the rezoning itself on comprehensive-plan-consistency and procedural grounds even after a council majority approves it.
- Developers and lenders underwriting data center, logistics, or other heavy-industrial acquisitions should diligence the host jurisdiction's comprehensive plan designation for the site, not just the zoning map, since a mismatch is now a standard litigation theory.
- Expect this fact pattern — a rural county weighing noise, water use and infrastructure strain against the tax and economic-development case for a data center — to keep generating entitlement litigation as AI-driven demand pushes data center siting into smaller jurisdictions without established industrial zoning frameworks.
- Transaction timelines and construction financing contingencies for data center and large industrial sites should build in the realistic possibility of a post-approval entitlement challenge, particularly where a planning commission has already recommended against the rezoning, as McCormick County's did here.
