# Sunrise Realty Trust Stockholders Sued Over Southern Realty Trust Merger Disclosures

### Two New York suits claimed the REIT's proxy statement omitted material facts ahead of an all-stock combination that closed anyway on November 5

Two Sunrise Realty Trust, Inc. (NASDAQ: SUNS) stockholders sued the commercial real estate finance REIT and its board of directors in New York state court in late September 2026, alleging that the proxy statement prepared for its merger with Southern Realty Trust Inc. (SRT) omitted material information stockholders needed to cast an informed vote. The merger closed anyway on November 5, 2026, after Sunrise filed supplemental disclosures that it says were meant to moot the claims rather than concede they had merit — a now-familiar pattern in REIT and other public-company merger litigation.

The first suit, *Miller v. Sunrise Realty Trust, Inc., et al.*, was filed September 23, 2026 in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, New York County, under Index No. 655415/2026. A second, nearly identical complaint, *Smith v. Sunrise Realty Trust, Inc., et al.*, followed a day later under Index No. 655426/2026. Both name Sunrise and its board as defendants and assert disclosure-based claims under New York common law. Sunrise disclosed both suits, along with pre-suit demand letters raising similar objections, in the "Merger Litigation" section of its Form 10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, filed with the SEC on November 10.

### The underlying deal

Sunrise and Southern Realty Trust signed a merger agreement on August 5, 2026, structured as a stock-for-stock combination: each share of SRT common stock would convert into 1.45 shares of newly issued SUNS common stock, plus $0.05 in cash per share funded by Sunrise's external manager, Sunrise Manager LLC, which was itself a party to the merger agreement for limited purposes. The deal required approval from stockholders of both companies, including a vote by SUNS stockholders to approve the issuance of the merger shares — roughly 8.46 million of them.

Plaintiffs' core theory, typical of this genre of merger-objection litigation, is that the Proxy Statement omitted material information about the deal's background, the financial advisors' analyses, or potential conflicts of interest, information a reasonable stockholder would want before voting. Sunrise has said it believes the claims lack merit but chose to supplement its proxy disclosures anyway, stating in its 10-Q that doing so was intended "to moot certain plaintiffs' disclosure claims and avoid the risk that the Litigation Matters delay or otherwise adversely affect the Merger." That is a standard risk-management calculation in deal litigation: the cost of additional disclosure is far lower than the cost of a delayed or enjoined stockholder vote, even when management is confident the underlying claims would not ultimately succeed.

### The vote and the closing

Both companies held special stockholder meetings on October 23, 2026. At Sunrise's meeting, roughly 59.5% of outstanding shares were voted, and approximately 91.6% of votes cast approved the share issuance proposal. SRT stockholders approved the merger at their own meeting the same day. With both approvals secured, the merger closed on November 5, 2026; Sunrise issued approximately 8,464,977 new shares as merger consideration, bringing its total shares outstanding to roughly 21,980,828.

Sunrise's 10-Q does not disclose that either suit has been dismissed, withdrawn, or settled, so both remain pending as of the filing date even though the transaction they sought to influence has already closed. That sequencing — litigation outlasting the deal it targeted — is itself typical: once a transaction closes, disclosure-based merger suits frequently wind down through a negotiated "mootness fee" paid to plaintiffs' counsel in exchange for a voluntary dismissal, rather than through a substantive ruling on the merits.

### Practical takeaways

For REIT boards, external managers, and the counsel who shepherd merger proxies through SEC review, the Sunrise/Southern Realty Trust episode is a reminder that even a conventional, board-approved stock-for-stock combination with no obvious controversy will likely draw at least one disclosure-based stockholder suit shortly after the proxy is mailed — the claims arrive almost reflexively and rarely track the deal's actual risk profile. Supplementing proxy disclosures promptly, as Sunrise did, is generally far cheaper than absorbing a delay to the stockholder vote, and it does not require conceding that the original disclosures were deficient. Boards should also expect this litigation to persist past closing: a deal closing on schedule is not the same as the litigation being resolved, and the company's deal timeline and litigation docket should be tracked separately. Finally, external managers who are themselves parties to the merger agreement, as Sunrise Manager LLC was here, should anticipate being drawn into the disclosure narrative even when they are not named as defendants, since their compensation and conflicts are frequently the subject of the alleged omissions.
