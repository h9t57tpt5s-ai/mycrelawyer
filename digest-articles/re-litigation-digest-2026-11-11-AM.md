# Mississippi Appeals Court Upholds Solar Farm and Battery Storage Permits Over Neighbor's Zoning Challenge

### A unanimous Court of Appeals panel affirms Hinds County's approval of a 5,800-acre renewable energy project, rejecting a residents' group's argument that the county board's informal vote could not stand without written findings.

The Mississippi Court of Appeals on November 10, 2026 affirmed Hinds County's approval of conditional use permits for a large-scale solar farm and battery storage facility, rejecting a challenge brought by a neighboring landowner who argued the county Board of Supervisors acted arbitrarily by approving the project without issuing written findings. The decision in *Allison Lauderdale v. Hinds County Mississippi Board of Supervisors, Soul City Storage, LLC and Soul City Energy, LLC* closes out a roughly two-and-a-half-year administrative and judicial fight over roughly 5,800 acres of undeveloped land stretching from Bolton to an area north of the Natchez Trace, and offers a clear marker for how much process a local zoning board actually owes the public before approving a conditional use permit for an energy or industrial project.

The underlying facts trace back to April 2024, when Soul City Storage LLC and Soul City Energy LLC applied to the Hinds County Planning Commission for conditional use permits to build a solar farm and battery storage facility. Soul City held a community open house that drew more than 100 attendees and voluntarily revised its application to address concerns raised there, including relocating construction traffic, agreeing to a road-use agreement, and committing to tree and vegetation management along with wildlife corridors. At a May 2024 public hearing, the Planning Commission nonetheless voted unanimously to deny both permit applications after hearing substantial opposition, including from Allison Lauderdale, who spoke as a self-described "designated spokesperson" for Hinds County residents opposed to the project.

Soul City appealed that denial to the county Board of Supervisors, which held its own public hearing in June 2024. After hearing further testimony on both sides — including a request from the mayor of nearby Raymond for a one-year moratorium on solar projects — a county zoning official confirmed that Soul City's applications complied with the zoning ordinance and that solar farms were a permissible conditional use in the applicable district. The Board approved both applications by a 3-2 vote, memorializing its decision in a written order and accompanying minutes.

Lauderdale appealed that approval to the Hinds County Circuit Court, arguing the Board's decision was invalid because it failed to issue written findings addressing each of the specific factors — ingress and egress, parking, screening, utilities, and general compatibility with adjoining property, among others — that the county's zoning ordinance lists as considerations for a conditional use permit. The circuit court, sitting in its appellate capacity, affirmed in April 2025, finding that Soul City's detailed supplemental applications and site plans were substantive enough to address those factors even without a separate point-by-point written certification, and that the absence of a more formal written decision was not reversible error.

On further appeal, the Mississippi Court of Appeals agreed. Writing for the court, Judge Amy Lassitter St. Pé explained that the zoning ordinance's written-decision requirement does not prescribe any particular form, and does not require the Board to issue a separate certificate making an explicit finding on each individual consideration listed in the ordinance. Because Soul City's supplemental applications, site plans, and responses to community input in the record adequately addressed the ordinance's substantive concerns, the panel held the Board's written order and minutes approving the permits satisfied the ordinance on their face. The court applied Mississippi's well-established "fairly debatable" standard for reviewing local zoning decisions, under which a board's action will not be disturbed so long as substantial evidence supports it on both sides of the question — a deferential standard that leaves little room for an appellate court to second-guess a board's judgment call once the paperwork is in order.

For developers and local governments navigating conditional-use approvals for solar, battery storage, and other large-footprint energy infrastructure, the decision is a useful data point on two fronts. First, it confirms that a county need not draft a bespoke, findings-by-findings written opinion to survive judicial review, so long as the application record itself — site plans, supplemental filings, responses to public comment — speaks to the ordinance's substantive criteria. Second, it illustrates how a developer's voluntary, pre-hearing concessions on traffic routing, buffering, and wildlife protection can do double duty: they help win over a local board even after an initial denial, and they build the very administrative record an appellate court will later point to in rejecting a neighbor's procedural challenge.

**Practical takeaways.** Developers pursuing conditional use permits for solar, battery storage, or similarly contested land uses should treat the application record — not just the board's ultimate vote — as the thing that will actually be reviewed on appeal, since a thin written order can be rescued by a thorough supporting file. Local governments do not need to draft findings tracking every enumerated ordinance factor individually, but should ensure their written order and minutes reference the application materials that address those factors. And opponents of a project should recognize that under Mississippi's "fairly debatable" standard, simply showing that a board's decision was contested is not enough to get it overturned; the challenger must show the decision lacked any substantial evidentiary basis at all.
