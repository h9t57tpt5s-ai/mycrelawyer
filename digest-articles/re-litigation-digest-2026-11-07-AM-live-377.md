# West Virginia Appeals Court Kills Shopping Center's 25-Year Tax Rebate Claim Against City of Logan

### The Intermediate Court of Appeals of West Virginia affirmed dismissal of Fountain Place's breach-of-contract suit over an unpaid business-tax rebate once used to induce the center's original annexation, relying on statutory immunity and estoppel doctrine rather than reaching a live constitutional debt-limit question

The Intermediate Court of Appeals of West Virginia, in an opinion by Judge Ryan White filed November 6, 2026, affirmed the dismissal of a breach-of-contract suit brought by Fountain Place, LLC against the City of Logan and the now-defunct City of Logan Development Authority over a decades-old economic development agreement that had once paid the shopping center's owner more than half of the municipal business taxes collected from its tenants. The ruling, in Fountain Place, LLC v. City of Logan Development Authority, closes off — at least for now — a revenue stream the center's ownership had relied on since the late 1990s, and offers a cautionary lesson for developers who structure long-term incentive deals around a municipal authority's continued existence.

The dispute traces to 1997, when the City of Logan and the Development Authority it had created entered into a Development Agreement with Monterra, the predecessor-in-interest to what is now Fountain Place, LLC, to facilitate construction of a shopping center in an unincorporated part of Logan County. In exchange for Monterra's agreement not to object to the City annexing the property, the City and Development Authority agreed to remit 57% of the business and occupancy ("B&O") taxes collected from businesses operating at the completed shopping center to Monterra and its successors for an initial 20-year term, with an option to renew for an additional ten years. The agreement also exempted the developer from paying its own B&O taxes on construction, rental, and leasing activity at the property — an exemption from which neither Monterra nor Fountain Place has ever deviated.

Monterra built the shopping center, which still operates as the Fountain Place mall, but filed for bankruptcy in 1999; a federal bankruptcy court assigned the Development Agreement's rights, including the tax-rebate payments, to Fountain Place, LLC without objection. The City kept making the 57% payments for years, even after the Development Authority itself dissolved — the record is unclear on whether that happened by deliberate City action or simple attrition as its members died or moved away — with Fountain Place collecting directly from the City instead. In 2016, Fountain Place formally exercised its option to renew the agreement through 2027, and the City did not object, continuing to pay until May 2021, when it abruptly concluded it was no longer legally obligated to do so.

Fountain Place sued for breach of contract and declaratory judgment in the Circuit Court of Logan County in August 2023. The City and Development Authority moved to dismiss, and the circuit court granted that motion in July 2025 on three independent grounds: that the Development Agreement created an unconstitutional municipal "indebtedness" under Article X, Section 8 of the West Virginia Constitution because it was never submitted to a public vote; that the City was statutorily immune from any liabilities of the Development Authority, which had dissolved years earlier; and that the City was not estopped from challenging the agreement's lawfulness despite having honored it for more than two decades.

On appeal, the Intermediate Court of Appeals affirmed, but notably declined to resolve the constitutional debt-limit question the circuit court had reached first. Instead, Judge White's opinion rested the affirmance on narrower grounds: the dissolution of the Development Authority under West Virginia's statutory scheme governing such authorities, and the court's estoppel jurisprudence, which the panel found dispositive without need to decide whether the original arrangement amounted to unconstitutional municipal debt. The West Virginia Attorney General's office filed an amicus brief supporting the City and Development Authority, underscoring the state's own interest in how courts treat long-running municipal incentive commitments that were never put to voters.

Shopping center affiliates Fountain Place Realty, LLC and Namdar DG Realty, LLC were also named as respondents in the circuit court proceedings and on appeal, reflecting the property's ties to Namdar Realty Group, a frequent acquirer of regional malls nationally that has drawn separate litigation elsewhere over property conditions at centers it controls. The appellate court's ruling does not resolve every claim in the underlying circuit court case; counterclaims the City brought against Fountain Place reportedly remain pending below, and the matter is remanded for further proceedings on those issues.

**Practical takeaways**

- Developers who negotiate long-term municipal incentive payments tied to a dedicated development authority should build in contingencies for what happens if that authority dissolves — courts may treat a municipality's post-dissolution statutory immunity as controlling even where the city itself kept making payments for years afterward.
- A municipality's years of voluntary compliance with an incentive agreement will not necessarily estop it from later challenging that agreement's validity; reliance-based estoppel arguments carry less weight against government defendants than they might against a private counterparty.
- Appellate courts will often resolve a case on the narrowest available statutory or procedural ground, leaving live constitutional questions — here, whether the arrangement violated the state's municipal debt-limit clause — unresolved for a future dispute to test.
- Owners negotiating annexation-linked tax-sharing deals should assume judicial scrutiny may eventually turn on whether the agreement was ever submitted to voters, particularly in states with constitutional debt-limitation provisions that require public approval for municipal indebtedness above a statutory threshold.
