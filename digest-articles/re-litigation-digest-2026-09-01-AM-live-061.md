# Arizona AG Sues Out-of-State Landlords Over Tucson Apartments Left Without AC Through Record Heat

### The suit against Dasmen Residential, Luxford Living, and affiliated ownership entities seeks emergency injunctive relief, restitution, and civil penalties over conditions the state calls years of "intentional neglect"

Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes filed suit on August 7, 2026 in Maricopa County Superior Court against the owners and managers of three Tucson apartment complexes, alleging a multi-year pattern of neglect that left more than 1,000 units without functioning air conditioning through some of the hottest months on record. The case, filed together with an application for a temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction, names Sedona Springs, Sienna Ridge, and Summit Ridge apartment complexes and a cluster of affiliated ownership and management entities — Sedona Springs AZ, Sienna Ridge AZ, Summit Ridge AZ, DMR Sedona Springs AZ, DMR Tucson 1166, Tucson 1166 IG, Dasmen Residential, and Luxford Living — as defendants. For commercial multifamily owners and managers, the suit is a pointed illustration of how far habitability enforcement can escalate when a state attorney general, rather than an individual tenant, brings the claim.

The complaint alleges a pattern of neglect and deception dating back to at least 2023 across the three affiliated properties, which together house more than 1,000 units in Tucson's Pima County. According to the state's filing, tenants this summer struggled to cool their units to 80 degrees Fahrenheit or below — the maximum indoor temperature Pima County's habitability standards require landlords to maintain — with indoor temperatures at times reportedly topping 90 degrees. Roughly half of Sedona Springs has allegedly gone without any central air conditioning since March. The complaint pairs the AC failures with a broader list of alleged defects: chronic lack of hot water, derelict plumbing and frequent leaks, mold growth, and pest infestations, along with allegations that the owners and managers misrepresented the condition of the properties and their responsiveness to tenant complaints.

Because the plaintiff is the state itself, acting through the Attorney General's office rather than an individual tenant or a certified class, the remedies sought go well beyond what a typical habitability lawsuit would pursue. In addition to the temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction filed alongside the complaint, the state is seeking permanent injunctive relief requiring the properties to complete necessary repairs, an order barring the defendants from renting to any additional tenants until the units meet legal habitability standards, and both restitution for affected tenants and civil penalties against the ownership and management entities. That combination — an outright rental freeze until compliance, paired with restitution and penalties — represents meaningfully more exposure than a private habitability suit typically carries, since it targets the properties' ability to generate new leasing revenue at all until the alleged defects are cured.

The Tucson case arrives amid what Arizona officials and consumer advocates describe as an emerging enforcement pattern around extreme indoor heat as a housing-code violation, not merely a comfort issue. As record summer temperatures become a recurring feature across the Southwest, state and local regulators in several jurisdictions have signaled a willingness to treat chronic AC failures with the same seriousness traditionally reserved for structural defects, gas leaks, or water intrusion — categories that have long triggered emergency injunctive relief. The Arizona complaint leans into that framing directly, characterizing indoor heat exposure at the alleged levels as a habitability and, implicitly, a public-health emergency warranting immediate court intervention rather than the ordinary pace of civil litigation.

The identity of the defendants also matters for how the case is likely to unfold. The state's press materials describe the ownership and management entities as out-of-state landlords, a framing that tracks a broader trend of state attorneys general scrutinizing institutional or absentee ownership of workforce and affordable multifamily housing more aggressively than smaller, locally based landlords. Multi-entity ownership structures of the kind alleged here — separate LLCs for each property layered under common management and financial sponsors — are a standard risk-segmentation tool in multifamily real estate, but they do not appear to have deterred the state from naming every layer of the ownership chain as a defendant, nor from seeking relief, such as the rental freeze, that would affect the properties' operations regardless of which specific entity is ultimately found liable.

Practical takeaways: Multifamily owners and managers operating in jurisdictions with maximum-indoor-temperature habitability standards should treat AC system capacity and maintenance budgets as compliance-critical infrastructure, not a discretionary amenity, particularly in markets where summer temperatures increasingly test equipment limits. Deferred maintenance that accumulates across a portfolio — rather than at a single property — invites exactly the kind of pattern-and-practice allegations the state is pressing here, so owners should audit recurring habitability complaints across affiliated properties before a regulator does it for them. Out-of-state or absentee ownership structures should also anticipate that multi-entity layering will not prevent a state attorney general from naming every entity in the chain, and that the remedies available to a government plaintiff — including a freeze on new leasing — can impose immediate operational and revenue consequences well before liability is ever established on the merits.
