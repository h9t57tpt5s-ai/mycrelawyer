# Developer's $1 Billion Data Center Bet Collides With a Small Ohio City's Emergency Moratorium

### Thor Equities' federal suit against Urbana, Ohio tests how far a municipality can go to stop a permitted project it no longer wants

A proposal to build a $1 billion, 460,000-square-foot data center on the edge of Urbana, Ohio has turned into a federal civil rights and zoning fight after the city passed an emergency moratorium and rezoned the developer's own land out from under its pending application. Thor Equities, LLC, the New York-based real estate investment and development firm led by Joseph Sitt, sued the City of Urbana, its city council, and its building and zoning appeals board in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Ohio on June 19, 2026, in a case that has since drawn national attention as one of the clearest test cases yet in the fast-spreading wave of local data-center moratoria.

Thor's "Urbana Technology Hub" was planned for a site near the intersection of Ohio 55 and U.S. 68, close to an existing Rittal manufacturing plant, on land zoned M-1 for manufacturing use — a classification that, at the time, permitted data centers as of right. According to Thor's complaint and subsequent press coverage, the company submitted a complete site plan for the roughly 460,000-square-foot facility in February 2026 and says it had already spent $19.6 million of its own money on preliminary engineering, environmental, and site-development work in reliance on the existing zoning.

Urbana's city council responded in March 2026 by adopting a 12-month emergency moratorium on new data center development, timed, Thor alleges, specifically to intercept its pending application before it could be approved under the zoning rules then in effect. The council followed that with a more permanent fix: it rezoned Thor's parcel as unsuitable for data center use and amended the M-1 manufacturing zoning code to strip data centers of their previously permitted status in that district. Thor's lawsuit alleges that both the moratorium and the city's handling of its administrative appeal violated Ohio law, Urbana's own city charter, the zoning code as it stood when Thor applied, and federal constitutional due process protections. A legal analysis of the complaint, authored by counsel at Vorys, frames the core argument as a claim that the city's about-face broke "fundamental protections" the Constitution affords to a property owner with a vested, pending application.

The dispute has not stayed confined to the courtroom. Public meetings in Urbana have drawn large, vocal crowds, with some residents calling for the removal of city administration officials and others pushing to keep the zoning change in place. A recall effort against Urbana's mayor, Bill Bean, has also gathered momentum, reflecting how politically charged the underlying land-use question has become at the local level even as the legal fight over vested rights and procedural fairness plays out in federal court.

### A fast-moving national pattern, with Urbana as a sharper test case

Urbana is far from alone. Developers have filed similar suits against moratoria or sudden zoning reversals in Wixom, Michigan; Cave City, Kentucky; DeKalb County, Georgia; and Chatham County, North Carolina, among other jurisdictions racing to pump the brakes on gigawatt-scale data center campuses amid resident concerns over noise, water use, and strain on the electrical grid. What distinguishes Urbana's case is the sequence: Thor says it filed a complete application before the moratorium existed, which sharpens the vested-rights question that has proven decisive in several of the other disputes, and the city's subsequent, permanent rezoning of Thor's specific parcel — rather than a facially neutral, districtwide pause — gives Thor a stronger argument that the city targeted its project rather than adopting a generally applicable policy.

For commercial developers, the case is a reminder that a site plan submission and zoning compliance at the time of filing do not guarantee insulation from a municipality's later policy reversal, particularly once a project becomes locally controversial. Vested-rights protection varies significantly by state, and in jurisdictions like Ohio, the strength of a developer's position often depends on how far along the permitting and investment process had progressed, and how specifically later legislative or administrative action targeted the developer's own property, before any change in the rules.

For municipalities, Urbana's experience illustrates the legal exposure that comes with a hurried response to resident opposition. A moratorium adopted to buy time for a broader zoning study is generally defensible; one that a plaintiff can plausibly characterize, as Thor does here, as timed and targeted to defeat one specific, already-pending application invites exactly the kind of due-process and equal-protection claims now before the federal court in the Southern District of Ohio. The case remains in its early stages, with no ruling yet on the merits, but its outcome is likely to be closely watched by both data center developers and local governments drafting their own moratoria elsewhere in the country.

### Practical takeaways

- Developers pursuing large, potentially controversial projects such as data centers should document the precise date and completeness of site plan and permit submissions, since the strength of a vested-rights claim against a later moratorium or rezoning often turns on exactly what had been filed, and what had been spent, before the policy changed.
- Municipalities considering an emergency moratorium in response to a specific pending application, rather than as a generally applicable pause adopted before any application is filed, should expect that timing and targeting will become central issues in any resulting litigation.
- Both developers and local governments should treat the growing wave of data-center moratorium litigation nationally — in Ohio, Michigan, Kentucky, Georgia, and North Carolina alike — as a single, fast-developing body of precedent on vested rights and procedural due process in the land-use context, not a series of isolated local disputes.
