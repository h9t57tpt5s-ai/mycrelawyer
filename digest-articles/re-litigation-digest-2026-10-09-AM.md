# Five-Year Wait Sinks Rival Hotelier's Bid to Block Hilton-Branded Project in Puerto Rico

### A Bayamón court's dismissal of DBR Dorado Owner LLC's suit against a 171-room Tapestry Collection by Hilton project offers a clean illustration of how laches and delay can doom even a facially plausible land-use challenge.

A Puerto Rico trial court has dismissed, with prejudice, a lawsuit that sought to halt construction of a 171-room hotel in Dorado on the grounds that the project violated land-use restrictions governing the surrounding development. The ruling, entered by the Bayamón Court of First Instance and reported October 6, 2026, closes out — at least at the trial level — a dispute that pitted one commercial property owner against a competing hotel developer over restrictive conditions recorded against a shared parcel of land, and offers a pointed reminder to commercial landowners that the right to enforce land-use restrictions can expire long before the underlying document does.

The plaintiff, DBR Dorado Owner LLC, which holds the land underlying the Dorado Beach Resort complex, sued to stop construction of the Casa Dorado project — a 171-room hotel operating under Hilton's Tapestry Collection brand and developed by The Dawn Hotel at Dorado LLC. DBR's theory was straightforward: in a notice to its own residents and in its pleadings, the company asserted that land-use restrictions applicable to the site barred lodging facilities altogether and capped any structure at two stories. A 171-room hotel, DBR argued, exceeded both limits, and DBR said it had never been asked to evaluate or approve the project's plans.

The Dawn, for its part, framed the stakes in stark financial terms, telling the court that DBR's claim put a $48 million investment and hundreds of construction and hospitality jobs at risk. The company asked the Bayamón court to dismiss the suit outright rather than litigate the merits of the land-use restrictions, and the court agreed — but not on the substantive question of what the restrictions actually permit. Instead, the court's dismissal turned on timing: the judge found that DBR had been aware of the Casa Dorado development since 2020, yet waited more than five years to bring a legal challenge to it. By the time DBR sued, in the court's assessment, the delay had itself become fatal to the claim, and the dismissal was entered with prejudice — foreclosing DBR from refiling the same claim. Press accounts indicate the hotel, which was already substantially built out by the time of the ruling, remains on track to open during Thanksgiving week in 2027.

For commercial real estate owners and developers, the decision is a useful illustration of how delay doctrines — laches, waiver, and analogous timeliness defenses — operate independently of the underlying merits of a land-use or restrictive-covenant dispute. A restriction can be real, specific, and even plainly violated on its face, and a challenge to it can still fail if the party seeking to enforce it sat on its rights while the opposing party invested capital and broke ground in reliance on the status quo. Courts across jurisdictions have shown increasing willingness to treat years of inaction — particularly inaction that spans from pre-construction planning through substantial completion — as its own form of waiver, regardless of whether the underlying zoning or covenant argument might otherwise have had legs.

The case also underscores the practical reality that in resort and hospitality markets, where adjacent landowners frequently operate under decades-old master declarations, covenants, or land-use agreements negotiated long before any particular project was contemplated, disputes over what those documents actually permit are common — and often arise only after a competing project is well underway. A developer who believes its plans may be vulnerable to a neighboring owner's restrictive-covenant claim has an incentive to put that owner on notice early and invite objection, precisely so that a belated challenge can later be met with the same delay defense that prevailed here. Conversely, an owner holding enforcement rights under restrictive covenants cannot assume those rights remain available indefinitely once a competing project becomes public; the Dorado ruling suggests that watching construction proceed for years without formal legal action can itself extinguish the claim.

Practical takeaways: Commercial landowners who hold enforcement rights under restrictive covenants, deed restrictions, or land-use agreements should treat any competing development they believe violates those terms as requiring prompt action — sending objection notices and, if necessary, filing suit well before a project reaches substantial completion, since years of visible inaction can support a laches-based dismissal regardless of the covenant's substantive merits. Developers facing a neighbor with plausible but dormant restrictive rights should consider documenting the neighbor's actual or constructive knowledge of the project from its earliest stages, since that record becomes the foundation of a delay defense if litigation follows later. And parties on both sides of recorded land-use restrictions in resort, mixed-use, or multi-owner developments should periodically review whether those restrictions remain enforceable against current development plans, rather than assuming decades-old documents will be interpreted and enforced exactly as written whenever a dispute eventually arises.
