# A Decade In, Singer Island Condo Owners Push for Punitive Damages Over Disputed Resort Fees

### The Palm Beach Marriott Singer Island's condominium association is asking a Florida circuit court to freeze a disputed daily fee and award punitive damages, as a trial approaches in a fee dispute with hotel operator Urgo Hotels that traces back more than a decade

More than ten years after it first went to court over what it calls duplicative resort fees, the condominium association for the Palm Beach Marriott Singer Island has escalated its long-running dispute with the property's commercial operator, Urgo Hotels LP, moving in August for punitive damages and a temporary stay of the disputed charges as the case heads toward trial in early 2027. The litigation, pending in the Circuit Court for the Fifteenth Judicial Circuit in and for Palm Beach County, Florida, centers on a roughly $45-per-occupied-day administrative fee that the association says duplicates costs already covered by owners' regular quarterly assessments. For owners and operators of hotel-condominium properties nationally, a category where a commercial operator and an association of individual unit owners must coexist under a shared operating structure for decades, the case is a study in how an unresolved fee dispute can compound into tens of millions of dollars in claimed exposure.

The underlying property, a 239-unit resort at 3800 North Ocean Drive in Riviera Beach, operates under a structure common to hotel-condominiums: individual owners hold title to their units, while a commercial operator manages day-to-day hotel operations, shared amenities, and a rental program, funded through a combination of ownership assessments and operator-imposed fees. That structure has generated friction almost since the property's early years. The association first sued Urgo Hotels in 2010 over shared-facility maintenance charges, a dispute the parties settled in 2013. According to the association's current filings, the disputed per-occupied-day administrative fee at issue in this case dates to that same year, and by 2015 the association had returned to court, alleging that owners continued to be charged for shared-facility upkeep without any assurance that the fee bore a reasonable relationship to documented, actual expenses.

More than a decade of litigation later, the case remains unresolved, and the dollar figures at stake have grown substantially. Attorneys Stevan Pardo and Greg Weiss, representing the condominium association, filed motions in August 2026 seeking two forms of relief: a temporary injunction staying collection of the disputed fee while the underlying claims proceed to trial, and an amendment adding a claim for punitive damages tied to the operator's continued collection of the challenged charge. Pardo has stated that damages tied to fees collected since 2013 could exceed $45 million, a figure that reflects both the scale of a per-occupied-day fee compounded across a quarter-million square feet of hotel-condominium operations and the length of time the dispute has remained unresolved. Urgo Hotels has opposed the association's characterization of the case, and its counsel has argued in related proceedings that portions of the current dispute are foreclosed by the 2013 settlement of the association's earlier suit, while also contending that support for the association's position among individual owners is not as unified as the litigation suggests.

The dispute illustrates a structural tension inherent to hotel-condominium ownership that extends well beyond this one property. Unlike a conventional condominium, where an elected board answers directly and exclusively to unit owners, a hotel-condominium's operator typically retains significant contractual authority over shared facilities and amenity operations under a long-term management or operating agreement negotiated at the property's inception, often decades before the specific fee disputes that later arise. When an operator sets an administrative or amenity fee under broad contractual language, and owners later come to believe that fee has drifted from actual documented costs into something closer to an independent profit center, the resulting dispute can be difficult to resolve short of litigation, because the operator's authority to set and collect the fee in the first instance is rarely in serious question, only whether the specific amount charged is reasonable and adequately substantiated.

The Singer Island case also highlights how long this category of dispute can persist once it takes root. A fee dispute that began as a relatively contained disagreement over shared-facility maintenance charges in 2010 has, through a settlement, a second lawsuit, and now a punitive-damages motion, spanned sixteen years and, on the association's account, tens of millions of dollars in cumulative exposure. That trajectory matters for any hotel-condominium operator managing a comparable fee structure elsewhere: an unresolved dispute over whether a recurring fee is adequately tied to documented costs does not go away on its own, and each year it remains unresolved adds to the damages base a court may eventually be asked to evaluate.

Owners of hotel-condominium interests, and the associations that represent them, should insist on regular, itemized accounting from operators for any fee billed on a per-occupied-day or similarly formulaic basis, since the gap between a contractually permitted fee and a fee substantiated by actual costs is precisely where this kind of dispute takes root. Operators managing shared-facility or administrative fees under decades-old agreements should audit those charges periodically against current documented costs rather than relying on original contract language alone, because a formula that seemed reasonable at a property's opening can look very different, and far more exposed to challenge, after a decade of unadjusted collection. And both sides should recognize that punitive-damages exposure tends to accumulate specifically where a dispute festers rather than resolves, making early, well-documented resolution materially cheaper than years of compounding litigation risk.
