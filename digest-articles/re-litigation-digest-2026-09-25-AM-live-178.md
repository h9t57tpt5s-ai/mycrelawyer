# Cohen's $204 Million Countersuit Keeps the Largest UCC Foreclosure in CRE History Alive

### After paying off a $187 million personal guaranty judgment, developer Charles Cohen is pressing a commercial-reasonableness challenge to Fortress's landmark 2024 auction of his real estate portfolio — a fight with lasting implications for how lenders conduct UCC Article 9 foreclosure sales of CRE-holding entities.

Nearly two years after Fortress Investment Group forced a sale of developer Charles S. Cohen's real estate holdings in what was widely described as the largest Uniform Commercial Code foreclosure auction on record, the dispute between the two sides is still very much alive. Cohen has satisfied the roughly $187 million personal-guaranty judgment a New York court entered against him in 2024 and that the Appellate Division, First Department affirmed in February 2025. But rather than closing the book on the matter, Cohen is now pressing forward with his own claim against Fortress seeking approximately $204 million in damages, arguing that the auction which stripped him of his properties was never conducted in the "commercially reasonable" manner Article 9 of the UCC requires. Fortress, for its part, has indicated it is not finished with Cohen and intends to continue pursuing its own remaining claims against him.

The underlying dispute traces to September 2022, when Fortress Credit Corp., an affiliate of Fortress Investment Group, extended roughly $534 million in financing to a group of limited liability companies affiliated with Cohen Brothers Realty Corp., the real estate firm Cohen has run for decades. The loan was secured by interests in a portfolio that included Tower 57, the Cohen-owned office building at 135 East 57th Street in Manhattan that houses the D&D Building design showrooms, along with hospitality and design-center assets in Dania Beach, Florida, near Fort Lauderdale. Cohen personally guaranteed the debt. When the loan went into default, Fortress moved to enforce its rights not through a conventional mortgage foreclosure but through a UCC Article 9 foreclosure on the pledged equity interests in the borrower entities — a mechanism increasingly used by CRE lenders because it can move faster than judicial foreclosure in many states.

Fortress's first attempt to schedule the auction drew a formal objection from Cohen, and a New York court agreed that the proposed sale process fell short of Article 9's commercial-reasonableness standard, forcing Fortress to revise its marketing plan. A second, court-sanctioned auction went forward on November 8, 2024. It drew a single bidder: Fortress itself, which submitted credit bids that included roughly $148.7 million for equity in the core portfolio, plus separate bids of $76 million for the Design Center of the Americas and $30 million for a Le Méridien hotel in Dania Beach. Separately, in October 2024, the Supreme Court of the State of New York, New York County granted Fortress summary judgment in lieu of complaint on Cohen's personal guaranty, entering judgment for roughly $187 million; the First Department affirmed that ruling in February 2025, and Cohen paid the debt in full in June 2026.

It is that November 2024 auction Cohen is now challenging on the merits. His suit alleges that Fortress structured the sale — including its choice of marketing approach, brokers, and timeline — in a way that depressed the price paid for his properties, arguing the portfolio sold for roughly $150 million against what he contends was an internal Fortress valuation closer to $235 million, an $85 million gap he says reflects a process that fell short of Article 9's requirements a second time. Fortress has signaled it disputes that characterization and intends to keep litigating its own remaining claims against Cohen, which its counsel has suggested could produce additional judgments. Cohen, in turn, is expected to move to dismiss those remaining claims while pressing his own damages counterclaim.

For CRE lenders and their workout counsel, the case is a live reminder that satisfying a guaranty judgment does not necessarily end a borrower's ability to attack the foreclosure process that produced it. Article 9's commercial-reasonableness standard applies to every element of a disposition — marketing method, timing, and choice of intermediary among them — and a borrower who loses the underlying collateral fight can still recover damages later if a court finds the sale process itself was deficient, even years after the auction closed and even where the lender was the only bidder. The size of the numbers involved, a $534 million original loan, a $187 million guaranty judgment, and now a $204 million damages claim, also illustrates how disputes over a UCC foreclosure's fairness can rival the original credit exposure in magnitude, making the process discipline around any equity-pledge foreclosure a genuine litigation-risk issue rather than a formality.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Lenders enforcing UCC Article 9 remedies against CRE-holding entities should document, contemporaneously, the commercial reasonableness of every element of a disposition (marketing scope, broker selection, timeline, and how any internal valuation compares to the eventual sale price), since a borrower can revisit that record in a separate damages action even after paying off a related guaranty judgment.
- Borrowers who lose collateral in a UCC sale but believe the process was flawed retain an independent damages remedy under Article 9 separate from, and not foreclosed by, satisfying a personal guaranty judgment tied to the same credit facility.
- A single-bidder auction where the secured lender is also the buyer draws particular scrutiny on commercial reasonableness, and parties on both sides should expect courts to examine the gap between internal valuations and sale price closely.
- Workout counsel should treat the conclusion of a guaranty-judgment collection effort as a milestone, not necessarily a resolution, when a parallel commercial-reasonableness challenge to the underlying foreclosure remains pending.
