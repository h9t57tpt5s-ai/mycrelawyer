# Sixth Circuit: A Deed in Lieu of Foreclosure Can Wipe Out More Than the Debt

### Appeals court affirms that a commercial lender's settlement and deed-in-lieu arrangement extinguished its own insurable mortgagee interest, dooming its suit against the borrower's insurers

A commercial real estate lender that resolves a defaulted loan through a deed in lieu of foreclosure needs to think carefully about what else it is giving up besides the debt. On November 2, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit affirmed summary judgment against Sinmier, LLC, a commercial real estate lender, in its suit against the insurance broker and insurers of its former borrower, holding that Sinmier's own settlement agreement had already extinguished the only legal interest that could have supported its claims. The decision, *Sinmier, LLC v. Everest Indemnity Insurance Co.*, is a cautionary tale for lenders who negotiate workouts without accounting for how those workouts affect collateral insurance disputes already in progress.

The case traces back to 2018, when Sinmier loaned Vintro Hotels and Resorts Ohio LLC seven million dollars to purchase the Maui Sands Resort and Waterpark, a hotel and indoor waterpark on Lake Erie in Sandusky, Ohio. Sinmier recorded an open-end mortgage and security agreement to secure the loan. Vintro was contractually obligated to insure the property, and it used an insurance broker, Bankers Insurance (later Bearing Insurance Group), to place coverage through two insurers, Everest Indemnity Insurance Company and EverSports & Entertainment Insurance. The insurance placement did not go smoothly: an initial Everest policy was cancelled when the purchase closing was delayed, and when Everest reissued the policy for the later closing date, it listed Sinmier only as an additional insured under the general liability portion of the policy, not as a mortgagee entitled to protection of its loan interest.

That gap mattered. A brutal Ohio winter hit the property hard: a frozen pipe burst and flooded the resort, two rooftop HVAC units froze and then caught fire, and additional wind and cold-related damage piled up. Vintro submitted an insurance claim to Everest in January 2019, and the parties eventually settled that claim for a combined $1,125,000. But because the policy did not protect Sinmier's mortgagee interest, none of that recovery flowed to the lender. When Vintro subsequently defaulted on its loan, Sinmier sued Vintro directly in March 2019 for breach of contract, fraud, and intentional misrepresentation, seeking to foreclose on the resort. The parties settled that suit in October 2019: Vintro conveyed Maui Sands to Sinmier by deed in lieu of foreclosure, with the deed expressly "considered payment in full of Vintro's monetary obligations under the Note or Mortgage," and Vintro's owner agreed to pay Sinmier an additional $627,375 over nine months, secured by a consent judgment.

Having resolved its claim against the borrower, Sinmier turned to the insurance side of the loss, suing Bankers, Everest, and EverSports as a third-party beneficiary of the insurance placement, alleging breach of contract, negligence, and related claims, all premised on its position as the property's mortgagee. The U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Ohio granted summary judgment to each defendant, and the Sixth Circuit, in an opinion by Judge Jeffrey S. Sutton, affirmed.

The panel's reasoning turned on a well-established, if easily overlooked, principle of insurance law: a mortgagee's insurable interest in a property exists only to secure payment of the underlying debt, and it shrinks or disappears entirely once that debt is reduced or satisfied. Citing Ohio case law dating back to the nineteenth century, as well as parallel authority from Alabama, Alaska, Connecticut, New Jersey, and leading insurance treatises, the court explained that when a mortgagee forecloses, or accepts a deed in lieu of foreclosure, and the underlying debt is deemed paid in full as a result, the mortgagee's insurable interest in the property is extinguished along with it. Applying that rule here, the court held that Sinmier's own settlement agreement, which expressly treated the deed in lieu of foreclosure as full payment of Vintro's obligations, ended Sinmier's insurable mortgagee interest the moment it was signed. Because every one of Sinmier's claims against the broker and insurers depended on the continued existence of that mortgagee interest, and Sinmier held no other interest in the property as an owner or investor, all of its claims failed as a matter of law. The court rejected Sinmier's argument that the settlement agreement and the deed created ambiguous or conflicting terms about whether the mortgage survived, finding the documents consistent in treating the debt as satisfied.

For commercial real estate lenders, the decision is a reminder that the mechanics of how a defaulted loan gets resolved can have consequences well beyond the four corners of that settlement. A lender that structures a workout, consent judgment, or deed in lieu of foreclosure as full satisfaction of the debt should expect courts to treat that characterization literally in any related insurance-coverage dispute, even one the lender views as entirely separate from the borrower settlement. Lenders pursuing both a borrower settlement and a parallel insurance claim arising from the same loss should sequence and draft those resolutions carefully, because resolving one can inadvertently extinguish the legal basis for the other.

Practical takeaways for commercial lenders and their counsel: first, before finalizing a deed in lieu of foreclosure or any settlement that recites full satisfaction of the underlying debt, confirm whether any insurance claims tied to the same collateral remain unresolved, and if so, consider carve-out language preserving the lender's insurable interest or assigning insurance claim rights expressly. Second, verify at the time a policy is placed or renewed that the lender is actually listed as a mortgagee or loss payee under the property coverage section of the policy, not merely as an additional insured under general liability coverage, since the two protect very different interests. Third, remember that an insurable interest is not fixed at origination; it rises and falls with the outstanding debt, so any partial paydown, principal reduction, or restructuring should prompt a fresh look at what interest remains to be insured. Finally, where a loss and a loan default occur close in time, resolve the insurance claim before, or at least in coordination with, any settlement of the underlying loan default, rather than treating them as sequential, unrelated matters.
