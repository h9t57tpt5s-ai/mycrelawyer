# Texas Appeals Court Affirms Summary Judgment for Shopping Center Landlord in Restaurant Tenant's Repair and Sublease Dispute

### A Houston Court of Appeals ruling underscores how lease allocation clauses, written-notice requirements, and the Deceptive Trade Practices Act's high-dollar exemption can combine to defeat a commercial tenant's claims even after years of litigation.

The Court of Appeals for the First District of Texas, sitting in Houston, has affirmed a trial court's summary judgment in favor of a Harwin-area shopping center ownership group, rejecting a restaurant tenant's claims for breach of lease, fraud, violations of the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA), and quantum meruit. The October 16, 2026 memorandum opinion in a dispute arising from a Richmond Avenue restaurant space in Houston offers a compact lesson in how commercial lease drafting, procedural notice requirements, and a little-known DTPA carve-out can combine to foreclose a tenant's claims no matter how sympathetic the underlying facts appear.

### What Happened

The dispute traces back to October 2019, when the tenant executed a commercial lease, using a standard Texas Association of Realtors form, for a restaurant space in a Harwin-area shopping center in Houston. The five-year lease divided maintenance responsibilities in a common but consequential way: the landlord bore responsibility for roof repairs, conditioned on the tenant giving written notice of any needed repair, while the tenant bore responsibility for grounds maintenance, parking areas, plumbing and drainage systems, and the HVAC system, including a mandatory maintenance contract. The lease also barred the tenant from assigning or subletting without the landlord's written consent, while expressly permitting the landlord to assign the lease to a successor owner.

According to the tenant's pleadings, the landlord's interest was assigned to a successor ownership entity in January 2021, a transfer the tenant said it was never properly notified of. The tenant alleged that it spent hundreds of thousands of dollars maintaining and improving the premises -- plumbing, electrical, air conditioning, and roof repairs among them -- expecting reimbursement that never came. Separately, the tenant claimed the landlord's principal had orally promised to consent to a sublease and sale of the restaurant business; relying on that promise, the tenant located a buyer and, as a condition of obtaining consent, executed an estoppel certificate representing that the landlord was not in default and that the tenant had accepted the premises as delivered. The landlord ultimately refused to consent to the sublease, and after the tenant notified its vendors and contractors that the landlord would not pay for the improvements, a materialmen's lien was recorded against the property.

### The Court's Reasoning

The trial court granted the landlord's hybrid no-evidence and traditional motion for summary judgment on every claim, and awarded the landlord $23,580 in trial-level attorney's fees and $15,000 in appellate fees under the lease's prevailing-party clause. The Court of Appeals affirmed across the board.

On the breach-of-lease claims, the court held the lease's plain terms doomed the tenant's position: electrical, plumbing, and HVAC maintenance were the tenant's own contractual responsibility, not the landlord's, and the roof-repair claim failed because the record contained no evidence the tenant ever gave the landlord the written notice the lease required as a condition of any roof-repair obligation. The court also rejected the tenant's argument that the landlord had an implied duty to repair the roof before move-in, pointing to the lease's as-is acceptance clause and its express disclaimer of any warranty as to the property's condition.

The alleged oral promise to consent to a sublease fared no better. Because the proposed sublease term exceeded one year, the court held it was subject to the statute of frauds and had to be in writing to be enforceable -- an oral assurance, however reasonably relied upon, could not bind the landlord.

Perhaps the opinion's most instructive holding concerns the DTPA claim. Texas law exempts from DTPA coverage any transaction relating to the same nonresidential project where the consumer's total consideration exceeds $500,000. Here, the lease's sixty-month term at $8,700 in base monthly rent totaled $522,000 -- just over the statutory threshold -- which the trial court cited as an independent basis for dismissing the DTPA claim, a holding the tenant did not meaningfully contest on appeal.

### Why It Matters

For commercial landlords and tenants alike, the decision is a reminder that lease maintenance-allocation clauses are read literally and enforced strictly, and that a tenant seeking to hold a landlord to a repair obligation must satisfy any written-notice precondition the lease imposes, not merely show that a repair was objectively needed. The DTPA ruling is a quieter but equally important point: parties structuring longer-term leases with base rent calculations that land just above or below the $500,000 nonresidential-transaction threshold should recognize that the figure can determine whether a tenant retains a consumer-protection cause of action at all, independent of the lease's own terms. And the case is a cautionary tale about estoppel certificates signed mid-relationship -- the tenant's own representation that the landlord was not in default, offered to grease the wheels of a sublease that never happened, became one more obstacle to its later claims that the landlord had, in fact, defaulted.

### Practical Takeaways

Commercial tenants should treat a lease's written-notice requirements for landlord repair obligations as a strict condition precedent, not a formality, and should document notice in writing even when a landlord's awareness of a defect seems obvious. Parties negotiating base rent and lease term for Texas nonresidential leases should calculate total lease consideration against the DTPA's $500,000 exemption threshold early, since crossing that line removes a significant tenant remedy. Any oral promise affecting lease rights that will run more than a year -- consent to a sublease chief among them -- should be reduced to a written amendment before a tenant relies on it or forgoes other options. And tenants asked to sign an estoppel certificate as a condition of a landlord's cooperation should understand that its representations can be used against them if the relationship later sours, regardless of the certificate's original purpose.
