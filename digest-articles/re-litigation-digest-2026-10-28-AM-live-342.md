# Washington Appeals Court Revives Affordable-Housing Owner's Regulatory-Takings Claim Against Seattle's Rent Ordinances

### GRE Downtowner LLC's inverse condemnation suit survives dismissal, giving multifamily owners a new published decision on when a city's cumulative tenant-protection laws can amount to a compensable taking

A published decision from Washington's Court of Appeals, Division One, is giving multifamily owners and their lenders a fresh data point in the long-running fight over how far a city's rent and eviction regulations can go before they cross into a compensable taking of property. On October 26, 2026, the court reversed a trial court's dismissal of GRE Downtowner LLC's inverse condemnation claims against the City of Seattle, holding that the company pleaded enough facts about the cumulative financial impact of a series of tenant-protection ordinances to let its case proceed past the pleading stage.

**The property and the business model.** GRE Downtowner owns and operates Addison on Fourth, an affordable housing complex of 254 studio and one-bedroom units, 46 artist studios, and four commercial spaces near downtown Seattle. GRE Downtowner bought the property in 2012 for $12.5 million and put in another $26.5 million to qualify it for the federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credit program, with financing support from the Washington State Housing Finance Commission, Citigroup as bond underwriter, and Freddie Mac and East West Bank as credit enhancers. The project stabilized by 2015, and from 2015 through 2018 it ran a positive annual cash flow, collecting between roughly 90 and 94 percent of its maximum allowable rental income each year under HUD's income-based rent caps.

**The ordinances and the alleged financial collapse.** According to the complaint, that changed as Seattle's City Council adopted a succession of tenant-protection measures beginning in 2018: the Fair Chance Housing Ordinance barring criminal-history-based tenant screening and eviction; a 2019 Roommate Ordinance extending occupancy rights to a tenant's family members and other residents; a Winter Eviction Ban; a COVID-19 eviction moratorium running from March 2020 to February 2022; and, in September 2021, both an extension of the minimum rent-increase notice period from 60 to 180 days and a new Economic Displacement Relocation Assistance ordinance requiring landlords to pay relocation costs to tenants who leave after a rent increase of 10 percent or more. GRE Downtowner alleges its maximum-rent collection rate fell from 87 percent in 2019 to just 44.1 percent in 2023, producing annual losses of $683,353 in 2020, $980,043 in 2021, $1.8 million in 2022, and $2.7 million in 2023, and leaving the property, in the company's telling, functionally valueless on an income-approach appraisal.

**The litigation.** GRE Downtowner sued the City in King County Superior Court in October 2024, asserting inverse condemnation claims for both "partial" and "per se" regulatory takings under article I, section 16 of the Washington Constitution, the state's analog to the Fifth Amendment's takings clause. The City moved to dismiss under Civil Rule 12(b)(6), and the trial court granted the motion in February 2025. After the Washington Supreme Court declined to pull the appeal up to itself, Division One took up the case and, in an opinion authored by Judge David S. Mann, reversed. Applying Washington's comparatively lenient 12(b)(6) standard — which, unlike the federal "plausibility" test, asks only whether the plaintiff could prove any set of facts consistent with the complaint that would justify relief — the court held that GRE Downtowner's allegations about the ordinances' cumulative economic effect were legally sufficient to proceed, under the regulatory-takings framework Washington courts borrow from the U.S. Supreme Court's decision in Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc. and the state's own 2023 ruling in Washington Food Industry Association v. City of Seattle.

**Why it matters.** The ruling does not decide whether Seattle's ordinances actually worked a taking of GRE Downtowner's property — that question returns to the trial court — but it is a significant procedural win for owners arguing that a city's regulations should be judged by their combined effect rather than evaluated one ordinance at a time. For multifamily owners operating under rent control, eviction-restriction, or relocation-assistance regimes in Seattle and other jurisdictions considering similar rules, the decision is a reminder that a regulatory-takings theory premised on the cumulative weight of multiple overlapping ordinances can survive a motion to dismiss even without a single ordinance that, standing alone, destroys the property's value. It also underscores the evidentiary path such a claim requires: GRE Downtowner's complaint tied specific dollar losses and collection-rate declines to specific ordinances and specific years, the kind of granular financial record that regulatory-takings plaintiffs will need if the case is to survive summary judgment on remand.

**Practical takeaways.** Owners of affordable or rent-regulated multifamily housing facing new local tenant-protection measures should document the property's financial performance before and after each new ordinance takes effect, since a takings claim premised on cumulative regulatory impact depends on being able to isolate and quantify that impact year by year. Lenders and investors underwriting LIHTC and other subsidized housing deals in jurisdictions with an active regulatory agenda should factor in the risk that a stack of individually modest tenant-protection rules can, over time, support a viable takings claim — and should watch how GRE Downtowner's case develops on remand, since it will test how far Washington courts are willing to let that theory go on the merits. And municipalities drafting new landlord-tenant regulations should take note that courts are willing to look past the loss-of-use test ordinance by ordinance and instead ask whether the regulatory scheme, taken as a whole, has left an owner with no economically viable use of the property.
