# Goldman-Affiliated CMBS Trust Takes Pittsburgh's Historic Chamber of Commerce Building After No-Bid Foreclosure Auction

### A sheriff's sale that drew no outside bidders shows how little competitive interest remains for mid-tier Pittsburgh office towers, even landmark ones with deep ties to the city's business history.

The lender behind a defaulted mortgage on 411 Seventh Avenue in downtown Pittsburgh — long known as the Chamber of Commerce Building — walked away from last week's foreclosure auction as the property's new owner after no competing bidder showed up to challenge its own credit bid. According to a Pittsburgh Business Times report on the Monday, November 2, 2026 sheriff's sale, the plaintiff, a Goldman Sachs-affiliated commercial mortgage-backed securities (CMBS) trust, submitted an opening bid of just $1.4 million. With no other party willing to bid above that figure, the trust took title to the roughly 16-story, Beaux-Arts-style tower from its prior owner, 411 Seventh Avenue Associates LP.

The outcome is a small but telling data point in a now-familiar story for office landlords and CMBS investors nationally: a loan secured by an aging, mid-sized downtown office building goes into default, the lender forecloses, and when the building finally reaches the auction block, the market's verdict is that almost nobody wants to own it, even at a price that would have seemed implausibly low a decade ago. A $1.4 million winning bid for a 269-foot office tower in a major city's downtown core is a number that, by itself, says more about where buyers currently price lease-up risk, capital-expenditure needs, and financing availability for older office stock than any amount of market commentary could.

### A landmark with a long institutional pedigree

The building has carried the Chamber of Commerce name since its original construction in the early twentieth century for Pittsburgh's own chamber of commerce, and it has functioned as conventional multi-tenant office space for decades since, most recently holding ENERGY STAR certification and counting at least one longtime law-firm tenant among its roster. Commercial listing data shows the building has carried meaningful vacancy in recent years, including large blocks of contiguous space on multiple floors — the kind of overhang that has made older, amenity-light office buildings in secondary markets especially difficult to refinance as their loans mature.

That dynamic is precisely what appears to have played out here. 411 Seventh Avenue Associates LP's loan, held in a CMBS trust organized with Goldman Sachs' involvement, went into default, and the trust pursued a mortgage foreclosure through Allegheny County's court system, culminating in the November 2 monthly sheriff's sale. Pennsylvania foreclosure procedure allows a lender to credit-bid the amount it is owed (or a strategically chosen lower figure) rather than pay cash, and when no third party outbids that credit bid, the lender becomes the record owner by operation of the sheriff's deed. That is exactly what happened here: the trust's $1.4 million bid went unchallenged, and ownership passed by operation of the foreclosure sale rather than through any negotiated transaction.

### Why it matters beyond one Pittsburgh address

For CMBS special servicers, bondholders, and workout counsel, a no-bid sheriff's sale is as much a signal as a court ruling. It confirms, in the most market-tested way possible, that the collateral's current fair value is at or below whatever credit bid the lender was willing to make — information that matters for loss-severity assumptions across an entire CMBS pool, not just the single loan involved. When a trust is left holding title after an auction nobody else wanted to enter, it inherits not just the asset but every carrying cost that comes with it: property taxes, insurance, deferred maintenance, leasing commissions to backfill vacancy, and the ordinary burdens of being a landlord in a market where tenant demand for comparable space has not kept pace with available supply.

For owners and lenders on similarly positioned buildings — mid-rise, pre-World War II office towers in secondary downtown markets, carrying partial vacancy and limited recent capital investment — the result is a useful valuation data point independent of any appraisal. A sophisticated institutional seller effectively validated $1.4 million, net of whatever debt it extinguished in the process, as the clearing price for this asset today. That is a figure workout teams evaluating comparable collateral elsewhere should treat as real market evidence, not a worst-case hypothetical, when they are deciding whether to pursue a deed-in-lieu, a consensual sale, or a contested foreclosure of their own.

### Practical takeaways

Lenders and special servicers holding loans against older, partially vacant office buildings in secondary downtown markets should treat a no-bid outcome at a comparable property's sheriff's sale as real pricing evidence for their own loss-severity and reserve calculations, not as an isolated anomaly. Borrowers and sponsors facing a looming maturity default on similar assets should recognize that a credit-bid foreclosure, rather than a negotiated sale, may be the most likely outcome once a lender concludes no cash buyer will clear the debt, and should weigh a deed-in-lieu or early workout accordingly. And buyers and investors tracking distressed office product should watch for a wave of lender-owned assets emerging from exactly this kind of auction, since trusts that end up as reluctant landlords are frequently motivated, near-term sellers once they have stabilized a property's basic occupancy and operations.
