# Fourth Circuit Revives Convenience-Store Slip-and-Fall, Narrowing the "Open and Obvious" Defense

### A published reversal from Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson III reminds commercial property owners that a visibly wet surface is not automatically a complete defense to a premises-liability claim

Commercial property owners and the convenience-store, gas-station, and retail operators that occupy their sites received a reminder in May that the "open and obvious" defense to a premises-liability claim is narrower than many defendants assume, particularly where an employee actively creates the hazardous condition. In *Lewis v. Circle K Stores Inc.*, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit reversed a grant of summary judgment for the store operator, holding that a jury — not a judge on summary judgment — should decide whether a wet, freshly cleaned parking-lot surface was truly "open and obvious" and whether the plaintiff needed expert testimony to prove what caused his fall.

The case arose after Jonathan Lewis was injured leaving a Circle K convenience store in South Carolina. A store employee had just cleaned a painted line in the parking lot using water and a powdered concrete cleaner. According to the complaint, no warning signs, safety cones, or hazard markers were placed around the freshly cleaned area, despite the store's own internal policies calling for such warnings, and Lewis slipped on the wet surface as he exited, severely injuring his right leg. Lewis sued Circle K for premises liability, and the case was removed to the U.S. District Court for the District of South Carolina, where it proceeded through discovery before the store moved for summary judgment.

The district court sided with Circle K on three independent grounds: that the wet, visibly cleaned surface was an "open and obvious" hazard that eliminated any duty to warn under South Carolina premises-liability law; that Circle K could not have reasonably foreseen the specific risk that materialized; and that Lewis had failed to offer expert testimony establishing that the wet surface, as opposed to some other cause, was what made him fall. On appeal, a Fourth Circuit panel — Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson III writing, joined by Judges Julius N. Richardson and Toby Heytens — rejected all three grounds and reversed. The panel held that genuine disputes of material fact remained as to whether the hazard was truly "open and obvious" under the circumstances, given that a store employee had just created the condition and no warning was posted despite an internal policy calling for one, and that a jury could reasonably conclude the danger was not as apparent to a departing customer as the district court assumed. The panel likewise held that expert testimony was not categorically required to establish causation on facts of this kind, where a jury could rely on ordinary experience and the timing of the cleaning to connect the wet surface to the fall without a scientific or technical opinion.

The ruling matters well beyond one convenience store because "open and obvious" is one of the most heavily litigated defenses in commercial premises-liability practice, and it is frequently resolved at summary judgment precisely because it can end a case before trial exposure ever materializes. The Fourth Circuit's opinion signals that a defendant cannot rely on the mere visibility of a wet or otherwise altered surface to automatically satisfy the defense, particularly where the defendant's own employee created the condition and the defendant's own internal safety protocols called for a warning that was not given. That combination — self-created hazard plus a documented, unfollowed internal policy — gave the appellate panel enough to conclude a reasonable jury could see the situation differently than the trial judge did on a cold summary-judgment record. The opinion also lowers, at least in some factual patterns, the bar plaintiffs must clear on causation, holding that a jury does not always need a retained expert to connect a freshly cleaned, wet surface to a fall that occurred moments later.

For commercial landlords, retail and convenience-store operators, and their insurers, the case is a useful trigger to revisit internal maintenance and cleaning protocols, since the existence of a written policy requiring warning signs or cones became evidence used against the defendant once it was not followed. The case will now return to the district court in South Carolina for further proceedings, most likely trial, rather than resolving the underlying liability question itself — the Fourth Circuit's ruling only restores Lewis's ability to have a jury hear the case.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Internal safety policies requiring warning signs, cones, or barriers after cleaning or maintenance work create a documentary record that can be used against an operator if the policy is not actually followed in a given instance — audit compliance, not just the existence of the policy.
- "Open and obvious" is not a categorical shield; courts increasingly ask whether the specific circumstances (who created the hazard, when, and whether a warning should reasonably have accompanied it) made the danger genuinely apparent to a visitor in the moment.
- Property owners and tenant-operators should not assume expert testimony is required to defeat a premises-liability claim's causation element on straightforward slip-and-fall facts; the absence of an expert is not automatically fatal to a plaintiff's case.
- Because commercial leases often allocate maintenance responsibility and indemnification obligations between landlord and operating tenant, owners should confirm that lease language and insurance coverage account for liability arising from a tenant's own maintenance activities, not just structural conditions of the property itself.
