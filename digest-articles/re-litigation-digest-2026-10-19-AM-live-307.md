# ACORE Capital Declares Default on GPI Companies' Vacant West End Office Campus in West L.A.

**The San Francisco lender says more than $80 million is owed on the 235,000-square-foot creative office project built on the former Westside Pavilion Macy's site, which has sat empty since completion.**

*Prepared as a legal news update for commercial real estate owners, developers, and lenders.*

A creative office campus built on the bones of a shuttered Macy's department store in West Los Angeles has landed in default, with its lender, ACORE Capital, declaring that developer GPI Companies owes more than $80 million on the project. Public records reviewed by The Real Deal and WestsideToday show ACORE recorded a notice of default against GPI in September 2026 after the developer failed to make required payments on the debt secured by West End, a 235,000-square-foot office campus at 10730 West Pico Boulevard.

The notice marks a sharp reversal for a project that, on paper, should have benefited from one of the Westside's more creative adaptive-reuse stories. GPI purchased the shuttered Macy's store and an adjoining parking structure at the former Westside Pavilion mall in an off-market deal in 2017, then set about converting the site into modern office space aimed at entertainment, media, and technology tenants chasing creative-office product on the Westside. Construction financing came from ACORE Capital, which reporting indicates provided a $120 million construction loan in 2019; the parties are reported to have entered into a separate $97.6 million refinancing agreement in 2024 once the building was substantially complete. The two outlets' accounts differ on precisely how that 2024 debt relates to the original construction note, but both agree the campus, completed in 2022, has apparently never signed an anchor tenant and appears to sit entirely vacant today, based on marketing materials and site observations cited in the reporting.

For a project whose marketing has long centered on move-in-ready creative space, zero recorded leasing activity four years after completion is itself a significant data point, independent of the default. Neither GPI's co-founders and managing partners, Cliff Goldstein and Drew Planting, nor representatives for ACORE Capital responded to requests for comment reported in the coverage, leaving the borrower's plan for curing the default, if any, unclear as of this writing.

**What the default notice sets in motion**

California is a non-judicial foreclosure state, meaning ACORE does not need to file a lawsuit to foreclose; recording a notice of default under California Civil Code Section 2924 is itself the formal first step in the trustee's-sale process that can culminate in a public auction of the property, typically several months after the notice is recorded, absent a cure, forbearance agreement, or negotiated workout. That procedural posture distinguishes this matter from judicial foreclosure actions filed in court, but the legal and practical stakes for the borrower are the same: a lender moving to take control of a stalled, income-producing asset by exercising its security interest rather than its litigation rights.

GPI's position here is a familiar one in the current cycle for sponsors who financed speculative office conversions during the low-rate years preceding 2022: the underlying real estate thesis (high-quality creative office space on a well-located Westside site) ran into a leasing market that, post-pandemic, has been unusually slow to absorb new office product regardless of quality, particularly product built on spec without a signed anchor. When a construction or bridge loan matures, or a refinancing comes due, against an asset still searching for its first tenant, the borrower has little operating income to service the debt and correspondingly little leverage in workout negotiations, especially once a lender concludes that even a well-built asset isn't worth propping up without a credible leasing timeline.

**Practical takeaways**

For commercial real estate sponsors with vacant or under-leased spec office product nearing a loan maturity or refinancing deadline, the West End default is a reminder that a strong physical asset and a sound redevelopment concept do not, by themselves, generate the operating cash flow a lender needs to see before extending further patience; lenders increasingly appear willing to move to a recorded notice of default relatively quickly once a maturity or payment default occurs, rather than carrying a non-performing, unleased asset indefinitely. Borrowers facing a similar gap between completion and lease-up should engage lenders proactively, well before a scheduled payment is missed, documenting a credible leasing pipeline or alternative use plan, since post-default negotiating leverage in a non-judicial foreclosure state is materially weaker once the statutory trustee's-sale clock has started running. Lenders and special servicers evaluating workouts on comparable vacant creative-office collateral should treat this default, along with the still-unresolved question of how the 2019 construction loan and 2024 refinancing actually relate to one another, as a cautionary data point on the risk of financing speculative office product without a leasing covenant or pre-leasing threshold built into the loan documents.
