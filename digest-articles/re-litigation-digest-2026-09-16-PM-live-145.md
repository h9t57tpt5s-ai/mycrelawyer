# SEC Fraud Settlement Over $86M Hotel Fund Spawns Investor Class Action Against Non-Traded REIT Sponsor

### A Dallas-based hospitality fund manager that just settled SEC fraud charges over misrepresented hotel holdings now faces a parallel securities class action from the same investors it allegedly misled

Commercial real estate sponsors that raise capital from retail investors through non-traded REIT structures got a pointed reminder this week of how quickly a regulatory settlement can become a second, private lawsuit. On September 16, 2026, the plaintiffs' firm Federman & Sherwood filed a securities class action, captioned *Proven Business Skills v. Phoenix American Hospitality, LLC, et al.*, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas against Phoenix American Hospitality, LLC ("PAH"), its two affiliated non-traded REIT funds, American Hospitality Properties REIT, Inc. and American Hospitality Properties REIT II, Inc., and PAH's president, William Lee "Perch" Nelson. The filing arrives roughly three months after the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission announced a settled enforcement action against the same defendants over nearly identical conduct, illustrating how a government settlement — even one reached without an admission of wrongdoing — can hand plaintiffs' counsel a roadmap for a follow-on private suit.

The SEC's case, filed and settled on June 4, 2026 in the same Northern District of Texas courthouse, alleged that PAH and Nelson raised approximately $86 million from more than 2,000 retail investors across the two REIT funds between March 2022 and July 2024. According to the SEC's complaint, PAH told investors that one of the funds owned "as many as 11 hotels," when in reality the fund held only a preferred equity interest in a single hotel property until January 2024. The SEC's litigation release states that PAH first disclosed receipt of a related SEC subpoena in July 2024, roughly two years after the alleged misstatements began. Without admitting or denying the SEC's allegations, PAH and Nelson agreed to final judgments requiring PAH to pay a $591,127 civil penalty and Nelson to pay a $118,225 civil penalty, along with a five-year bar on Nelson serving as an officer or director of a public company, subject to court approval.

The newly filed private class action tracks the SEC's factual findings closely but seeks a different remedy: compensatory damages for the class of investors who purchased interests in the funds during the March 1, 2022 through July 31, 2024 class period. The complaint asserts claims under Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and SEC Rule 10b-5, which prohibit material misstatements and omissions made in connection with the purchase or sale of a security, along with a "control person" claim under Section 20(a) against Nelson and other individual officers for allegedly enabling the underlying misstatements. Investors have until November 16, 2026 to petition the court to serve as lead plaintiff, the procedural mechanism under the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act by which the class's most significant shareholder typically directs the litigation on behalf of the putative class.

For sponsors and asset managers who raise capital through non-traded REIT vehicles or similar retail-facing fund structures, the sequencing here is the real lesson. A regulatory settlement is frequently treated internally as the end of a matter — the penalty is paid, the officer bar is accepted, and the company moves on without admitting liability. But the "without admitting or denying" language that makes a settlement more palatable to sign does nothing to prevent a private plaintiffs' firm from using the SEC's own factual findings, publicly filed in a federal complaint, as the factual backbone of a follow-on securities class action. Because the SEC's complaint already establishes a detailed, sourced narrative of what was allegedly represented to investors and what the underlying assets actually were, the private plaintiffs' bar can move quickly — in this instance, filing suit roughly fifteen weeks after the SEC settlement was announced — without having to conduct the initial fact investigation itself.

The case also underscores a structural exposure specific to non-traded REITs marketed to retail investors: unlike exchange-listed REITs, whose share prices provide investors and courts with an independent, market-tested benchmark for measuring alleged harm, non-traded REIT interests typically have no public trading market, which can complicate both damages calculations and an investor's ability to discover a misrepresentation before committing additional capital. Sponsors of these vehicles should expect that any SEC inquiry into asset-level representations — hotel counts, occupancy figures, portfolio composition, or similar operational claims made in offering materials — carries meaningfully elevated follow-on litigation risk precisely because those representations are usually the primary, and sometimes only, information retail investors have to evaluate the investment.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A settled SEC enforcement action is not necessarily the end of the exposure; it can function as a factual predicate for a subsequent private securities class action, particularly where the settlement includes a detailed factual complaint.
- Non-traded REIT sponsors and their counsel should treat offering-document representations about portfolio composition (asset counts, ownership interests, occupancy) as high-risk statements warranting the same diligence given to audited financials, since these are frequently the statements later cited in both regulatory and private litigation.
- Individual officers named in a "control person" claim under Section 20(a) face personal exposure distinct from the entity's liability, and an SEC-negotiated officer/director bar does not resolve that separate private-litigation risk.
- Counsel advising sponsors facing SEC scrutiny should factor the likely timeline and shape of follow-on private litigation into settlement negotiations, since the settlement's own factual findings will likely become public and usable by plaintiffs' firms almost immediately.
