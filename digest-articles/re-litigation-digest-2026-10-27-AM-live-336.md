# Illinois Appeals Court: Landlord Must Prove Fair Market Value to Recover for a Removed Billboard, Even After Winning on Conversion

### A nearly 70-year-old land lease for two roadside poster panels produced a lesson for commercial landlords: proving a tenant took your property is only half the case

A tenant that dismantles and hauls away structures from a commercial landlord's property has committed conversion — but that finding, standing alone, does not put a dollar figure in the landlord's pocket. The Illinois Appellate Court's Fourth District made that point on October 26, 2026, in *Michelmann Steel Construction Co. v. Link Media Midwest, LLC*, 2026 IL App (4th) 251262-U, reversing a trial court's finding that a landlord lost its conversion claim outright, but limiting the landlord's recovery to nominal damages because it never put on evidence of the removed property's fair market value.

**What happened.** Michelmann Steel Construction Company, an Illinois corporation, has owned a lot on 24th Street in Quincy, Illinois since at least the 1950s. Beginning with a 1956 agreement with Quincy Outdoor Advertising Co., Michelmann repeatedly leased space on the lot for outdoor billboard structures to a succession of tenants, each lease using largely the same preprinted form: an original one-year term followed by automatic year-to-year renewals for up to five additional years, cut off only by 30 days' written notice. The lease at the center of this case, signed May 1, 1987 with Outdoor Graphics, Inc., set rent at $400 a year with 10% annual increases and let the tenant keep ownership of its billboard structures, removable "at any time up to a reasonable time after the termination of this lease."

Link Media Midwest, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company, later became Outdoor Graphics' assignee under that lease. On April 19, 2019, Link Media had the billboard structures on the lot dismantled and removed. Michelmann sued in the Adams County circuit court for conversion (Count I), tortious interference with prospective business relationships (Count II), and breach of contract for unpaid rent (Count III). After a bench trial conducted entirely on documentary evidence, the trial court — the Honorable Scott Douglas Larson presiding — ruled against Michelmann on the conversion and tortious-interference counts but for Michelmann on the rent claim. Both sides appealed: Link Media challenged the rent ruling, and Michelmann cross-appealed the rejection of its conversion and interference claims.

**What the appellate court held.** Writing for a unanimous panel (Justices DeArmond and Grischow concurring), Justice Peter C. Cavanagh agreed with the circuit court on most points but sided with Michelmann on conversion. The court's holding rests on four propositions. First, unless a lease gives a tenant a defined grace period after termination to remove its trade fixtures and personal property, the tenant must remove that property while it remains in possession as a tenant — whether during the original term, a renewal, or a holdover tenancy — or the property becomes the landlord's. Second, Michelmann did prove conversion, since Link Media removed structures that, under that rule, had become Michelmann's property, but Michelmann presented no evidence of the billboard structures' fair market value — only, at most, evidence of replacement cost — and without authority establishing replacement value as a proper damages measure, it was entitled to nominal damages only. Third, Michelmann forfeited its tortious-interference claim by failing to support it with a developed, authority-backed argument on appeal. Fourth, because Michelmann had elected to treat Link Media as a holdover tenant rather than a trespasser, a new lease arose on the same terms as the 1987 lease, including its year-to-year renewal structure, which supported the rent judgment Link Media had separately challenged.

The court reversed the judgment against Michelmann on the conversion count, remanded with directions to enter judgment for Michelmann on that count and award nominal damages, and otherwise affirmed the trial court across the board.

**Why it matters for commercial landlords.** The case is a compact, two-sided lesson for anyone negotiating or litigating a ground lease, billboard easement, or similar arrangement where a tenant's structures sit on someone else's land. On the landlord's side, a lease's holdover provisions matter long after signing: Michelmann prevailed on its rent claim largely because its decades-old lease language kept renewing the deal on the same terms even after the named tenant disappeared through a chain of assignments. On the removal side, the decision is a warning that winning the liability fight over a tenant's unauthorized removal of fixtures is not the same as collecting real money for it — a landlord who wants more than a token damages award needs proof of fair market value in hand, whether from comparable sales, replacement-cost testimony tied to depreciation, or an expert appraisal, not just a statement of what it would cost to rebuild. For tenants and their assignees, the opinion is a reminder that silence in a lease about post-termination removal rights defaults against them: absent an explicit grace period, removal has to happen while possession as a tenant continues, and waiting too long risks converting valuable equipment into the landlord's property by operation of law.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial landlords negotiating billboard, rooftop, ground, or similar structure leases should build in clear post-termination removal windows if they want tenants to retain ownership rights after the lease ends, and should think ahead about how they will prove fair market value — not just replacement cost — if they ever need to pursue a conversion claim over removed equipment. Tenants and assignees taking over legacy leases should calendar removal deadlines carefully, since an ambiguous or silent lease will be read against a tenant that leaves structures behind too long. And parties on either side of a long-running lease relationship should recognize that electing to treat an occupant as a holdover tenant, rather than pursuing eviction, can quietly extend the original lease's terms — including rent escalators — for years beyond what either side may have expected.
