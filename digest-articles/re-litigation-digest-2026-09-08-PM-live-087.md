# Jordache Family's Chicago Hotel Landed in $25.4M Foreclosure Suit as Nakash Debt Troubles Spread Across Three States

### Wilmington Trust's foreclosure complaint over Hotel Lincoln's matured CMBS loan is the third Nakash-family commercial property to face lender action in 2026, underscoring how quickly a single missed loan maturity can cascade into portfolio-wide litigation exposure.

Wilmington Trust National Association filed a $25.4 million foreclosure lawsuit on August 14, 2026 in the Circuit Court of Cook County against Nakash Lincoln LLC and Lincoln 1816 LLC, entities controlled by the Nakash family — founders of the Jordache jeans empire — over the mortgage encumbering Hotel Lincoln, a 184-room boutique property overlooking Lincoln Park Zoo at 1816 North Clark Street in Chicago. The filing is the latest and most concrete step in a distress story that has been building since spring: the loan, securitized into a commercial mortgage-backed securities trust for which Wilmington Trust acts as trustee, matured on March 6, 2026 without repayment, and the debt was transferred to special servicing weeks later. For CMBS investors, hospitality lenders, and any owner watching how quickly a maturity default can escalate into contested litigation, the case is a compact but instructive example of the current office-and-hotel workout cycle playing out in real time.

The property itself has a well-documented pedigree. The Nakash family acquired Hotel Lincoln for $73 million in 2016 from an entity controlled by billionaire developer Neil Bluhm's Walton Street Capital, positioning it as a boutique asset in one of Chicago's most recognizable tourist corridors. A decade later, the $25.4 million loan securing the property has become the flashpoint for a foreclosure complaint that, like most CMBS foreclosure actions, does not allege waste or mismanagement of the asset itself so much as a straightforward failure to satisfy a balloon payment at maturity. Wilmington Trust's complaint seeks the customary CMBS remedies: a judgment of foreclosure, appointment of a receiver to manage the property pending sale, and an award of the outstanding principal, accrued interest, and the trust's costs and attorneys' fees in enforcing the loan documents.

What elevates this filing beyond an isolated maturity default is its place in a broader pattern across the Nakash family's commercial real estate holdings. In New York, Wells Fargo has filed a separate foreclosure complaint in New York State Supreme Court targeting roughly 40,000 square feet of retail space at 633 Third Avenue in Midtown Manhattan, owned by Joe Nakash, over a troubled $32 million loan. In South Florida, the family has defaulted on a $28 million loan tied to the Ocean Drive Breakwater Hotel in Miami Beach. Taken together, the three matters describe a family real estate platform facing simultaneous maturity-default litigation in three separate jurisdictions and across three distinct asset classes — hospitality in Chicago, retail in Manhattan, and hospitality again in Miami Beach — within the same calendar year.

That pattern matters to more than just the Nakash family's balance sheet. It illustrates a dynamic special servicers, CMBS bondholders, and workout counsel are watching closely across the broader hospitality and retail lending markets: borrowers who built diversified regional portfolios during the low-rate years of the last decade are now facing coordinated refinancing gaps as loans reach maturity in a materially higher-rate environment, and a single sponsor's distress can surface as multiple, geographically unconnected foreclosure filings rather than one consolidated proceeding. Because each loan sits in its own securitization or origination silo, with its own trustee, special servicer, and local counsel, there is no structural mechanism forcing a unified resolution — each lender pursues its own remedy in its own court, on its own timeline, often with limited visibility into the borrower's other pending litigation.

For hospitality and retail lenders, the case is a reminder to treat a maturity default not as an isolated credit event but as a potential signal about a sponsor's broader capital position. A borrower facing simultaneous defaults across unrelated assets and lenders is, almost by definition, capital-constrained in a way that a single missed payment does not fully convey, and workout counsel evaluating forbearance or modification terms on any one loan should ask whether the sponsor is managing parallel defaults elsewhere before agreeing to concessions that assume the borrower's distress is confined to the asset at hand. It is also a reminder to loan servicers and CMBS special servicing teams that maturity-default litigation in one market can be a useful early warning indicator worth cross-referencing against a sponsor's other holdings, particularly for family-controlled or closely held portfolios that do not carry public reporting obligations. Finally, for hotel and retail owners generally, the case underscores that boutique, well-located assets are not insulated from the current lending cycle's maturity wall: an irreplaceable location and a strong operating history did not prevent this loan from landing in default, and owners approaching their own loan maturities in the next twelve to twenty-four months should begin refinancing conversations with lenders well before the deadline rather than risk joining the growing docket of 2026 CMBS foreclosure filings.
