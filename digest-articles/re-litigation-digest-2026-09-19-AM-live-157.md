# Vermont's Highest Court Says Short-Term Rental Owners Can't Preempt Enforcement With a Lawsuit of Their Own

### The Vermont Supreme Court's ripeness ruling in 32 Intervale, LLC v. City of Burlington leaves a group of nonowner-occupied rental operators no better off than when their years-long challenge to the city's short-term rental ordinance began — and offers municipalities nationwide a roadmap for defeating preemptive litigation.

The Vermont Supreme Court has affirmed the dismissal of a long-running challenge brought by a group of Burlington short-term rental operators against the city's 2022 ordinance restricting nonowner-occupied rentals, ruling in 32 Intervale, LLC v. City of Burlington (2026 VT 9, decided April 10, 2026, Docket No. 25-AP-248) that the dispute was not yet ripe for judicial review. The decision closes out, at least for now, a multi-year effort by property owners to get ahead of enforcement through a declaratory judgment action rather than waiting for the city to act against them individually — and it offers municipalities elsewhere navigating short-term rental disputes a template for defeating similar preemptive suits.

The case traces back to Burlington's 2022 short-term rental ordinance, which imposed new definitions and requirements on the practice and, notably, prohibited most nonowner-occupied short-term rentals within the city. Seventeen property owners, collectively holding more than sixteen short-term rental units concentrated in Burlington's Old North End, sued the city seeking a declaratory judgment that the ordinance did not apply to their twenty-two units because those uses predated the ordinance and qualified as legal nonconforming uses exempt from the new restrictions. The lead plaintiff, Airbnb host Sam Gardner, brought the suit under his business entity, 32 Intervale LLC.

The litigation has already made one prior trip through Vermont's court system. The owners initially filed their declaratory judgment action in the Civil Division of the Chittenden Superior Court in 2023, which dismissed the case on the ground that Vermont's Environmental Division, not the Civil Division, held exclusive subject matter jurisdiction over the dispute because it concerned the application of a municipal land use regulation. The Vermont Supreme Court affirmed that jurisdictional ruling in a 2025 decision, sending the matter to the Environmental Division. Once there, however, the Environmental Division dismissed the refiled case on an independent ground: that no live controversy existed for it to resolve.

In this second appeal, the Vermont Supreme Court agreed. Because the city had not yet taken enforcement action against any of the seventeen owners' properties, and because resolving whether any given unit qualified as a protected nonconforming use would require individualized factual findings — how long each unit had operated as a rental, under what conditions, and whether operations had lapsed in a way that could forfeit nonconforming status — the court held that the dispute was not ripe. A blanket declaratory ruling covering twenty-two distinct properties, the court reasoned, was not something it could issue in the abstract; the question was inherently property-specific and would only become concrete once the city moved to enforce the ordinance against a particular owner's particular unit.

The ripeness holding leaves the underlying merits question — whether these Old North End rentals in fact qualify as legal nonconforming uses grandfathered against the 2022 ordinance — entirely undecided. For the property owners, that is a mixed result: they avoided an adverse ruling on the merits, but they also gained nothing, and remain exposed to enforcement at any time, at which point they would need to raise their nonconforming-use defense in that individual enforcement proceeding rather than through a preemptive citywide challenge. For Burlington and other municipalities, the decision is a clean win on procedure, confirming that a broad-based preemptive challenge to a rental ordinance's applicability can be defeated on ripeness grounds without the city ever having to litigate the substance of whether any particular property is exempt.

The case sits at the intersection of two trends now playing out in tourist-heavy and housing-constrained municipalities nationwide: aggressive local regulation of short-term rentals, and rental operators' attempts to challenge those regulations before the city acts against them, when litigation posture and legal costs may be more favorable. Vermont's decision signals that, at least under its jurisprudence, courts will generally decline the invitation to referee a rental ordinance's scope in the abstract, preferring instead to wait for a concrete enforcement dispute involving a specific property.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Short-term rental operators considering a preemptive challenge to a new local ordinance should expect ripeness to be a significant hurdle where the challenge would require resolving property-specific factual questions across multiple units, rather than a single, generally applicable legal question.
- Municipalities defending rental ordinances against broad declaratory judgment challenges should consider moving to dismiss on ripeness grounds before reaching the merits, particularly where no enforcement action has yet been taken against the specific plaintiffs.
- Owners relying on a legal nonconforming use defense should maintain contemporaneous documentation of when and how a rental use began and has continued, since that individualized factual record will ultimately have to be developed in an enforcement proceeding rather than resolved wholesale in advance.
- Operators in jurisdictions with new or pending short-term rental restrictions should not assume that a jurisdictional or procedural win, like the one Burlington achieved here, resolves the underlying merits question — it may simply defer the dispute to a later, more individualized proceeding.
