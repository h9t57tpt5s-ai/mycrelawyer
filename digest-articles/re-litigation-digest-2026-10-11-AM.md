# Texas Supreme Court Tightens the Proof Standard for Slip-and-Fall Claims Against Commercial Property Owners

### In *H-E-B, LP v. Peterson*, the state's highest court reaffirms that a plaintiff must tie "how long the hazard was there" evidence to the specific spill that caused the fall — not to a store's maintenance history generally.

Commercial landowners in Texas picked up a significant defense-side precedent this year when the Supreme Court of Texas reversed an appellate ruling that had allowed a customer's slip-and-fall claim against grocery chain H-E-B to proceed to trial on what the court found was the wrong kind of evidence. The decision, *H-E-B, LP v. Peterson*, No. 24-0310, sharpens an already demanding evidentiary bar for premises-liability plaintiffs in Texas and gives retail, office, and other publicly accessible commercial property owners a stronger, citable tool for defeating transient-hazard claims before they ever reach a jury.

The underlying facts were unremarkable, which is part of what makes the ruling useful: a customer, Marissa Peterson, slipped on a puddle of unidentified liquid in a toy aisle at an H-E-B store. She was unable to say how the liquid got there or how long it had been on the floor before she fell — a gap that matters a great deal under Texas premises-liability law, which requires a plaintiff suing a property owner over a transient hazard (as opposed to a hazard the owner created) to prove the owner had actual or constructive knowledge of the condition. Constructive knowledge, in turn, generally requires some evidence of how long the hazard existed, because a property owner cannot reasonably be expected to discover and remedy a spill that appeared moments before an accident.

Unable to produce direct evidence of how long the puddle had been there, Peterson's case survived at the intermediate appellate level on a different theory. The Thirteenth Court of Appeals pointed to evidence of unrelated roof leaks elsewhere in the store as circumstantial support for an inference that H-E-B had general maintenance problems, and held that evidence sufficient to let a jury infer constructive notice of the specific puddle that caused Peterson's fall. H-E-B petitioned the Texas Supreme Court for review, arguing that generalized evidence about a store's overall maintenance record cannot substitute for evidence tied to the specific hazard at issue.

The Supreme Court agreed with H-E-B and reversed. In an opinion issued April 10, 2026, the court held that constructive-notice evidence must be specific to the hazard that actually caused the plaintiff's injury — proof that a store had problems with its roof, its parking lot, or some other unrelated part of the premises does not support an inference that an employee knew or should have known about a particular puddle in a particular aisle. Because Peterson could point to no evidence tying the duration of the spill itself to any period long enough to charge H-E-B with constructive knowledge, the court reinstated the trial court's original summary judgment in H-E-B's favor, ending the case.

For commercial property owners, the significance of the ruling lies less in its facts than in its method. Texas courts have long required "time-liness" evidence in slip-and-fall cases — some basis for estimating how long a hazard existed before the accident — and plaintiffs have periodically tried to satisfy that requirement with looser, more general proof of a defendant's housekeeping or maintenance practices. *Peterson* forecloses that workaround cleanly: evidence of unrelated maintenance issues elsewhere on a property is simply irrelevant to constructive notice of the specific condition that caused a specific fall. That holding will matter well beyond grocery stores. Any commercial landlord, retailer, hospitality operator, or property manager facing a premises-liability claim over a floor hazard, loose flooring, or similar transient condition can now point to *Peterson* for the proposition that a plaintiff's duration evidence has to be particularized, not generalized.

The ruling also reinforces, once again, the practical value of surveillance and incident-documentation systems in defending these claims. Where a property owner can show — through video, inspection logs, or employee testimony — that a specific area was clear shortly before an accident, that evidence directly rebuts any constructive-notice theory, specific or general. *Peterson* simply confirms that plaintiffs cannot avoid that burden by substituting unrelated maintenance history for proof about the hazard actually at issue.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Texas commercial property owners facing a transient-hazard premises claim should scrutinize whether the plaintiff's constructive-notice evidence is actually tied to the specific condition that caused the injury, rather than to the property's general maintenance or repair history — *Peterson* gives a clean basis to move for summary judgment when it is not.
- The decision is a reminder to maintain routine floor-inspection logs and, where feasible, video coverage of high-traffic areas; such records remain the most direct way to rebut a constructive-notice claim regardless of how the plaintiff frames the evidence.
- Counsel defending slip-and-fall suits elsewhere should watch for whether other states' courts cite *Peterson* in addressing similar "generalized maintenance history" theories, since the underlying evidentiary logic is not unique to Texas law.
