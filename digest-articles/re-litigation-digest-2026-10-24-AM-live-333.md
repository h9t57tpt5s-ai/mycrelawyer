# Arizona's New "Fortuity" Test Revives a $2.7M Commercial Property Insurance Claim

### Ninth Circuit reverses summary judgment for an insurer after the Arizona Supreme Court redefines when a property loss is "fortuitous" — and therefore insurable

A commercial building owner's fight to recover $2.7 million for years of water and salt damage is back on, after the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed a federal district court's summary judgment in favor of the owner's property insurer. The October 16, 2026 ruling in *Industrial Park Center LLC v. Great Northern Insurance Co.* turns on a question that matters well beyond this one claim: when is property damage "fortuitous," and therefore covered by an all-risk policy, versus an uninsurable certainty the owner should have expected? The Arizona Supreme Court answered that question for the first time just weeks earlier, at the Ninth Circuit's request, and the new standard it set is now the law any Arizona property owner, and any insurer writing policies there, has to apply.

Industrial Park Center LLC, doing business as Mainspring Capital Group, owns a commercial building in Tempe, Arizona that it leased to a fish-processing tenant and insured under an all-risk commercial property policy issued by Great Northern Insurance Company. Over several years, the building suffered recurring water and salt intrusion. According to court filings, an engineering firm Mainspring retained, MBJ, had recommended specific preventive measures — a waterproof floor coating, a vapor barrier, and additional drainage systems — that were never installed. When Mainspring submitted a claim for roughly $2.7 million in resulting damage, Great Northern denied it, arguing the loss was not "fortuitous": because the damage was reasonably foreseeable and effectively certain to occur without those preventive measures, the insurer argued, it fell outside what an all-risk policy is meant to cover in the first place.

Mainspring sued in Maricopa County Superior Court in 2022; Great Northern removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the District of Arizona on diversity jurisdiction. In July 2024, the district court granted Great Northern summary judgment, applying the Ninth Circuit's existing fortuity test from *Ingenco*, which asks whether a loss was reasonably foreseeable and almost certain to occur. The court found Mainspring's loss met that description and was therefore not fortuitous, and later awarded Great Northern roughly $198,234 in attorneys' fees.

On appeal, the Ninth Circuit concluded Arizona law did not yet resolve what "fortuitous" means in this context, and in late 2025 certified the question to the Arizona Supreme Court: is property damage a fortuitous loss when, based on the insured's knowledge at the time the policy issued, it was reasonably foreseeable that the damage was almost certain to occur absent preventive measures? The Arizona Supreme Court answered on September 1, 2026, in an opinion by Justice Clint Bolick, and rejected the stricter foreseeability-based test the district court had used. Instead, the court adopted a subjective, knowledge-based standard, drawing on the Restatement (First) of Contracts: a loss is fortuitous, and therefore insurable, unless the insured actually knew, at the time the policy was issued, that the damage had already occurred, was already in progress, or was certain to occur. Foreseeability alone, even strong foreseeability, is not enough to strip a loss of coverage.

Applying that newly announced standard, the Ninth Circuit's October 16 memorandum disposition reversed the district court's summary judgment and remanded, reviving Mainspring's breach-of-contract and bad-faith claims against Great Northern for further proceedings. The decision is unpublished and therefore not binding precedent in future Ninth Circuit cases, but the underlying Arizona Supreme Court ruling it applies is now the controlling statement of Arizona insurance law on fortuity, and will govern how Arizona courts and insurers evaluate every comparable claim going forward.

For commercial property owners, the practical stakes are significant. All-risk policies are the backbone of CRE lenders' insurance requirements, and insurers frequently resist claims tied to a known, long-deferred maintenance or design problem by arguing the eventual failure was predictable and therefore not a fortuitous, insurable event. Arizona's new rule narrows that defense considerably: an insurer can no longer defeat coverage merely by showing the loss was statistically likely or even "almost certain" absent repairs. It must show the owner actually knew, when the policy was purchased or renewed, that the damage had already happened, was already underway, or was a sure thing. Owners who have deferred an engineer's or contractor's recommended fix should not assume that deferral forfeits coverage if the predicted failure eventually occurs; conversely, owners should be cautious about what internal reports, engineering assessments, and board materials say about the certainty of an outcome, since those documents will be the first thing an insurer's coverage counsel examines in any future claim. Lenders and asset managers overseeing assets with known deferred-maintenance issues should likewise confirm that insurance placement and claims strategy account for this more owner-favorable fortuity standard, particularly if the asset sits in Arizona or another state that may look to this decision as persuasive authority on an issue many states have not yet resolved.

**Practical takeaways:**
- An insurer's "it was foreseeable" argument against fortuity no longer defeats an Arizona all-risk coverage claim on its own; under the new test, the insurer must show the owner actually knew the damage was certain, already occurring, or already done when the policy was purchased or renewed.
- Owners who defer an engineer's recommended preventive fix should carefully document the basis for that decision, since the record created at the time, not the eventual outcome, is now what fortuity turns on in Arizona.
- Because many states have not yet addressed this exact question, Arizona's new "subjective knowledge at inception" standard is likely to be cited as persuasive authority elsewhere, making this a live issue to track even for owners and insurers outside Arizona.
- Because this disposition is unpublished, it cannot be cited as controlling Ninth Circuit precedent, but the Arizona Supreme Court opinion it applies is binding statewide law and should inform how claims are prepared and litigated in Arizona going forward.
