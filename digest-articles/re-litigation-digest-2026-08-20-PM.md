# Brokers Sue Tribeca Developer Over Unpaid Commissions on Distressed Condo Recapitalization

### Corcoran, Corcoran Sunshine, and Brown Harris Stevens allege Circle F Capital shorted them $450,000 after they agreed to a discount to help the developer through a cash crunch

Three of New York's most prominent residential brokerages — Corcoran, its new-development affiliate Corcoran Sunshine Marketing Group, and Brown Harris Stevens — have sued developer Circle F Capital in Supreme Court, New York County, alleging the firm failed to pay the full commission owed on a December 2025 recapitalization of 1 Park Row, the 23-story mixed-use condominium tower rising in Manhattan's Financial District. The complaint, reported August 18, says Circle F stiffed the brokers on $450,000 of a $1.45 million agreed commission tied to a roughly $94 million sale-and-restructuring transaction, despite the brokers having already agreed to cut their fee to help the developer through what the complaint describes as its "purported cash problems." The brokers are now seeking $3.2 million in damages, along with interest and costs.

The underlying transaction is not, on its face, unusual for a project of 1 Park Row's scale and history. Circle F Capital recapitalized the tower in December 2025, bringing on Grand Rapids, Michigan-based investor Eenhoorn as a new equity partner and securing a $77 million refinancing loan arranged by Walker & Dunlop and PCCP. That kind of capital-stack restructuring — swapping in fresh equity and locking in a new loan to replace maturing or underperforming debt — has become commonplace across New York's condo development sector as 2021- and 2022-vintage construction loans come due into a tighter lending market. What makes the current dispute notable is not the restructuring itself, but what the brokers allege happened to their compensation once it closed.

According to the complaint, Corcoran, Corcoran Sunshine, and Brown Harris Stevens had marketed and sold units at 1 Park Row under a commission arrangement tied to the project's sales and, later, to the recapitalization transaction itself. When Circle F represented that it was facing cash-flow strain, the brokers agreed to reduce the commission they were owed — a concession real estate brokerages extend fairly routinely to preserve a client relationship and keep a deal moving. The complaint alleges that even after accepting that discount, Circle F failed to pay the reduced, already-agreed amount in full, withholding $450,000 of the negotiated $1.45 million fee. The brokers' $3.2 million demand reflects not just the shortfall itself but interest, statutory add-ons, and litigation costs that accumulate once a commission dispute proceeds to a filed lawsuit rather than a negotiated resolution.

For commercial real estate counsel, the case is a useful reminder that fee concessions made in good faith during a sponsor's period of financial distress do not extinguish the underlying payment obligation — they simply reset it. A broker or vendor that accepts a reduced fee to accommodate a developer's cash position is still owed that reduced amount in full, and a subsequent failure to pay the discounted sum can expose the developer to the same breach-of-contract and, depending on the facts, quantum meruit theories it would have faced had no concession been offered at all. Sponsors negotiating fee relief from brokers, general contractors, or consultants during a workout should treat any such concession as a binding modification of the original agreement, not an invitation to renegotiate again once the immediate liquidity pressure passes.

The dispute also lands amid a broader pattern of litigation activity around 1 Park Row specifically. A separate $6 million lawsuit tied to the project was dismissed by a Michigan judge earlier this month, and the entity behind Eenhoorn's investment in the tower was separately accused of fraud in an unrelated April filing. None of those matters appears to involve the same parties or claims as the brokers' commission suit, but taken together they illustrate how a single distressed or recapitalized project can generate multiple, legally distinct fronts of litigation as different counterparties — lenders, equity partners, brokers, and buyers — each press their own claims once a deal shows signs of financial strain.

Circle F Capital had not publicly responded to the brokers' allegations as of this writing.

## Practical takeaways

Developers negotiating fee or price concessions from brokers, contractors, or other transaction counterparties during a liquidity crunch should document any reduced terms in a clear written modification, since the reduced amount remains a binding and separately enforceable obligation. Brokerages and other service providers extending concessions to preserve a client relationship should likewise memorialize the revised terms and build in remedies — interest, acceleration, or a right to revert to the original fee — for a subsequent default on the discounted amount. Owners and lenders evaluating a distressed sponsor's exposure should treat a cluster of unrelated suits tied to a single project as a signal worth investigating collectively, even when the individual claims have nothing to do with one another, since it often reflects a sponsor under sustained financial pressure across multiple counterparty relationships at once.
