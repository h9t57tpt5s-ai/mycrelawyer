# Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac Servicer Accuse Chicago Investor's Portfolio Entities of Multistate Mortgage Fraud

### A widening web of agency-lender foreclosure and fraud suits now spans Illinois, Oklahoma and Connecticut

A Chicago-area multifamily investor's rapid, agency-debt-fueled acquisition spree has collapsed into a widening web of foreclosure and fraud litigation spanning three states. Since June 2026, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's loan servicer, Walker & Dunlop, have filed a series of suits against portfolio entities tied to investor Chaim Bialostozky, alleging that he obtained inflated government-backed loans by artificially driving up purchase prices through same-day, back-to-back property sales — buying buildings and reselling them to his own entities, on paper, at a markup, before using the inflated sale price to justify a larger loan. The pattern matters well beyond one investor's portfolio: it shows federal-agency lenders increasingly treating a single discovered irregularity as grounds to examine, and sue, every other property a common principal controls.

The pattern first surfaced in Chicago, where Fannie Mae sued over a nine-property South Side portfolio bought in 2022 with a loan exceeding $17 million originated through New York-based Arbor Realty Trust, and has since expanded to additional Fannie Mae foreclosure suits in Cook County Circuit Court's Chancery Division targeting other Bialostozky-controlled multifamily holding entities on Chicago's South and West Sides. By a September 22, 2026 report, at least one of those Cook County foreclosure actions had already passed its response deadline without any appearance or answer filed on behalf of the borrower entities, a pattern reporters say has now repeated across nearly every suit in the broader campaign.

Walker & Dunlop, acting on Freddie Mac's behalf, has brought parallel foreclosure and fraud suits outside Illinois: in Tulsa County, Oklahoma, over roughly $4.8 million in loans tied to two apartment buildings, and in Waterbury, Connecticut, over loans secured by two more apartment complexes. Combined with the Chicago-area Fannie Mae suits, reporting puts the total mortgage debt now caught up in default or foreclosure proceedings across the three states at approximately $44 million.

Court filings in the Freddie Mac-backed suits go further than a conventional foreclosure complaint, accusing Bialostozky of executing a "common plan and unlawful fraudulent scheme" across the properties rather than a series of unrelated defaults, and several of the lender filings seek the appointment of an emergency receiver. Walker & Dunlop's filings also allege that poor property management under Bialostozky's control has "greatly diminished the value of the collateral" securing the loans and created a health and safety risk for tenants still living in the buildings while the litigation proceeds.

For multifamily owners and their lenders, the episode is a reminder that agency-backed debt comes with underwriting assumptions — about purchase price, property condition, and borrower representations — that can be unwound years after closing if a lender later concludes those assumptions were manufactured, and that a single discovered irregularity rarely stays contained to one loan or one property once a servicer starts examining the rest of a common principal's portfolio.

### Practical takeaways

- Expect a lender who uncovers an inflated-value or fraud irregularity on one loan to examine every other property the same borrower, guarantor, or principal controls, not just the loan in question.
- Structuring acquisitions through a web of related single-asset or multi-asset LLCs does not insulate the other entities once a servicer begins a pattern investigation; a fraud allegation against one can prompt suits against all of them.
- An unanswered foreclosure complaint — even one of several filed against related entities — can accelerate a receiver's appointment well before any fraud claim is tested on the merits, so borrowers facing a multi-property campaign should not assume they can let individual suits default without consequence to the rest of the portfolio.
- Lenders and servicers financing rapid multifamily acquisitions should treat same-day or back-to-back resale patterns in a loan file as a red flag warranting closer underwriting scrutiny before closing, not just after a default.
