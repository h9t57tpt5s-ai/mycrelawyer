# Lender Moves for Emergency Receiver Over Denver's X Apartments After $170M Loan Default

### A five-year-old, 450-unit luxury complex becomes the latest Denver multifamily asset to face lender-driven receivership, as a maturity default on a nine-figure construction loan lands in state court barely 18 months after the note came due.

New York-based Mack Real Estate Group asked a Colorado state court on September 4 to appoint an emergency, ex parte receiver over X Denver, the 12-story, 450-unit apartment complex at 3100 Inca Street in Denver's Ballpark neighborhood, alleging that the $170 million loan it made against the property has been in default since the note matured in January 2025. The filing, made in Colorado state court, seeks to place day-to-day control of the five-year-old building into the hands of a court-appointed receiver while the underlying debt dispute plays out, a remedy lenders increasingly favor over a slower foreclosure process when a borrower's ability to manage a distressed asset is itself in question.

X Denver opened in 2021, developed by Chicago-based The X Co. as one of two X-branded apartment towers the developer built in Denver's Arapahoe Square and Ballpark submarkets during the city's last major multifamily construction wave. In January 2022, the property's then-owner secured the $170 million loan from Mack Real Estate Group to finance the completed asset, a debt load reflecting the aggressive underwriting many construction and bridge lenders extended to stabilized, newly delivered multifamily properties at the top of that cycle. According to Mack's filing, the loan reached its scheduled maturity in January 2025, and rather than being repaid or refinanced, the note has sat in default for roughly eight months without resolution. The current record ownership of X Denver is not entirely clear from public filings, a common feature of distressed assets that have passed through multiple special-servicing or workout arrangements since a loan's original closing.

The case adds X Denver to a small but growing list of formerly marquee Denver multifamily and office assets now facing lender-driven receivership actions, following a similar filing weeks earlier by U.S. Bank National Association against the ownership of Republic Plaza, Denver's tallest office tower, after a second maturity default on that property's CMBS loan. Both cases illustrate a pattern playing out across gateway and secondary markets alike: lenders confronting a maturity default on a well-located, physically sound asset are increasingly bypassing extended forbearance negotiations in favor of an immediate receivership filing, particularly where a loan has already gone unresolved for months past maturity. An ex parte receivership request, as Mack has made here, asks the court to act before the borrower has had a full opportunity to respond, a procedural posture reserved for situations where a lender can show urgency, such as a risk that rents, security deposits, or other collateral proceeds might be dissipated or mismanaged absent immediate court supervision.

For a 450-unit asset generating substantial monthly rental income, the practical effect of a receiver's appointment is significant well before any final adjudication of the underlying default: a court-appointed receiver typically takes over rent collection, vendor payments, and operating decisions immediately, effectively transferring functional control of the property away from ownership while litigation over the debt itself proceeds on a separate, usually slower track. That makes the receivership motion, rather than any eventual foreclosure judgment, the practical battleground in cases like this one, and borrowers facing an ex parte request typically have only a narrow window to contest the appointment or negotiate an alternative before a receiver is installed.

**Practical takeaways:** Borrowers with loans approaching, or already past, a scheduled maturity date should not assume that a lender's silence in the months immediately following default reflects an intention to negotiate an extension or workout; an extended post-maturity default period can instead be a lender building its record for exactly the kind of ex parte receivership motion filed here. Because an ex parte receivership request can be granted with limited advance notice, ownership entities carrying matured, unresolved CRE debt should have workout counsel and a response strategy in place well before a lender files, not after. And with Denver now producing multiple lender-driven receivership actions against well-known, recently built assets in quick succession, owners and equity investors in the market's multifamily and office towers should treat a maturity default, even on an otherwise physically healthy property, as carrying meaningfully higher and faster litigation risk than it might have a few years ago.
