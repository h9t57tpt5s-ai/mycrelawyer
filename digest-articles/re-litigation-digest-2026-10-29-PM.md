# Florida Supreme Court: An Easement's Attorney's-Fee Clause Doesn't Bind the Next Owner

### Answering a certified question from the Eleventh Circuit, the court holds that a prevailing-party fee provision is a personal covenant, not one that runs with the land — leaving successors to easement agreements exposed on fees even when they inherit everything else

On October 29, 2026, the Supreme Court of Florida issued its decision in *RJ's International Trading, LLC v. Crown Castle South, LLC*, No. SC2024-0547, resolving a question that commercial property owners, telecom infrastructure companies, and utilities negotiate around constantly but rarely see tested all the way to a state's highest court: when an easement agreement promises attorney's fees to whichever side wins an enforcement fight, does that promise travel with the land to bind later owners who never signed it? In an opinion authored by Justice Jamie Grosshans, the court held that it does not.

The case reached Tallahassee by an unusual route. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, confronting the issue in a federal appeal, certified the question directly to the Florida Supreme Court rather than guess at how state property law would resolve it: "Under Florida law, when an easement agreement contains a prevailing-party attorney's fee provision, is the fee provision a real covenant such that it runs with the land?"

The underlying dispute traces back more than three decades. Hidden Valley Corporation once owned two adjoining parcels in Miami. It leased one to BellSouth Mobility and granted BellSouth's successors an easement over a portion of the other for telecommunications use, an agreement that included a "Remedies for Breach" clause entitling the prevailing party in any enforcement suit to its attorney's fees and costs. Both properties changed hands repeatedly after that. By the time this case arose, RJ's International Trading, LLC (RJI) owned the servient parcel, while Crown Castle South, LLC held the easement rights through a sublease from BellSouth.

In 2020, Crown Castle excavated beyond the easement's boundaries and installed underground fiber-optic cable on RJI's land. RJI sued in federal court for trespass and breach of the easement agreement, among other claims. The district court granted partial summary judgment for RJI on liability, and after a six-day trial a jury awarded RJI $1 for the easement breach and $40,000 for trespass. Crown Castle did not appeal that judgment. RJI then asked the district court for its attorney's fees under the easement's fee-shifting clause, arguing the provision was a "real covenant" that bound Crown Castle as Hidden Valley's and BellSouth's successor, even though Crown Castle itself never agreed to it.

The Florida Supreme Court rejected that theory. For an obligation to run with the land and bind successors, three conditions must be met: the original parties must have intended successors to be bound, successors must have notice of the covenant, and the covenant must "touch and concern" the land. The court's opinion focused entirely on the third requirement, tracing it back to the 16th-century English case Spencer's Case and through a line of Florida precedent, including the court's own 2022 decision in Hayslip v. U.S. Home Corp., which had found an arbitration provision sufficiently connected to the land to run with it.

The court declined to extend Hayslip's reasoning here. A covenant touches and concerns the land, the court held, when it affects how the land is used, occupied, or enjoyed — not merely when it enhances the land's value or arises out of a transaction involving the land. An attorney's fee provision, the court reasoned, does nothing to govern how either party uses or occupies the property; it only allocates litigation risk between whoever happens to be fighting at the time. That makes it a personal covenant binding Hidden Valley and BellSouth alone, not a real covenant that travels automatically to RJI and Crown Castle as their successors.

### Why It Matters for Commercial Owners, Developers, and Infrastructure Tenants

Easement agreements for cell towers, fiber lines, pipelines, billboards, and utility corridors are everywhere in commercial real estate, and they are frequently negotiated to last far longer than either original party's ownership. This decision tells every lawyer drafting one of those agreements in Florida — and gives persuasive authority to lawyers elsewhere wrestling with the same common-law doctrine — that a fee-shifting clause cannot be assumed to survive a change of ownership on either side of the easement.

### Practical Takeaways

- An attorney's fee provision in an easement, ground lease, or similar instrument should not be relied upon to bind a successor owner or assignee unless the transfer documents expressly require the new party to assume that specific obligation.
- Owners acquiring land burdened or benefited by an existing easement should assume they will bear their own litigation costs in any future enforcement dispute unless they negotiate an express assumption of the fee provision at the time of transfer.
- The "touch and concern" test turns on whether a provision governs the use, occupation, or enjoyment of the land itself — provisions that merely allocate financial or litigation risk, however tightly linked to enforcing the easement, are vulnerable to being classified as merely personal.
- Because the ruling answers a certified federal question, it carries weight beyond this single dispute and is likely to be cited wherever infrastructure and utility easements are contested by parties who are not the original signatories.
