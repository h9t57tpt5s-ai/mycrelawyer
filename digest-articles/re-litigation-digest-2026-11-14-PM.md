# Kentucky Appeals Court Upholds Retail Tenant's Lease-Default Liability, But Vacates Interest and Future-Rent Award

### Ghasem Properties keeps its win on a defaulted 62-month retail lease, but the landlord's own damages calculation gets sent back for a second look

The Court of Appeals of Kentucky has affirmed that a retail tenant and its guarantor are liable for defaulting on a commercial lease, but has vacated the portion of the judgment awarding the landlord 8% prejudgment interest and future rent accruing after the trial court's judgment, sending those two damages components back to the Jefferson Circuit Court for further proceedings. The November 13, 2026 opinion in *Audrey Lynn Arcelli and Ms. Audrey's Crafting Emporium, LLC v. Ghasem Properties, Inc.* is a reminder that an uncontested summary judgment motion can lock in a tenant's liability on appeal — but that a landlord's own damages math still has to trace back to the lease itself.

The case began in February 2023, when Ms. Audrey's Crafting Emporium, LLC signed a 62-month commercial lease with Ghasem Properties, Inc. (GPI) for retail space, with monthly rent and common-area-maintenance payments of $2,585.41 starting in the lease's third month. The same day, Audrey Arcelli personally guaranteed the lease up to $155,124.60. The arrangement held until January 2024, when, according to GPI's complaint, the tenant stopped paying rent. GPI said it mailed a notice of default on January 6, 2024, warning that the lease would terminate in seven days absent payment. The tenant never cured, and GPI pursued a forcible-detainer action in Jefferson District Court; by the time that case was dismissed in April 2024, the tenant had already vacated the space.

GPI then sued in Jefferson Circuit Court, seeking the rent it said it was owed, lost future lease payments, advertising costs to re-let the space, accruing late fees, 8% prejudgment interest, post-judgment interest, and attorney fees. The tenant and Arcelli admitted in their answer that they had signed the lease, defaulted, and stopped paying rent, but disputed the damages GPI claimed. When GPI moved for summary judgment, supported by an affidavit from its president, Reza Ghasem, laying out the default and a principal damages figure of $131,855.91 representing rent through the remainder of the lease term, the tenant and Arcelli did not respond. The circuit court granted the motion on September 30, 2024, entering judgment for $131,855.91 plus 8% prejudgment interest from January 1, 2024, 6% post-judgment interest, and attorney fees and costs — later supplemented by an $8,299.30 fee and cost award.

Only after judgment did the tenant and Arcelli move to vacate under Kentucky's Rule 59.05, arguing for the first time that nothing in the lease entitled GPI to future rent as liquidated damages or to 8% prejudgment interest. The circuit court denied that motion, finding the arguments should have been raised before judgment was entered, not after.

On appeal, the Court of Appeals largely sided with the circuit court on the question of liability: because the tenant and Arcelli never responded to GPI's summary judgment motion, and the motion was supported by a sworn affidavit and the underlying lease and guaranty documents, the appellate panel found no basis to disturb the finding that they breached the lease and were liable under the guaranty. But the court agreed that the specific contractual basis for two components of the damages award — the 8% prejudgment interest rate and future rent accruing after the September 2024 judgment date — had not been established in the record GPI submitted, and vacated those portions, remanding for further proceedings to sort out what the lease actually supports.

For commercial landlords, the case cuts two ways. A tenant who fails to timely contest a well-supported summary judgment motion, including a guarantor relying on a broad personal guaranty, will have a hard time undoing that liability finding later by raising belated objections in a post-judgment motion. But the ruling also signals that appellate courts will look past a landlord's own damages tally to ask whether the lease itself actually authorizes items like prejudgment interest at a stated rate or acceleration of rent for the full remaining term — a landlord cannot assume that liability alone carries every dollar claimed. For both sides, the case underscores the value of making every damages argument, including challenges to a plaintiff's interest and acceleration theories, before judgment is entered rather than waiting for a motion to vacate.

**Practical takeaways:**
- A commercial tenant or guarantor that fails to respond to a well-documented summary judgment motion risks having liability findings affirmed on appeal even if later objections have some merit; damages-specific objections should be raised before judgment, not in a post-judgment motion.
- Landlords seeking prejudgment interest or future-rent acceleration as damages for a lease default should be prepared to show the lease itself — not just an affidavit — supports the specific rate and the remedy, particularly where there is no express liquidated-damages or acceleration clause.
- A personal guaranty capped at a stated dollar amount remains enforceable against the guarantor even where the underlying damages calculation is later revised, so guarantors should pay close attention to how a landlord's claimed principal amount is derived.
