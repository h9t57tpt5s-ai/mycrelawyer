# NSI, A.1 LLC v. City of Springfield: Developer Sues to Block Data Center Moratorium After Relying on the City's Own Zoning Sign-Off

**A Springfield, Missouri developer says the city confirmed his data center use in writing — twice — before adopting a moratorium that would bar it anyway, in a case testing how far a municipality can retreat from its own zoning verifications.**

Developer Trent Overhue, through his company NSI, A.1 LLC, filed a 31-page, six-count petition on September 17, 2026 in the Circuit Court of Greene County, Missouri, seeking to block the City of Springfield from enforcing a newly adopted moratorium on data center development against his own project. The suit is one of the more pointed examples yet of a legal fight playing out across Missouri and other states as AI-driven demand for data center capacity collides with local governments trying to slow down or study projects before approving them — and it turns on a fact pattern that should worry any municipality tempted to use a moratorium to unwind a project it already blessed in writing.

According to the petition, Overhue's company sought and received written zoning verification from the city on two separate occasions confirming that his proposed headquarters office building could include a data-processing center in its basement to support the company's own business operations. That confirmation, the complaint alleges, was consistent with the site's existing planned-development zoning, identified in the pleadings as PD 251. Relying on that written sign-off and on communications from city staff, NSI proceeded through predevelopment review and says it incurred substantial planning, design, professional, and project-development expense before the city "changed course."

The change came on June 29, 2026, when the Springfield City Council adopted a 120-day moratorium on new data center development. NSI's petition argues the moratorium is, in substance, a zoning enactment dressed up as a temporary administrative measure, and that adopting it the way the city did skipped the procedural steps Missouri law requires for an actual zoning change — including the plan commission review and public hearing process that a formal amendment to PD 251 would have had to go through. A separate count alleges the city violated Missouri's Sunshine Law, the state's open-meetings and public-records statute, in the process of adopting the ordinance. NSI is asking the court to declare the moratorium invalid as applied to its project and to enjoin the city from enforcing it.

The Springfield suit does not stand alone. In Benton County, roughly ninety miles away, Overhue and an affiliated entity, NSI6 LLC, spent early September arguing for a temporary restraining order against a separate, county-level data center moratorium adopted August 10, 2026 — a fight that packed a courtroom with roughly 100 spectators and remains before the court. And in Montgomery County, a citizen advocacy group's own challenge to a data-center incentive package recently survived a motion to dismiss in Cole County Circuit Court. Taken together, the three matters make Missouri something of a bellwether for how quickly local moratorium politics around data centers can generate real litigation once developers have money on the line.

For commercial real estate developers generally, the Springfield case is a useful illustration of both the value and the limits of a written zoning verification. Municipal planning staff often issue exactly this kind of confirmation letter early in a project's life, and developers reasonably treat it as a green light to begin spending on design, engineering, and predevelopment work. But a verification letter is not a permit, much less a vested right, and a municipality retains its general police-power authority to regulate land use in the public interest, including through a properly adopted moratorium aimed at studying an emerging use's infrastructure and community impacts. The dispute in Springfield will likely turn less on whether the city had the general power to pause data center development than on whether it followed the right procedural path to exercise that power against a project it had already told the developer was permitted.

That procedural focus is what makes the case worth watching well beyond Missouri. Data centers draw outsized scrutiny over their electricity and water demands, noise, and tax-incentive costs, and local governments nationally are increasingly reaching for moratoria as a stopgap while they write more durable rules. A moratorium adopted through an ordinary council vote, without the hearing and notice procedures a jurisdiction's own zoning code reserves for zoning-type actions, is vulnerable to exactly the kind of "zoning legislation in substance" argument NSI is now making, particularly where a specific developer had already received a project-specific written commitment. Developers who have obtained written zoning verifications and have begun spending in reliance on them should preserve a clear paper trail of that reliance — the verification itself, staff communications, and dated invoices for planning and design work — since that record is exactly what a court will look to in weighing whether a subsequent moratorium can fairly be applied against an already-approved project. Municipalities, for their part, have their own lesson here: routing a de facto zoning change through anything other than the zoning ordinance's own amendment procedure, including public notice under the state's sunshine law, invites the sort of procedural challenge Springfield now faces, regardless of how strong the underlying policy rationale for pausing data center growth might be.
