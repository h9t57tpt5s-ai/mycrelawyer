# Neighbors Sue to Unwind $26 Million Sale-Leaseback Behind West Palm Beach's Second Convention Center Hotel

### Two neighborhood groups say Palm Beach County commissioners approved a 34-part transaction with Related Ross on 90 minutes' notice, violating Florida's open-meetings law

Two West Palm Beach neighborhood associations sued Palm Beach County, the City of West Palm Beach, its Community Redevelopment Agency, the CityPlace Community Development District, and affiliates of developer Related Ross on October 14, 2026, seeking to void the county's approval of a $26 million public financing package for a planned 400-room convention center hotel. The complaint, filed in Florida's Fifteenth Judicial Circuit by the Grandview Heights Neighborhood Association and the West Palm Beach Neighborhood Alliance, does not quarrel with the hotel's design or its zoning entitlements. It instead targets the process: the groups allege that county commissioners voted on a 34-part transaction document running 715 pages without giving the public a meaningful chance to review it first, in violation of Florida's Government in the Sunshine Law.

The hotel at the center of the dispute is an 18-story, roughly 400-room project branded Hilton Curio, planned for 940 South Rosemary Avenue near the Palm Beach County Convention Center and the CityPlace retail district. Related Ross, an affiliate of developer Related Companies, is the project sponsor. On September 29, 2026, the Palm Beach County Commission voted 6-1 — with Commissioner Gregg Weiss, whose district includes the hotel site, casting the lone dissent — to approve a sale-leaseback arrangement in which the county would advance $26 million from its General Fund reserves to a Related Ross affiliate to help fund construction deposits. Under the deal, once the roughly $290 million hotel is completed, the county would acquire it outright and then lease it back to the developer's operating entity for an initial term of 33 years, with an option to extend to as long as 97 years.

The lawsuit's core claim is procedural rather than substantive. According to the complaint and subsequent reporting, the full 715-page transaction package was not made available to the public until 8 a.m. on September 29 — roughly 90 minutes before the commission's meeting began. County staff have defended the notice as adequate, telling commissioners that Florida law requires only 24 hours' notice for such items and that the county's own agenda practices typically provide about five days. The plaintiffs argue that neither 90 minutes nor even the county's usual five-day window gave the public or affected commissioners a realistic opportunity to review a complex, multi-part financing structure before a binding vote, and that action taken on inadequate notice under the Sunshine Law is "void ab initio" — void from the moment it was taken, not merely voidable. The suit asks the court to invalidate the September 29 approval and to pause further construction activity while that question is litigated.

The Sunshine Law claim arrives against a backdrop of sustained neighborhood opposition to the project dating back to earlier this year, when Grandview Heights residents raised concerns about the hotel's scale, traffic, and noise during the site-plan approval process; Related Ross made some design accommodations, including repositioning outdoor speakers away from the neighborhood, which residents have said fall short of addressing their concerns. Reporting has also referenced a separate, earlier challenge to the project's land-use approval itself, suggesting the sale-leaseback financing fight is one front in a broader legal campaign against the hotel rather than an isolated dispute.

For public agencies that rely on public-private financing structures to attract hotel and convention-related development, the case is a reminder that Florida's open-meetings statute polices not just whether a meeting was open to the public, but whether the public had a genuine opportunity to engage with what was being decided. A commission's compliance with a bare statutory minimum — here, 24 hours' notice — does not necessarily immunize a vote from a "reasonable notice" challenge when the underlying documentation is unusually voluminous or was supplied at the last minute. For developers structuring complex sale-leaseback or incentive packages with local governments, the ruling direction in this case will matter well beyond West Palm Beach: large or multi-part transactions that surface for a vote only shortly before the meeting are an increasingly visible target for neighborhood litigants looking to unwind a deal after the fact, and a void-ab-initio finding can put months of pre-construction work and committed public financing back into limbo.

**Practical takeaways:** Developers and public-sector partners negotiating sale-leaseback, incentive, or land-disposition deals with a county or municipality should push for the complete transaction package to be posted publicly well ahead of a scheduled vote — not just the statutory minimum — since last-minute document drops are becoming a favored target for post-approval Sunshine Law or open-meetings challenges nationally. Counsel should build a contemporaneous record showing when materials were actually made available to the public and to the governing body itself, since that timeline, not just the agenda notice date, is likely to be dispositive if a reasonable-notice challenge follows. Because a successful Sunshine Law challenge can render a governmental approval void from inception rather than merely voidable going forward, parties relying on the approval should factor in the risk of having to unwind financing, construction contracts, and vendor commitments entered into in the interim. And where a project already faces organized neighborhood opposition over land use or design, expect that opposition to look for process-based openings — procedural, open-meetings, or notice defects — once substantive zoning challenges are exhausted or appear to be losing traction.
