# Legacy Lease Covenant Becomes a Weapon in Ohio Data Center Fight

### A steel manufacturer's 2018 industrial lease is testing whether Bitdeer's AI/HPC campus in Monroe County can go forward as planned — and a judge has just kept the manufacturer's law firm in the case over Bitdeer's objection.

A restrictive covenant buried in a 2018 industrial lease has become the flashpoint in a fight over one of Ohio's largest announced data center projects. American Heavy Plate Solutions, LLC (AHP), a steel manufacturer that leases 9.9 acres of the 100-acre Hannibal Industrial Park in Monroe County from the Monroe County Port Authority, sued Bitdeer subsidiaries Erie Creek LLC and Whitetail Creek LLC, parent company Bitdeer Inc., and the Port Authority itself on February 2, 2026, in the Monroe County Court of Common Pleas. AHP's complaint seeks declaratory and injunctive relief, arguing that Bitdeer's construction of a high-performance-computing and AI data center campus on adjoining port authority land violates a clause in AHP's own lease. In a September 8, 2026 order, the court handed AHP an early procedural win, denying Bitdeer's bid to disqualify AHP's law firm from the case.

The dispute turns on language common in older industrial park leases but rarely tested in a fight this large: AHP's 2018 lease provides that the Port Authority, as landlord, will not grant "any person or entity any easement, license, lease or similar rights of use or occupancy with respect to any portion of the Hannibal Property that adversely affects Tenant's use and/or occupancy of the Hannibal Property during the Term, without Tenant's prior written consent." AHP contends Bitdeer's data center construction — and the accompanying power, cooling, and site infrastructure a large HPC campus requires — adversely affects its own manufacturing operations at the site, and that the Port Authority never obtained its consent before extending rights to Bitdeer's entities. Bitdeer has said it will "vigorously defend" the suit and has acknowledged that construction and power-delivery timing on the project "may be affected" by the litigation.

Before the underlying covenant dispute could move forward on the merits, it detoured into a fight over AHP's own counsel. Bitdeer moved to disqualify Quinn Emanuel Urquhart & Sullivan, AHP's law firm, arguing a conflict of interest because Quinn Emanuel also represents Bitdeer in a separate, unrelated matter in New York. Monroe County Court of Common Pleas Judge Julie Selmon rejected that bid in a September 8, 2026 order, finding that Bitdeer had signed an advance conflict waiver when it retained separate counsel in the New York matter, and that Quinn Emanuel had put in place an adequate ethical wall between the two engagements. Judge Selmon wrote that removing Quinn Emanuel at this stage "would impose material duplication, delay and substantial expense that would prejudice AHP" — a ruling that keeps AHP's chosen litigation team in place as the restrictive-covenant claims proceed.

For an industry racing to secure power-ready industrial sites, the case is a reminder that speed of construction is not the only constraint on a data center developer's timeline. Advance conflict waivers, once standard boilerplate for large law firms serving repeat institutional clients, are increasingly being tested in exactly this kind of scenario: a firm representing a client in one deal finds itself adverse to that same client in a different, unrelated dispute elsewhere in the country. Judge Selmon's ruling suggests that a properly documented waiver plus a genuine ethical wall can be enough to survive a disqualification challenge, but the fight itself cost both sides time and expense before the actual merits of the lease dispute were ever reached.

The underlying restrictive-covenant question — whether Bitdeer's data center construction actually "adversely affects" AHP's steel manufacturing operations in a way the 2018 lease was meant to prevent — remains unresolved and will need to be litigated on the facts: the nature and degree of interference AHP can show, what the Port Authority knew or approved before extending site rights to Bitdeer, and whether the covenant's broad "adversely affects" language reaches something as significant as a new large-scale co-tenant's power draw and construction footprint rather than only direct physical interference with AHP's leasehold. How Monroe County's court answers those questions will matter well beyond this one site, since the same tension — a legacy industrial or port-authority tenant with old-fashioned exclusivity language, sharing a site with a fast-moving AI infrastructure developer — is likely to recur anywhere data center capacity is being sited on previously industrial or port land.

**Practical takeaways:**
- Restrictive covenants written into older industrial and port-authority ground leases, often boilerplate at the time they were signed, can become a serious obstacle to a later tenant's data center build-out; developers should diligence every existing tenant's lease on a shared industrial site before committing to a construction timeline, not just the landlord's own title and zoning position.
- A landlord that grants new site rights to an incoming tenant without checking existing tenants' consent rights risks becoming a co-defendant in the resulting dispute, as the Monroe County Port Authority now is here.
- Advance conflict waivers between a law firm and a repeat institutional client are not self-executing protection against disqualification fights; firms should maintain a documented, genuinely enforced ethical wall between adverse engagements, since that is what carried the day for AHP's counsel here.
- Data center developers should treat legacy lease review on shared or previously industrial sites as a scheduling risk, not just a legal formality — Bitdeer itself has acknowledged that this litigation may affect its construction and power-delivery timeline.
