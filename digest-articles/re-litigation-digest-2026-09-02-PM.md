# Sarasota County's Live Local Act Showdown: Four Developer Lawsuits, Thousands of Units, One Zoning Question

### A county commission's vote to exclude two zoning districts from Florida's affordable-housing preemption law has drawn four separate lawsuits — and a hearing before a Sarasota circuit judge on whether commissioners can be sued personally.

Sarasota County is now defending against four separate lawsuits brought by developers and a church over its refusal to process multifamily housing applications under Florida's Live Local Act, with more than 4,000 proposed rental units caught in the dispute. The litigation, consolidated in procedural posture though filed as separate actions in the Twelfth Judicial Circuit Court, centers on a narrow but consequential question of statutory interpretation: whether the Live Local Act's mandate that local governments administratively approve qualifying affordable-housing developments on commercial, industrial, or mixed-use land also reaches Sarasota County's "Open Use" and "Residential Estate-1" zoning districts. For commercial developers, REITs, and municipal counsel across Florida — and in the growing number of states experimenting with their own zoning-preemption statutes — the outcome will help define how much room local governments retain to narrow a state affordability mandate through zoning-code interpretation rather than outright refusal.

The Live Local Act, first enacted in 2023 and expanded in 2024, requires Florida municipalities and counties to approve, without a rezoning or comprehensive-plan amendment, multifamily or mixed-income residential developments proposed on land zoned commercial, industrial, or mixed use, so long as at least 40% of the units are reserved as affordable for households earning up to 120% of area median income for a minimum of 30 years. In exchange, qualifying projects receive administrative approval and favorable height and density treatment tied to the tallest and densest development otherwise allowed within a mile of the site. The law was designed to strip local elected boards of the discretion to reject workforce-housing projects outright, and it has become one of the most litigated pieces of land-use legislation in the state since its passage.

Sarasota County's dispute traces to an April 7, 2026 commission meeting, at which commissioners determined that the Live Local Act's list of qualifying zoning categories does not encompass the county's Open Use and Residential Estate-1 districts — a interpretation that, if it stands, would block six of seven Live Local applications then pending before the county. Developers responded with a wave of litigation. SITC Inc., a corporation controlled by Jack Cox, president of Lakewood Ranch-based Halfacre Construction, sued the county and each individual county commissioner over the rejection of a roughly 500-unit apartment proposal spanning multiple 65-foot towers, arguing that the commission's zoning interpretation is legally wrong and that the meeting at which it was adopted violated Florida's open-meetings requirements by failing to give adequate public notice that a policy change of that magnitude was under consideration. A second major plaintiff, D.R. Horton Inc., together with the Cindy L. Smith Revocable Living Trust and individual trustees, is pursuing a related claim tied to a long-contested parcel fronting Raymond Road near the Celery Fields bird sanctuary — a fight that began as a single-family subdivision dispute and has evolved, after Horton walked away from a separate arbitration track, into a Live Local Act claim covering several hundred apartment units on the same land. A church with its own affordable-housing proposal rounds out the plaintiff group.

The county has moved to dismiss each of the complaints, including an effort to have the individual commissioners dismissed from suits that name them in their personal capacities — a defensive posture that matters because SITC's complaint specifically asks the court to hold commissioners personally liable, a request that, if it survives, would expose sitting elected officials to individual exposure for a land-use vote taken in their official capacity. On August 25, 2026, Twelfth Judicial Circuit Judge Hunter W. Carroll heard argument on the county's motion to dismiss the second complaint filed against it; a non-jury civil trial in the lead SITC matter has been set for March 29, 2027, giving both sides more than half a year to develop the record on the zoning-classification question before it reaches a fact-finder.

What makes the case worth watching well beyond Sarasota County's borders is that it is not a challenge to the Live Local Act's validity — no party disputes that the statute preempts local discretion for qualifying zoning categories — but a fight over how a local government can narrow that mandate through the technical mechanics of its own zoning code. If Sarasota's interpretation holds, other Florida counties and cities have a clear playbook for constraining Live Local Act exposure: define or redefine local zoning categories narrowly enough that a project's site never triggers the state mandate in the first place, without ever amending or repealing a zoning ordinance in a way that would itself draw preemption scrutiny. If the developers prevail, the ruling will instead signal that courts will read the Act's "commercial, industrial, or mixed use" trigger broadly and skeptically police last-minute reclassification efforts, particularly where, as SITC alleges here, the policy shift arrived through a meeting-agenda determination rather than a publicly noticed ordinance change.

Practical takeaways: Developers pursuing Live Local Act projects should document, at the time of application, exactly how the underlying parcel is zoned and whether that classification has historically been treated as commercial, industrial, or mixed use by the local government — a shift in interpretation adopted after an application is filed is far more vulnerable to challenge than one baked into the zoning code from the outset. Local governments and their counsel should treat any policy-level reinterpretation of which zoning districts trigger state land-use preemption statutes as substantively equivalent to a zoning-code amendment for open-meetings and public-notice purposes, given how readily plaintiffs are now pairing statutory-interpretation claims with Sunshine Law causes of action. And commissioners and board members voting on Live Local Act applications should understand that plaintiffs are increasingly willing to name individual officials in their personal capacity, making contemporaneous documentation of the legal basis for a zoning determination — not just the policy rationale — an increasingly important part of the record.
