# Ohio Supreme Court Denies Data Center Developer's Bid to Force Lordstown Site-Plan Review

### A yearlong mandamus fight over a $3.6 billion data center campus ends with the state's highest court siding with the village's moratorium, not its repealed outright ban

The Ohio Supreme Court on October 20, 2026 denied a writ of mandamus sought by Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. and BHGH Properties, L.L.C., rejecting the developers' effort to force the Village of Lordstown to process a site-plan application for a large-scale data center under the zoning rules that existed before the village moved to block data centers altogether. The decision, *State ex rel. Bristolville 25 Developer, L.L.C. v. Village of Lordstown*, 2026-Ohio-4073, closes out roughly a year of litigation that saw Lordstown pass an outright data center ban, repeal it under legal pressure, and replace it with a rolling temporary moratorium — a sequence the court found was enough to defeat the developers' claim that they had a "clear legal right" to have their application reviewed.

The case is a significant data point in a fast-growing national litigation category: developers suing municipalities that enact moratoria or bans to halt data center projects after community opposition surfaces. Lordstown's dispute sits alongside similar suits this year against Nashville, the City of Wixom, Michigan, Chatham County, North Carolina, Frederick County, Maryland, and Urbana, Ohio, among others — and it is one of the first to reach a definitive ruling from a state supreme court.

## What happened

Bristolville acquires and develops real property for large-scale data centers nationally. In February 2025, it agreed to buy a parcel in Lordstown — an industrially zoned Trumbull County village near Youngstown that has become a magnet for data center proposals — from BHGH Properties. On October 20, 2025, Bristolville formally kicked off Lordstown's multi-step site-plan-review process under the village's zoning ordinance, hand-delivering a narrative project outline and site plan to the village's Planning and Zoning Office.

The very next day, the village's planning-and-zoning administrator told Bristolville that the review would not proceed: the Planning & Zoning Committee had already recommended a ban on data centers villagewide, and that proposal was pending before the village council on its second reading. Bristolville pushed back, noting data centers were still legal under the ordinance in effect. Six days later, on November 3, 2025, the Lordstown Village Council passed Ordinance No. 57-2025 as an emergency measure, prohibiting any data center within village limits and barring issuance of any zoning permit, building permit or certificate of occupancy for one — though the ban exempted facilities already under construction. The village's solicitor subsequently told Bristolville's counsel the project would "likely be prohibited" by the new ordinance.

Bristolville and BHGH filed their mandamus action in the Ohio Supreme Court seeking an order compelling the village to accept, review and act on the site-plan submission under the ordinance as it stood on October 20, 2025 — before any ban existed. The court dismissed the claim against the individual village solicitor but allowed the case to proceed against the planning-and-zoning administrator and the village itself, issuing an alternative writ and a briefing schedule.

Before that briefing concluded, Lordstown repealed Ordinance No. 57-2025 outright. But the village did not simply let site-plan review resume: it adopted a 180-day moratorium on accepting or processing any data center application, effective January 5, 2026, which the village council later voted unanimously to extend by another 180 days — keeping new data center applications frozen through the end of 2026 while the village studies the projects' effects on local utilities, the environment and residents. According to local reporting, Bristolville's side put more than 3,000 pages of evidence before the court in support of its position, framing the moratorium extension and related zoning changes as an effort to keep its roughly $3.6 billion project in permanent limbo rather than a genuine, temporary pause.

## The court's reasoning

Mandamus relief in Ohio requires a relator to show a clear legal right to the requested relief, a corresponding clear legal duty on the respondent's part to provide it, and the absence of an adequate remedy at law. The Ohio Supreme Court's per curiam opinion held that Bristolville and BHGH could not clear that bar: because Lordstown's current moratorium ordinance — not the pre-ban ordinance in effect when Bristolville filed its application — governs whether the village must act, and because that moratorium places a temporary hold on issuing new data center permits and on receiving and processing new applications, the village has no clear legal duty right now to proceed with site-plan review under the rules that predated the moratorium. The court distinguished the developers' reliance on prior Ohio mandamus precedent (including *Gibson v. Oberlin* and *Fairmount Ctr. Co. v. Arnold*) and concluded the relators had not shown the clear legal right mandamus demands. The writ was denied.

## Why it matters

The ruling is a cautionary marker for data center and other large-footprint commercial developers betting on mandamus or similar compulsory-process claims to overcome local moratoria. Ohio's highest court effectively held that a municipality can reset the legal baseline for an in-process application by repealing an outright ban and substituting a moratorium — even one that is later extended — without thereby creating a clear legal duty to process applications under the superseded, pre-moratorium rules. For municipalities nationwide watching the wave of parallel data center moratorium suits, the decision offers a template: a temporary, renewable pause framed around studying utility, environmental and community impacts is likely to survive a mandamus challenge in a way an outright, permanent ban might not.

For developers, the case underscores that vested-rights and estoppel theories — not mandamus compelling review under old rules — are likely the stronger vehicle where a municipality substitutes a moratorium for a ban mid-dispute, and that the timing of a formal application relative to any public announcement of a forthcoming moratorium or ban can be decisive. Developers moving into jurisdictions where community opposition to data centers is mounting should expect that a moratorium, once in place, will substantially narrow their litigation options, and should document clearly any representations from local officials inviting continued review even as a ban or freeze is under consideration, since the strength of any vested-rights argument tends to turn on exactly that kind of record.

Practical takeaways: data center and similar large-scale developers facing a formal moratorium should not assume a mandamus action will succeed merely because their application predates the moratorium's enactment — Ohio's high court has now confirmed that a current moratorium, not the ordinance in effect when the application was filed, controls whether a municipality has a clear legal duty to act. Developers should build political-risk and moratorium contingencies into site-selection and acquisition timelines for large energy- or infrastructure-intensive projects, including data centers, given how quickly local sentiment and zoning rules can shift once a project becomes publicly known. Municipalities facing similar community pushback have a tested path — repeal an outright ban that invites swift legal challenge and substitute a time-limited, renewable moratorium tied to a stated study purpose — that this decision suggests is more litigation-resistant. And developers with substantial sunk costs in a stalled project should consider building a vested-rights or equitable-estoppel record contemporaneously, rather than relying solely on the timing of their original application, since that is the theory left most open by this ruling.
