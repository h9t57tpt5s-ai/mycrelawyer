# New Jersey Court Clears Elizabeth's $20 Rent-Cap Referendum, Rejecting Landlords' Takings Challenge

### A Union County Superior Court judge has dismissed a coalition of Elizabeth landlords' constitutional challenge to a ballot measure restoring a decades-old rent-increase cap, and the Appellate Division has now refused to pause the vote — sending the question to voters in November while the underlying appeal proceeds.

A citizen-driven effort to restore Elizabeth, New Jersey's longstanding $20 annual rent-increase cap will go to voters on November 3 after a Union County Superior Court judge rejected a takings challenge brought by a coalition of local landlords, and the Appellate Division separately declined to stay the referendum while the landlords' appeal moves forward. The dispute, arising from a citizen petition rather than a private lawsuit against any single owner, is nonetheless a live test of how far courts will let municipalities and voters go in re-tightening rent regulation after a period of landlord-favorable deregulation — and a reminder that even successfully weakened rent controls can be clawed back through the ballot box, not just the city council chamber.

The underlying regulation dates back more than three decades: Elizabeth's rent control ordinance capped annual increases at the lesser of $20 or 3% for units covered by the law. In 2023, the City Council eliminated the $20 cap at landlords' urging, leaving only the 3% ceiling in place — a change owners argued was necessary to keep pace with rising operating costs and reflect a rent-control scheme landlords say had become outdated. Tenant organizers responded in kind, using New Jersey's citizen-initiative process to place a measure back on the ballot restoring the original $20-or-3% formula. After collecting and certifying signatures in mid-2025, a group of 13 Elizabeth landlords sued to keep the question off the ballot altogether, arguing that reinstating the lower cap would restrict their ability to earn a "reasonable return" on their properties so severely that it would amount to an uncompensated taking of private property under the Fifth Amendment and the New Jersey Constitution.

Superior Court Judge John M. Deitch rejected that argument on July 27, 2026, ruling that the landlords had failed to show the proposed cap would deprive them of the "just and reasonable return" New Jersey law requires rent control schemes to preserve. That standard, drawn from New Jersey's own body of rent-control case law rather than federal takings doctrine alone, asks whether a regulation leaves an owner with a fair return on investment — not whether it caps rent increases at a level owners would prefer. Because the landlords' complaint rested on speculative harm rather than a demonstrated inability to operate profitably under the restored cap, Judge Deitch found it fell short of stating a viable constitutional claim at the threshold, pre-election stage. Fifteen New Jersey-based housing justice organizations, represented by the Rutgers Law School Housing Justice & Tenant Solidarity Clinic and Make the Road New Jersey, appeared as amici curiae in support of letting the referendum proceed.

The landlords appealed on August 3 and immediately sought a stay to keep the measure off the November ballot while that appeal is litigated. The Appellate Division denied the stay motion on August 5, meaning Elizabeth voters will decide the referendum's fate on Election Day regardless of how the underlying appeal is eventually resolved. That sequencing matters substantively, not just procedurally: if voters approve the tighter cap in November and the Appellate Division later sides with the landlords, the city could face a contentious rollback of a rent formula tenants have already voted into effect — a scenario with its own litigation risk, since retroactively unwinding a voter-approved regulation invites separate challenges from tenant advocates.

For owners of rent-regulated multifamily property, the case underscores that rent-control deregulation achieved through a city council vote is not necessarily durable if local law allows citizen initiatives to reverse it, and that a "reasonable return" framework — rather than a categorical prohibition on tightening rent formulas — is likely to govern any takings-style challenge to a ballot-driven cap. It also confirms that New Jersey courts are willing to let referenda proceed to a vote even while constitutional objections remain pending on appeal, treating the electoral process itself as presumptively entitled to move forward absent a clearer showing of irreparable harm.

**Practical takeaways:** Owners operating in jurisdictions where rent control has been loosened by legislative or council action should treat that outcome as provisional wherever a citizen-initiative or referendum process exists, since a subsequent ballot measure can restore a tighter cap regardless of the earlier legislative win. A takings challenge to a rent-cap formula needs concrete, property-specific evidence that the cap would deny a reasonable return — general objections to a lower ceiling, without more, are unlikely to survive a motion to dismiss under New Jersey's rent-control case law. Portfolio owners and REITs with regulated assets in referendum-eligible municipalities should model exposure under both the current and the pre-deregulation rent formula, given how quickly a ballot initiative can put the stricter version back in play. Finally, because New Jersey courts have shown a willingness to let contested measures reach the ballot while appeals are pending, owners should not assume that filing suit before an election will functionally delay a vote — and should plan compliance timelines around the referendum outcome itself, not the litigation calendar.
