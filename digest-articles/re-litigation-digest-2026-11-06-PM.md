# Colorado Appeals Court Upholds $122M Tax Hit on Ritz-Carlton Bachelor Gulch Timeshares

### A state appeals panel sides with Eagle County, ruling that Colorado's property tax law does not carve out special treatment for fractional-ownership "timeshare" condominiums — a result with implications well beyond one Beaver Creek hotel.

A division of Marriott International has lost its bid to roll back a roughly $122 million property tax assessment on the fractionally owned condominium units at the Ritz-Carlton, Bachelor Gulch, after the Colorado Court of Appeals on Thursday, November 5, 2026, affirmed Eagle County's valuation of the luxury ski-in, ski-out property. The ruling closes out — for now — a multi-year valuation fight that began when the county roughly doubled its assessment of the resort's 54 condominium units for the 2023 tax year, pushing the figure from about $68.6 million to as much as $124 million, depending on which stage of the dispute is being measured.

The property at issue is split into 648 fractional ownership interests, each entitling its holder to use a unit for 21 days a year — a common structure for ultra-luxury ski and resort properties that lets a developer sell high-end accommodations to buyers who want a seasonal second home without the cost or commitment of owning a whole unit outright. Ritz-Carlton Development Co. ("RCDC"), the Marriott subsidiary that built and operates the fractional program at Bachelor Gulch, challenged the county's valuation methodology, arguing the assessor had effectively double-counted value by pricing the 54 physical units as though they were conventional whole-ownership condominiums rather than accounting for the fractional structure.

At oral argument in October, the Court of Appeals panel pressed both sides on precisely that question: should the units be valued as ordinary fee-simple condominiums, full stop, or does Colorado's assessment framework require some downward adjustment to reflect the fact that each unit is actually owned by up to twelve different fractional interest-holders rather than a single owner? In Thursday's unpublished decision, the panel came down squarely on the county's side, holding that Colorado property tax law does not recognize a distinct valuation methodology for the fractional-ownership model RCDC employs at Bachelor Gulch. The court also rejected a two-step valuation analysis RCDC had urged it to adopt, leaving the county's substantially higher assessment intact.

Because the opinion is unpublished, it does not carry binding precedential weight for other fractional-ownership disputes elsewhere in Colorado. But the practical message for the ski-resort timeshare and private-residence-club sector is hard to miss: assessors are not required, as a matter of law, to discount a property's taxable value simply because it has been carved into fractional interests, and taxpayers hoping to argue otherwise will need a more persuasive doctrinal hook than RCDC's two-step approach provided here.

### Why it matters for CRE owners and lenders

Fractional-ownership and private residence club structures are a meaningful niche within the broader hospitality and resort real estate market, concentrated heavily in mountain and resort destinations where branded operators like Ritz-Carlton, Four Seasons and others have used the model to monetize ultra-high-end inventory. This decision signals to assessors nationally — and certainly within Colorado — that a county can value the underlying real property at or near what a conventional whole-ownership comparable would fetch, without being forced to build in a fractional-ownership discount absent a specific statutory directive to do so. For owners and operators of similar properties, that raises the stakes of any assessment challenge: absent a change in Colorado's statutory framework, taxpayers may need to focus their valuation arguments on appraisal methodology and comparable-sales evidence rather than on the fractional-ownership structure itself as a basis for a categorical discount.

The dispute is also a reminder that large swings in assessed value — here, nearly doubling in a single reassessment cycle — are increasingly common in resort markets that saw outsized price appreciation coming out of the pandemic, and that litigating those swings through a county's board of equalization, the state Board of Assessment Appeals, and ultimately the Court of Appeals is a multi-year process. Owners of other fractional or branded-residence resort properties in mountain jurisdictions should expect county assessors to view this ruling as support for aggressive revaluation, and should build the cost and timeline of a comparable appeal into their underwriting for properties using similar ownership structures.

### Practical takeaways

Owners of fractional-ownership, timeshare or private-residence-club resort properties should not assume a jurisdiction's assessor is required to apply a valuation discount for the fractional structure itself; in Colorado, at least as applied here, that argument failed. Expect assessors in resort counties to treat this decision as validation for valuing fractional interests at or near whole-ownership comparable values, and budget appraisal and litigation resources accordingly if a reassessment produces a similarly dramatic increase. Because the opinion is unpublished, its precedential force is limited, so owners facing a comparable dispute should still develop their own record on appraisal methodology rather than relying on this result alone. Finally, the multi-year path this dispute took — through the county, the state Board of Assessment Appeals, and the Court of Appeals — underscores that a tax valuation challenge on a major resort asset is a long-horizon commitment, not a quick fix, and should be factored into any refinancing or disposition timeline for the property.
