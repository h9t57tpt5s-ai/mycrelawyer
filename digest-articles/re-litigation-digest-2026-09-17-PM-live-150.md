# Idaho Supreme Court: Affiliated Businesses Can't Piggyback on a Neighbor's Condemnation Claim

### A ruling in a Canyon County highway-interchange taking holds that Idaho's statutory "business damages" remedy tracks title to the condemned parcel, not common ownership or control of nearby operations.

The Idaho Supreme Court has affirmed a ruling that sharply limits which businesses can recover "business damages" when the state condemns land for a highway project, rejecting an attempt by a family of commonly controlled companies to share in compensation for a taking that struck only one of their parcels. In a decision issued in Docket No. 52872, the Court sided with the State of Idaho, Idaho Transportation Board against Triple Crown Development, LLC and a group of intervenor entities controlled by developer Lance Thueson, holding that Idaho Code section 7-711(2)'s business-damages provision belongs to the fee owner of the condemned property alone.

The dispute arose after the Idaho Transportation Board condemned a portion of land owned by Triple Crown Development for a highway interchange project in Canyon County, part of the Third Judicial District. Triple Crown and the state stipulated to the value of the land itself, leaving only a separate claim for business damages to be litigated. That claim was pressed not just by Triple Crown but by a cluster of affiliated entities controlled by Thueson, including Thueson Construction, Inc., Americrete Ready Mix Concrete, Inc., Americrete Land Holding LLC, and River Rock Sand & Gravel LLC, all of which intervened in the condemnation case. Their theory rested on a planned but never-realized business: River Rock was to mine gravel from the condemned parcel, Thueson Construction would haul the material to a concrete plant operated by G&B Redi-Mix on Americrete's adjoining land, and the resulting output would supply Americrete's ready-mix operations. No mining permits were ever obtained, and no mining ever actually took place on the condemned property before the state took it.

The district court granted summary judgment against the business-damages claims, and the Idaho Supreme Court affirmed. The Court's reasoning turned on the structure of section 7-711(2) itself: the statute authorizes a business-damages award to compensate for injury to a business operating on the condemned property, but that remedy is tied to the property interest the claimant actually holds in the land being taken. Because the intervenor entities held no fee interest in the condemned parcel — Triple Crown alone did — they had no independent statutory basis to claim business damages regardless of how closely they were affiliated with Triple Crown or with each other through Thueson's common control. And Triple Crown's own business-damages theory failed on the facts, since no mining business had ever actually been established or operated on the parcel the state took; a business plan that never obtained permits or began operations is not the kind of ongoing business the statute is designed to compensate. The Court separately rejected the Transportation Board's request that Triple Crown and the intervenors pay its attorney fees, finding the appeal, while unsuccessful, was not frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation.

The ruling is a useful marker for how narrowly Idaho courts will read a compensation statute that, on its face, protects "business" interests affected by a taking. Eminent domain compensation schemes across states generally distinguish between just compensation for the land itself, measured by fair market value, and supplemental remedies like business damages, relocation costs, or severance damages, which are typically available only to parties with a defined, existing legal or possessory interest in the specific property taken. Idaho's approach here confirms that commonly controlled corporate structures, common in many family- or founder-controlled development and construction operations, do not create a shared compensable interest simply because the businesses are economically interdependent or share ownership. Each entity's right to compensation is measured by what that entity itself owns, leases, or otherwise holds in the parcel the government actually condemns.

For property owners and business operators structuring multi-entity operations on adjoining or nearby parcels, particularly where land might someday be subject to eminent domain for public infrastructure, the case is a reminder to think carefully about how ownership and operating entities are aligned with the parcels a project might actually need. A prospective use that has not been permitted or commenced is unlikely to support a business-damages claim even for the fee owner, let alone for an affiliated but separately titled business next door. The decision also illustrates that Idaho courts will not automatically award fees against a losing condemnee in eminent domain litigation, leaving room for good-faith, if ultimately unsuccessful, challenges to a condemning authority's damages theory without exposing the challenger to a fee award.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Idaho's business-damages remedy under Code section 7-711(2) belongs to the entity holding fee title to the condemned parcel; affiliated companies under common ownership or control cannot claim a share simply because they are economically connected to the fee owner's operations.
- A speculative or unpermitted future use — here, gravel mining that never obtained the necessary permits or began operating — will not support a business-damages claim, even for the parcel's actual owner; the statute protects established business operations, not business plans.
- Owners assembling multi-entity operations across adjoining parcels should align title and operating structure with an eye toward eminent domain exposure, since compensation rights will track legal ownership of the specific land taken, not the broader economic enterprise.
- A condemnee who loses an eminent domain appeal in Idaho is not automatically exposed to the condemning authority's attorney fees; fee-shifting requires a showing that the appeal itself was frivolous, unreasonable, or without foundation.
