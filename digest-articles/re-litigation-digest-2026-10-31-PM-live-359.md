# Texas Appeals Court: Dental Tenant's Square-Footage Fraud Claim Against Former Shopping-Center Landlord Is Time-Barred

### A decade-old lease and a 2023 discovery of a smaller-than-advertised space weren't enough to revive claims against a landlord who sold the property in 2018

A Texas appellate court has affirmed summary judgment for a commercial landlord accused of overcharging a dental-practice tenant by misrepresenting the size of its leased space, holding that the tenant's breach-of-contract, fraud, and consumer-protection claims were filed years too late — and that the landlord in question had sold the shopping center, and been released from the lease, long before the tenant ever complained. The Thirteenth Court of Appeals' October 29, 2026 decision in *Veena Dental PA v. Brixmor GA Apollo II TX LP* is a reminder to both commercial landlords and tenants that a lease's assignment-and-release provisions, combined with ordinary limitations rules, can foreclose a claim no matter how sympathetic the underlying factual dispute looks.

## What happened

In March 2013, Veena Dental PA leased roughly 1,863 square feet of retail space at 1548 Wildcat Drive in Portland, Texas — part of the Northshore Plaza shopping center — from Brixmor GA Apollo II TX LP, intending to build out and operate a dental office. The parties negotiated a per-square-foot rental rate and extended the lease by amendment in September 2018. That same year, Brixmor sold Northshore Plaza to a successor entity, Northshore Plaza LP, assigning Veena's lease and triggering a provision under which Brixmor was released from all further liability and obligations once the buyer assumed them.

Five years later, in 2023, Veena measured its space and says it discovered the true square footage was closer to 1,500 square feet — matching the dimensions shown on the dental office's own 2013 construction plans — rather than the 1,863 square feet stated in the lease. Veena demanded reimbursement for what it characterized as years of rent overpayments tied to the inflated square footage, and when Brixmor refused, filed suit on May 22, 2024, asserting breach of contract (more accurately, the court later noted, fraudulent inducement), common-law fraud, and violations of the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act.

Brixmor moved for traditional summary judgment on two independent grounds: that the statute of limitations had run on every claim, and that Brixmor was no longer the correct defendant at all, having sold the property and been contractually released from the lease back in 2018. The trial court in San Patricio County granted the motion and entered final judgment on September 30, 2025, and denied Veena's motion for reconsideration that November.

## The appellate ruling

Writing for a unanimous panel, Justice Ysmael D. Fonseca held that the record conclusively established the limitations defense, making it unnecessary to reach Brixmor's separate argument that it had been released as a party. Texas gives a plaintiff four years to sue for breach of contract or fraud and two years for a DTPA violation, but a fraud or DTPA claim's clock does not start until the plaintiff discovers, or in the exercise of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the wrongful act — the so-called discovery rule.

The court found that discovery rule couldn't rescue Veena's claims. In discovery, Veena had admitted that its own 2013 construction plans — prepared before it ever opened for business — reflected the space's true, smaller dimensions. Because those plans were in Veena's possession from the outset, the court reasoned, the actual square footage was reasonably discoverable well before 2023, regardless of when Veena says it actually got around to measuring the space. The court also noted, in a footnote, that Veena's claim was more properly characterized as fraudulent inducement than ordinary breach of contract, since the gravamen of the complaint was that a fraudulent misrepresentation induced Veena to sign the lease in the first place — but that distinction didn't change the four-year limitations period or the accrual analysis. With the accrual date pinned to the March 2013 lease signing, every claim was untimely by a comfortable margin, and the court affirmed the summary judgment in full.

## Why it matters for commercial landlords and tenants

The decision illustrates two separate but overlapping risks that recur across commercial leasing disputes. First, the discovery rule is not a free pass for a tenant that sits on readily available information: courts will charge a tenant with knowledge reflected in its own records, including architectural or construction plans it commissioned, even years before the tenant gets around to checking them against what the lease states. Second, a seller-landlord's standard assignment-and-release clause — boilerplate in most shopping-center and net-lease transactions — can extinguish liability for a transferring landlord's own alleged pre-sale misconduct once a successor assumes the lease, leaving a tenant with a claim against an entity that may be harder to locate, less capitalized, or less motivated to settle than the original landlord.

### Practical takeaways

Commercial tenants negotiating or renewing a lease should independently verify stated square footage against their own architectural drawings or a physical measurement at signing, rather than relying on the landlord's figures, since a later discovery claim will be measured against what the tenant's own contemporaneous records show was knowable. Landlords selling a leased property should confirm that assignment-and-release language is drafted to survive exactly this kind of after-the-fact dispute, and should retain records showing what the buyer assumed and when. Both sides should treat the accrual date for any lease-related misrepresentation claim as running from signing, not from whenever the harm is actually noticed, absent genuinely new facts that could not have been uncovered through ordinary diligence. And any tenant considering a stale claim against a long-departed former landlord should assume that a limitations defense, a release provision, or both will be litigated before the merits ever come into view.
