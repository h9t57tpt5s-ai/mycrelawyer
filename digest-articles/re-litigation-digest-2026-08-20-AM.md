# CMBS Trust Moves to Foreclose on Princeton-Area Office Complex After $130M Loan Matures Unpaid

### A Morgan Stanley-originated securitized loan on a Mercer County, New Jersey office park went unpaid at its January maturity — and the trustee's August 12 complaint asks for a receiver before it asks for a foreclosure sale

**What happened.** U.S. Bank National Association, acting as trustee for the registered holders of a 2016 Morgan Stanley Bank of America Merrill Lynch commercial mortgage trust, filed suit on August 12, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey against Lenox Drive Office Park LLC, the Delaware-registered entity that owns an office complex on Lenox Drive in Lawrence Township, part of the Princeton Pike Corporate Center in Mercer County. The complaint alleges that a $130 million loan originated by Morgan Stanley Bank, N.A. in December 2015 and later securitized into the 2016-C28 commercial mortgage trust matured on January 1, 2026 — and that the borrower never paid it off. Eight months after the maturity date came and went, the trustee is now in federal court seeking both a judicial foreclosure and, notably, the immediate appointment of a receiver to run the property while the case is pending.

**Why it matters.** The filing is a textbook illustration of how the current wave of office-loan distress is moving through the courts, and it is worth attention precisely because of what it does not allege. This is not a case built on tenant fraud, mismanagement, or a covenant violation buried in the loan documents — it is a maturity default, plain and simple. A loan that priced a decade of debt service against 2015-era office fundamentals came due in a market where refinancing that debt on comparable terms is, for many sponsors, no longer possible. Trepp and other CMBS trackers have flagged 2026 as a peak year for exactly this kind of "hard maturity," where a borrower has already exhausted whatever extension options its loan documents allowed and simply cannot pay the balance or refinance it elsewhere. Office collateral bears a disproportionate share of that wave, and Mercer County's suburban office corridor — like similar corporate parks ringing Princeton, Route 1, and I-95 — has seen occupancy and rental-rate erosion that makes a ten-year-old appraisal look increasingly disconnected from current value.

The receivership request is the more immediate legal story for practitioners. Rather than waiting out the months a contested judicial foreclosure and any post-judgment sale process can take, the trust's counsel asked the court to install a receiver now — a court-supervised custodian empowered to collect rents, pay operating expenses, and manage leasing decisions at the property while the underlying foreclosure claim proceeds. New Jersey courts, like those in most states, treat receivership as an equitable remedy available on a lender's showing that a defaulting borrower cannot be trusted to preserve the collateral's value, and it is increasingly the tool special servicers reach for first once workout negotiations stall. For an ownership entity like Lenox Drive Office Park LLC, the practical consequence of a receiver being appointed would arrive well before any foreclosure sale: operational and financial control of the asset would shift to a court-appointed custodian, leaving existing ownership as a bystander to decisions about tenants, vendors, and capital expenditures on its own building.

The case also underscores a structural feature of CMBS lending that owners sometimes underestimate until a maturity default forces the issue. Once a loan transfers to special servicing — as this one evidently did after the missed January payoff — the servicer's mandate runs to bondholders, not to the sponsor's relationship with the lender. A special servicer facing a hard maturity with no realistic refinancing path has limited incentive to extend forbearance indefinitely, particularly where continued interest accrual and declining property performance work against certificate holders. That dynamic has been playing out across a string of similar suburban and secondary-market office foreclosures this year, from Minnesota to Colorado to New Jersey, as loans originated in the 2015-2016 vintage window reach their ten-year maturities into a lending environment that looks nothing like the one in which they were written.

**Practical takeaways.** Sponsors holding 2015-2016-vintage CMBS office loans approaching maturity should assume that a special servicer's patience has a shorter runway than it once did, and should engage workout counsel well before a maturity date, not after a payment is missed. Because receivership can strip operational control of a property faster and more cheaply than a completed foreclosure, borrowers facing a maturity default should treat a receivership motion — not the foreclosure complaint itself — as the more urgent litigation risk to prepare for. Owners of suburban office assets financed a decade ago should revisit current appraisals and refinancing capacity now, since a lender's willingness to modify terms tends to track how far property value has fallen from the original loan basis, and the gap only widens with continued market softening. Finally, guarantors and asset managers should confirm exactly what non-recourse carve-outs or personal guaranties, if any, attach to the loan in question, since a maturity default — unlike some covenant breaches — does not always trigger the same recourse exposure, but the terms vary enough from deal to deal that assumptions are a poor substitute for reading the actual loan documents.
