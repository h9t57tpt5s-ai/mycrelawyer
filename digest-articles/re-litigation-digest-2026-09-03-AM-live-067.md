# Houston's $290M Ritz-Carlton Tower Draws Broker-Exclusivity Suit — And a Fast Trip to Arbitration

### A former brokerage chief's fight over unpaid compensation on the Ritz-Carlton Residences, Houston shows how quickly a development-stage compensation dispute can be diverted out of court — and why that matters for the underlying construction schedule.

Jacob Sudhoff, the former CEO of Douglas Elliman Texas, has sued the developer behind the Ritz-Carlton Residences, Houston, alleging he was never properly compensated for work he says helped secure the project's headline $30 million penthouse buyer and a significant equity investor. The suit, filed in Harris County District Court against an entity affiliated with developer Deiso Moss and its principals, Andrew Deiso and Taylor Moss, seeks damages Sudhoff says exceed $1 million. In a preliminary skirmish that will shape how the dispute plays out, the court has already declined to pause construction on the 45-story Uptown tower while the underlying claims are resolved, instead directing the parties toward arbitration.

According to the complaint, Sudhoff worked with Deiso Moss from October 2024 through August 2025 under what he characterizes as an exclusivity arrangement covering both capital-raising and sales activity on the roughly $290 million project. Sudhoff alleges the developer breached that exclusivity by terminating him and then working directly with clients and investors he had cultivated — including the buyer of the project's marquee penthouse unit. Deiso Moss disputes that framing entirely: a spokesperson for the developer says Sudhoff was engaged only to raise equity capital, not to sell condominium units, and that his engagement had already ended before the project's official sales launch. The developer further contends the penthouse sale was unrelated to Sudhoff's scope of work and that his termination reflected an underperformance on the equity-raising mandate itself, not any retaliatory maneuver around his client relationships.

That factual gap — over whether Sudhoff's role and any exclusivity obligation extended to unit sales at all — is likely to be the central battleground once the case proceeds, but for now it will proceed somewhere other than open court. Sudhoff asked the Harris County court for a temporary restraining order and a temporary injunction to halt work on the tower while the litigation was pending, a request the court denied on both fronts. The court instead directed the dispute into arbitration, a outcome that typically traces back to an arbitration clause embedded in the parties' original engagement agreement — a common feature of development-stage consulting and brokerage contracts precisely because sponsors want disputes over fees resolved quickly and privately rather than through public litigation that could unsettle lenders, equity partners, or prospective buyers during active construction and sales.

For CRE developers and the brokers, consultants, and capital-raising advisers who work with them, the case is a reminder that the scope of an engagement — and any exclusivity carve-out tied to it — needs to be defined with precision at the outset, not left to be litigated after a falling-out. A consultant retained to raise equity capital and one retained to market and sell finished units perform genuinely different functions, and a contract that blurs the line between the two invites exactly the kind of after-the-fact dispute over "who gets credit, and who gets paid" that Sudhoff and Deiso Moss are now having. The developer's position — that Sudhoff's engagement ended before the sales launch and that the penthouse transaction fell outside his mandate — will only be persuasive to an arbitrator if the underlying agreement actually says so in unambiguous terms.

The dispute also illustrates how effectively a well-drafted arbitration clause can insulate an active construction project from the disruption of a compensation fight. Had the court granted Sudhoff's requested injunction, a project already carrying $290 million in cost and timeline exposure could have faced a construction freeze over a dispute concerning, at most, a few million dollars in disputed fees — a mismatch in stakes that courts are typically reluctant to enable, and which arbitration clauses are specifically designed to prevent by keeping fee disputes on a separate track from the underlying project.

**Practical takeaways:** Developers negotiating with brokers, capital-raising consultants, or other transaction advisers on ground-up projects should define the engagement's scope — sales versus capital-raising versus both — explicitly and in writing, rather than relying on informal understandings that can be characterized very differently once a relationship ends. An arbitration clause in advisory and brokerage agreements remains one of the most effective tools for keeping a compensation dispute from threatening an active construction timeline, as this case's TRO denial demonstrates. And any party terminating a consultant or broker mid-project should document contemporaneously why the termination occurred and how it relates (or doesn't) to the specific deals the consultant claims credit for, since that record becomes central once a fee dispute is litigated or arbitrated after the fact.
