# Landowner's FOIA Suit Seeks to Void Botetourt County's Google Data Center Agreement

### Petition filed in Botetourt County Circuit Court argues the Board of Supervisors approved the county's economic-development deal with a Google subsidiary in closed session without disclosing its terms

A Botetourt County, Virginia landowner has sued the county, its Board of Supervisors, its Economic Development Authority, and Helio Capital LLC — a subsidiary wholly owned by Google — seeking to void the economic-development performance agreement that cleared the way for Google's data center campus at the Botetourt Center at Greenfield business park. The petition, filed November 2, 2026 in Botetourt County Circuit Court, asks the court to declare null and void both the board's closed-session discussion of the agreement and its subsequent open-session vote approving it, and to rescind any expedited permits issued under it, on the theory that the board violated Virginia's Freedom of Information Act.

The dispute traces back to June 24, 2025, when the Botetourt County Board of Supervisors approved the performance agreement with Helio Capital LLC that cleared the way for Google's purchase of roughly 312 acres at the Botetourt Center at Greenfield for approximately $14 million, plus an additional $4 million in community support payments over five years. According to reporting on the petition, the board met in closed session that day, discussed the agreement, and reached agreement on its terms before returning to open session, where it approved the agreement without disclosing what it contained. The agreement did more than bless the land sale: it committed the county to expedite permitting for the project and created performance grants that effectively cap the data center's personal-property-tax burden going forward — the kind of long-term fiscal commitment that Virginia's open-meetings law generally requires local governing bodies to adopt in public.

The petition, brought as an action for a writ of mandamus and injunctive relief, names the Board of Supervisors, Botetourt County itself, the county's Economic Development Authority, and Helio Capital LLC as respondents. Virginia's FOIA permits a local governing body to discuss some economic-development matters in closed session — typically where disclosure would be adverse to the county's bargaining position with a prospective business — but requires the body to return to open session to take any final action, and courts applying the statute have held that the public vote must give the public enough information to understand what is actually being approved, not simply a bare up-or-down vote on an undisclosed document. The petition's theory is that Botetourt's board skipped that step: it voted to approve the agreement in open session but never summarized or disclosed its substantive terms there, leaving the public in the dark about the scope of the tax concessions and permitting commitments the county had just made to Google.

The lawsuit lands amid broader, separate friction over the same project. Days before the suit was filed, Cardinal News reported that the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers had decided not to hold a public hearing on the data center campus despite receiving hundreds of requests for one — a separate federal permitting process, tied to wetlands and waterway impacts, that has become its own flashpoint for residents who feel shut out of decisions about a project of this scale. County officials have declined to comment on the FOIA suit's specific allegations, citing the pending litigation, while the plaintiff's attorney has said the suit is focused narrowly on how the performance agreement was adopted rather than on the merits of the data center project itself.

For commercial real estate owners, developers, and local governments negotiating similar incentive packages, the case is a reminder that an open-meetings or public-records defect in how a deal was approved — not any flaw in the deal's substance — can become the single biggest litigation risk to a completed economic-development agreement. Data center developers in particular have faced a wave of citizen and competitor suits across multiple states this year challenging zoning approvals, moratoria, and incentive deals, and a successful FOIA or open-meetings challenge carries a remedy — voiding the governing body's action outright — that a zoning fight typically does not. A project that has already closed on land, begun permitting, or started construction can still face a court order unwinding the governmental approvals that made the deal possible, with cascading effects on financing covenants, tax projections, and construction schedules that assumed the underlying agreement was final.

Practically, the case argues for building a paper trail that survives exactly this kind of challenge. Developers negotiating economic-development agreements, performance grants, or tax-abatement deals with a local governing body should confirm — and, where possible, get the locality to confirm in writing — that any closed-session discussion of the deal stays within the statutory exception the locality is relying on, and that the body's return to open session includes an actual disclosure of the agreement's substantive terms before the final vote, not just a vote on an unnamed document. Counsel should also build in severability and ratification mechanisms so that a procedural open-meetings defect, if one is later found, can be cured by a fresh, properly noticed open-session vote rather than unwinding the entire transaction, land purchase, and permits that followed from it. And because open-meetings and public-records theories are emerging as a favored vehicle for opponents of large-scale commercial projects — data centers especially — developers should expect that any agreement negotiated substantially behind closed doors, however common that practice has been for sensitive site-selection deals, is now a likely target for exactly this kind of suit.
