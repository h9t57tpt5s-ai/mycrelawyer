# Richmond Circuit Court Weighs Fraud Claims Over "Not Built to Design" Waterfront Apartment Tower

### A $40 million dispute between a Manchester apartment building's developer and the investor who bought it tests how far a seller's post-closing repair promises can shield it from a fraud claim once the defects turn out to run deeper than disclosed.

Four Mile Capital, the Denver-based investment firm that paid nearly $50 million in 2021 for River's Edge at Manchester, a 212-unit riverfront apartment building at 505 Porter Street in Richmond, Virginia, is asking a Richmond Circuit Court judge to let a fraud claim against the building's developer proceed to trial. The developer, Reynolds South Tower LLC — an entity associated with Guy Blundon of CMB Development and Mark Purcell of Purcell Construction Corp. — is fighting to have that fraud count dismissed, arguing in an August 7 hearing that Four Mile was never misled about the condition of the building it bought. Richmond Circuit Court Chief Judge Jacqueline S. McClenney heard argument from both sides and told counsel she expects to rule within a few weeks, leaving the case's core legal theory, and its viability past the pleading stage, unresolved for now.

The dispute traces back to the 2021 sale itself. Four Mile has acknowledged it knew River's Edge had experienced water leaks before the sale closed, and that the sellers agreed, as part of the deal, to perform repairs both before and after closing. For roughly a year, according to the complaint, that arrangement held: repair work continued into 2022. But Four Mile alleges that by 2022, Purcell had stopped making good on the repair commitment, and the leaks persisted. In 2023, after the water intrusion continued despite the earlier repairs, Four Mile hired an outside engineering firm to investigate the building envelope. That investigation, the complaint alleges, revealed that River's Edge was not built in accordance with its own architectural design: specifically, the building allegedly lacked the waterproof sheathing that Four Mile says it was told, before the sale, had been installed. First filed in Richmond Circuit Court in May 2025, the suit asserts fraud, breach of contract, negligence, and business conspiracy, and seeks more than $40 million in damages.

The legal fight now centers on a distinction that separates ordinary construction-defect claims from fraud claims: whether a seller's affirmative misrepresentation about how a building was actually constructed, as opposed to a general failure to disclose a known defect, survives a motion to dismiss under Virginia law. Reynolds South Tower's attorney, Stephen Faraci of Whiteford, Taylor & Preston, argued at the August hearing and in prior filings that Four Mile was not misled about the state of the building, framing the pre-sale water leaks and subsequent repair arrangement as evidence that Four Mile bought the property with eyes open to its condition. Four Mile's attorney, Dylan Graham of the Fairfax-based Wise Law Firm, countered that the misrepresentations extended beyond the leaks Four Mile knew about, to specific, verifiable claims about the building's underlying construction — namely, the presence of waterproof sheathing that an independent 2023 investigation found was never actually installed. That distinction matters because Virginia courts have generally been reluctant to let a fraud claim proceed where a sophisticated buyer had actual knowledge of the defect it later complains of, but far more willing to sustain one where the buyer relied on specific factual representations about construction methods or materials that turned out to be false.

If Judge McClenney allows the fraud count to survive, the case will proceed toward discovery on the theory that a seller's repair commitments during and after a sale process do not necessarily cure, or waive, an underlying misrepresentation about how the asset was built in the first place — a sequence increasingly common in multifamily transactions where sellers negotiate a "known issue, ongoing repair" framework to keep a deal moving rather than disclosing the full scope of a defect before closing. A dismissal, by contrast, would reinforce the more buyer-protective baseline: that a purchaser who proceeds to close with actual knowledge of a defect, even a partial one, assumes substantial risk that later-discovered aggravating facts will not resurrect a fraud theory once the deal is done.

**Practical takeaways:** Buyers of existing multifamily assets who negotiate a seller repair commitment for a known defect, rather than a price reduction or holdback, should document precisely what representations the seller makes about the underlying cause and construction of the problem, since a "we'll fix it" arrangement does not necessarily waive a later fraud claim if the seller's account of the defect's cause turns out to be false. An independent forensic investigation, commissioned promptly once negotiated repairs stall or fail to resolve a recurring issue, can be decisive both for quantifying damages and for establishing that a construction-method misrepresentation, not just a disclosed defect, is what the buyer is actually suing over. And sellers extending post-closing repair support for a known issue should recognize that continuing that support, rather than disclosing its full scope up front, does not insulate them from a later fraud claim if the underlying construction defect is more extensive than what was represented at the time of sale.
