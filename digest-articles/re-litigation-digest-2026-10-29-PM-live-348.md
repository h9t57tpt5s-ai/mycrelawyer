# Faulty Smoke Detectors Sink Hotel Owner's Fire-Insurance Claim, New York Appeals Court Rules

### A protective-safeguards endorsement proves fatal to coverage after a 2018 blaze at a Schoharie County hotel, restaurant and bowling alley complex

New York's Appellate Division, Third Department, has handed insurer Seneca Insurance Company Inc. a win in a long-running coverage fight over a 2018 fire at a hotel, restaurant and bowling-alley complex in the Town of Cobleskill, ruling on October 29, 2026 that the property owner's failure to maintain operational smoke detectors throughout the building voided coverage for the loss as a matter of law.

The case, *Seneca Ins. Co. Inc. v. Cobleskill Enter., LLP*, arose after a December 24, 2018 fire caused extensive smoke and burn damage at the Cobleskill Enterprise, LLP property, a mixed hospitality complex that included a hotel, restaurant, banquet room, kitchen and bowling alley. When the owner sought to collect under its commercial property insurance policy, Seneca denied the claim and filed a declaratory judgment action in Schoharie County, arguing among other things that Cobleskill Enterprise had breached the policy's protective safeguards endorsement, or PSE, a warranty requiring operational smoke detectors in all units and common areas as a condition of coverage.

The underlying facts, developed through discovery, were damaging to the insured. A technician from the fire-alarm contractor Cobleskill Enterprise had hired, after being cited by the Village of Cobleskill in October 2018 for missing carbon monoxide detectors, testified that the property's fire-detection system was outdated, relied on two incompatible types of equipment, and could not be adapted to add the required detectors without a full replacement. His inspection turned up no smoke detectors at all in the pool area, bowling alley, basement or the dining room ceiling; the banquet room and kitchen had only heat detectors, not smoke detectors; and several existing devices in the hallway and near the pool were discovered to be nonfunctional "dummy detectors." Separately, the reporting panel covering the restaurant, kitchen, banquet room and bowling alley could not communicate with the main fire alarm panel in the hotel lobby, meaning a fire breaking out in those areas would never have triggered the building's central alarm at all.

Supreme Court in Schoharie County had denied Seneca's motion for summary judgment on the PSE issue, finding that the insurer's own evidence raised triable fact questions. The Third Department reversed on that point. Applying New York Insurance Law § 3106, which provides that a breach of a policy warranty voids coverage only if the breach "materially increases the risk of loss," the appellate panel held that Seneca had established both the breach and its materiality as a matter of law, leaving no issue for a factfinder. The court reasoned that an "operational" smoke detector, given its plain and ordinary meaning, must be one that is actually capable of detecting smoke and alerting occupants -- and a property riddled with missing, incompatible or disconnected detectors across multiple common areas plainly fell short of that standard, materially increasing the risk that a fire like the one that occurred would go undetected.

The ruling did not end the broader litigation. Cobleskill Enterprise had also brought a third-party action against Links Insurance Services, LLC, the retail broker that placed the policy, alleging that any coverage gap was the product of Links' own negligence or breach of duty in procuring and servicing the policy; Seneca separately asserted direct fraud and negligent-misrepresentation claims against Links tied to how the claim was handled after the policy had reportedly already been cancelled for nonpayment. Those claims against the broker proceed notwithstanding the insurer's win on the core coverage question.

**Practical takeaways**

For commercial property owners -- particularly hospitality operators juggling multiple building systems across large, multi-use footprints -- the decision is a reminder that a protective safeguards endorsement is not boilerplate. Courts will enforce an unambiguous warranty requiring "operational" devices "in units and common areas" according to its plain terms, and partial or inconsistent compliance across different sections of a property can be enough to defeat an otherwise valid claim. Owners should treat any code citation or contractor finding of inadequate fire-detection equipment as a direct threat to insurance coverage, not merely a local compliance issue, and should document remediation promptly and completely once a gap is identified. Where an insurance broker is involved in placing or renewing a policy with safeguards conditions, owners facing a coverage denial should consider, as Cobleskill Enterprise did here, whether the broker's own conduct in explaining or monitoring compliance with those conditions created separate exposure, since a broker's negligence claim can survive even after the direct coverage claim against the insurer is lost.
