# Credit Union's $30 Million Fraud Suit Exposes Cracks in Construction-Loan Oversight

### Service Credit Union accuses its own former loan officer and the sponsors of a stalled New Hampshire senior living project of conspiring to divert construction-loan draws for personal use — a cautionary tale for lenders on funds-control discipline.

A ten-count civil suit filed in Grafton Superior Court is putting a spotlight on one of the more uncomfortable risks in commercial real estate lending: the possibility that a lender's own personnel help borrowers misdirect loan proceeds. Service Credit Union (SCU), a New Hampshire-chartered financial institution, filed suit on June 24, 2026, against the sponsors of a stalled senior living development in Littleton, New Hampshire, along with SCU's own former commercial loan officer, alleging a coordinated scheme to defraud the credit union out of a $30 million construction loan.

The 35-page complaint names brothers George and Michael Papadimatos, principals of Granite Senior Operations LLC and the broader entity behind the planned Artemis Living at Littleton campus, along with David A. Weed, the SCU loan officer who oversaw the credit and was terminated from the credit union in July 2025. Additional defendants include Shawn Harressey and CTC Commercial, along with related entities Blue Lodge Spray Foam and GSS. SCU alleges breach of contract, fraud, civil conspiracy, unlawful conversion, unjust enrichment, and fraudulent transfer against the group, and separately accuses Weed of gross negligence for allegedly acting outside the scope of his employment while the loan was being administered and disbursed.

According to the complaint, Weed and the Papadimatos brothers — together with CTC as to certain fund disbursements — conspired to induce SCU into issuing the $30 million construction loan and then diverted various draws for purposes unrelated to the project, rather than applying them to construction costs as the loan agreement required. Construction on the Artemis Living campus, a planned senior living facility along St. Johnsbury Road, stalled in September 2025 amid what the complaint describes as a lack of payment to both the lender and the project's own contractors, and the property has since gone into foreclosure. SCU's own valuation puts the collateral at roughly $22 million against the $30 million owed — meaning that even a successful liquidation of the property is unlikely to make the credit union whole. Alongside the complaint, SCU filed a motion for ex parte attachment, seeking to record liens against real estate and other assets held by the defendants at the Grafton County Registry of Deeds and the New Hampshire Secretary of State's office before those assets could be moved or encumbered further. Weed, the Papadimatos brothers, Blue Lodge Spray Foam, and GSS face the bulk of the $30 million claim, while SCU is separately seeking roughly $1.5 million from CTC Commercial and the Haresseys tied to their alleged role in specific disbursements.

For commercial lenders, the case is a pointed illustration of why construction-loan administration cannot be treated as a purely ministerial function once a loan closes. Draw requests, lien waivers, and inspection certifications are typically the last line of defense against exactly the kind of diversion SCU alleges — and when the loan officer responsible for enforcing those controls is himself accused of being part of the scheme, the usual internal checks fail simultaneously with the borrower-side fraud. The suit also underscores the ripple effects of a stalled construction project on the surrounding local economy: regional reporting on the Littleton campus's collapse has already noted that the episode has made some area lenders more cautious about extending new commercial credit, illustrating how a single troubled loan can tighten capital availability well beyond the parties actually in litigation.

The case remains at an early stage, with SCU's attachment motion aimed largely at preserving assets while the fraud and conspiracy claims are litigated. No trial date has been set, and the defendants have not yet filed public responses to the complaint's allegations.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Lenders should treat construction-loan administration — draw approvals, lien waiver collection, and site-inspection sign-offs — as a control function that itself needs oversight, particularly where a single loan officer has broad authority over disbursements on a large, multi-draw project.
- Segregating loan-origination and loan-administration duties, and periodically rotating or independently auditing the personnel who approve draws, can reduce the risk that a single insider can both originate a fraud-adjacent loan and control the mechanism for diverting its proceeds.
- Where a construction loan sours amid allegations of internal fraud, lenders should move quickly on asset-preservation remedies like ex parte attachment, since the same misconduct that diverted loan proceeds often also puts other borrower and guarantor assets at risk of dissipation before judgment.
