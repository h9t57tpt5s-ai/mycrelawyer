# Federal Judge Approves $7M Multistate Settlement With Blackstone-Backed Landlord LivCor Over Algorithmic Rent-Setting

### The consent judgment in the DOJ's RealPage antitrust case resolves claims that LivCor shared competitively sensitive pricing data with rival landlords and bars it from most algorithmic pricing tools going forward

A federal judge in North Carolina has signed off on a $7 million settlement between LivCor, LLC — a large multifamily landlord owned by Blackstone — and a coalition of the U.S. Department of Justice and nine state attorneys general, resolving claims that LivCor participated in an unlawful scheme to coordinate apartment rents using RealPage's revenue-management software and shared competitor data. U.S. District Judge William Lindsay Osteen Jr. of the Middle District of North Carolina entered the final consent judgment on June 18, 2026, closing out LivCor's piece of the sprawling algorithmic-pricing antitrust litigation that has reshaped multifamily housing law over the past two years.

The underlying case traces back to January 7, 2025, when the United States, joined by state co-plaintiffs, filed a complaint alleging that LivCor and five other large landlords actively participated in a scheme to set rents using each other's competitively sensitive information, channeled through RealPage's common pricing algorithm. That complaint built on the DOJ's broader antitrust action against RealPage itself, which alleges the software vendor operated as the hub of a hub-and-spoke conspiracy among competing landlords who used its revenue-management platform to align pricing rather than compete independently. LivCor, which controls approximately 3,500 apartment units in North Carolina alone as part of a much larger national multifamily portfolio, was one of the largest individual landlord-defendants named in that expanded complaint.

The Justice Department's Antitrust Division filed a proposed consent decree resolving the claims against LivCor in December 2025. Because the settlement resolves federal antitrust claims brought by the government, it was subject to the Tunney Act's public notice-and-comment process: the proposed decree and an accompanying competitive impact statement were published in the Federal Register, with a 60-day public comment period before the court could enter final judgment. That process concluded in June 2026, when Judge Osteen approved the settlement and entered it as a final consent judgment — a procedural step that carries the weight of a judicial order rather than a mere private settlement agreement, meaning violations of its terms can be enforced through the court's contempt power.

The dollar terms are relatively modest against the backdrop of a company the size of LivCor's institutional sponsor, but the settlement's real weight lies in its behavioral restrictions. LivCor must stop using third-party pricing software or algorithms to set apartment rents unless that software satisfies conditions the decree spells out as being "within the rules" — a standard the decree defines in detail to exclude tools that ingest non-public, competitively sensitive data from rival landlords. LivCor is separately barred from sharing or receiving competitively sensitive pricing, occupancy, or discount data with or from other landlords in any form, and from attending or otherwise participating in RealPage-hosted meetings or forums involving competing landlords. The $7 million in monetary relief is being distributed among the nine participating states — North Carolina, which led the state coalition under Attorney General Jeff Jackson, along with California, Colorado, Connecticut, Illinois, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Oregon, and Tennessee.

For CRE owners and multifamily operators nationally, the LivCor consent judgment is significant less for its size than for what it confirms about how the RealPage-adjacent antitrust litigation is resolving in practice. Rather than proceeding to trial, large landlord-defendants are increasingly settling on individual tracks — LivCor's resolution follows a similar cooperation agreement Greystar struck with the DOJ — accepting behavioral injunctions and comparatively modest payments in exchange for closing out potentially far larger exposure. That pattern gives other landlord-defendants still litigating, as well as multifamily owners who have never been sued but use similar revenue-management tools, a fairly detailed template for what a negotiated resolution looks like: not a ban on all pricing software, but a structural wall against ingesting or sharing non-public competitor data through whatever tool a landlord chooses to use.

The settlement also underscores how quickly the legal terrain around algorithmic pricing has shifted from a single sprawling antitrust theory into parallel enforcement tracks. Municipal and state legislatures in Philadelphia, San Diego, New Jersey, and New York have separately enacted outright bans or restrictions on algorithmic rent-pricing software, creating statutory liability that exists independently of any proof of the kind of multi-landlord coordination the DOJ's complaint against LivCor and its co-defendants centers on. A landlord that resolves its DOJ antitrust exposure through a consent decree like LivCor's does not necessarily resolve exposure under those separate statutory schemes, several of which authorize private rights of action with per-violation penalties.

Practical takeaways: Multifamily owners and managers using any third-party revenue-management or pricing software should audit precisely what data flows into that tool, since the enforcement pattern emerging from the RealPage litigation draws a sharp line around non-public, competitor-sourced pricing and occupancy data rather than algorithmic pricing tools as a category. Owners currently named in the broader DOJ action, or facing parallel state suits, should treat LivCor's resolution — behavioral restrictions plus a comparatively modest payment, rather than treble damages after trial — as a realistic settlement benchmark rather than assuming the litigation must be fought to a verdict. And because a DOJ antitrust settlement does not extinguish exposure under separate municipal or state algorithmic-pricing bans, owners operating in jurisdictions with those statutes on the books should confirm compliance with each framework independently rather than treating one resolution as covering the field.
