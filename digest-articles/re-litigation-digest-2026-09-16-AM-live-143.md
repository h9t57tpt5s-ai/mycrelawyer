# $644.7 Million Verdict Over a Bar's Second-Story Staircase Lands as One of the Largest Premises-Liability Awards on Record

### An Orange County, Florida jury's award against the owners and operator of a Winter Park bar puts a spotlight on how commercial landlords and tenants alike can be exposed when a building's own architecture creates a foreseeable hazard.

A jury in Orange County, Florida has returned a verdict of more than $644.7 million against the ownership and operating entities behind Park Social, a second-story bar and lounge in Winter Park, after a patron who fell down an interior staircase in 2017 was left partially quadriplegic. The award — reported at $644,751,855.08 — is among the largest premises-liability verdicts ever recorded in the United States, and it arrives as commercial property owners and operators nationwide are already contending with rising general-liability insurance costs and an increasingly aggressive plaintiffs' bar in the premises-liability space.

The case arose from a November 25, 2017 incident in which the plaintiff, Karim Elkasimi, then 57, fell down a flight of roughly 20 stairs while leaving Park Social, a venue occupying the second floor of a 1926-era mixed-use building on Winter Park's Park Avenue corridor. The fall left him with catastrophic, permanently disabling injuries, including partial quadriplegia. Elkasimi sued the entities that owned and operated the property and the bar — identified in court filings as Soho WP and BE-1 Concept Holdings — alleging that the staircase he used to exit the building was unreasonably dangerous and that the defendants knew, or should have known, about the hazard well before his fall.

At trial, Elkasimi's attorneys built their case around the staircase's physical design and the building's own internal safety practices. Evidence presented to the jury showed the stairs the plaintiff used were narrower and steeper than code-conforming egress stairs typically are, and that the treads lacked any grip tape or comparable slip-resistant surface. Perhaps most damaging to the defense, plaintiffs' counsel introduced evidence that a second, safer staircase existed elsewhere in the building — but that it had been restricted to employees only, and was not made available to the general public until the day after Elkasimi's fall. That sequence of facts allowed the jury to conclude that the property's owners and operators had a safer alternative readily at hand and chose not to extend it to patrons until after someone was catastrophically injured using the more dangerous option.

The jury's award, returned on March 12, 2026, combined compensation for past and future pain and suffering, lost earning capacity, and other economic and non-economic losses. Verdicts of this size in a premises-liability case — as opposed to a mass-tort or class-action context — are exceedingly rare, and legal commentators tracking Florida verdicts have already flagged it as one of the largest personal-injury awards of the year, a status it retains as of this writing more than five months after the verdict was announced.

For commercial property owners, the legal significance of the case lies less in the dollar figure than in the fact pattern the jury credited. Premises-liability law in Florida, as in most states, turns on whether a property owner or operator breached a duty of reasonable care to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition for invitees, and whether that breach foreseeably caused the plaintiff's injury. A building's compliance with the building code in effect when it was constructed — this structure dated to 1926, long before modern stair-width, riser-height, and slip-resistance standards existed — does not automatically satisfy that duty when the property is later put to commercial use inviting the general public. Nor does simply having a safer means of egress available insulate an owner if that safer option was not actually accessible to the people who needed it.

The case is also a pointed illustration of how tenant improvements and operational decisions inside a leased space can create liability exposure that extends well beyond the tenant itself. Both the property-owning entity and the bar's operating entity were named and held liable, underscoring that landlords cannot simply delegate away responsibility for a structural feature of the building — like a staircase — by leasing the space to an operating tenant. Where a landlord retains any degree of control over, or knowledge of, a dangerous condition inherent to the building itself, that landlord can be swept into liability alongside the tenant who operates the business on top of it.

Post-trial proceedings in cases of this magnitude are typically extensive. Defendants facing a verdict of this size commonly pursue post-trial motions challenging the sufficiency of the evidence or the size of the award, and may seek remittitur — a court-ordered reduction of an award found to be excessive — before any appeal is filed. Because the reported figure includes a substantial non-economic damages component, it is a natural candidate for exactly that kind of post-trial scrutiny, and the final enforceable judgment, if any, may look considerably different from the jury's initial number by the time the case is fully resolved.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial landlords and operating tenants sharing a building should jointly audit any pre-code or grandfathered architectural features — staircases in particular — that remain in daily public use, rather than assuming code-compliance-at-construction is a durable legal shield. Owners should review internal policies that restrict safer building features (alternate stairs, elevators, accessible routes) to staff or select tenants, since evidence that a safer option existed but was withheld from the public can be highly damaging at trial. Because landlord and tenant liability can attach jointly for a structural hazard, lease indemnification and insurance-procurement clauses should be revisited to ensure coverage limits are adequate for a catastrophic-injury claim, not just a routine slip-and-fall. Finally, general-liability and excess/umbrella carriers should be looped in early on any known stairway, egress, or historic-building hazard, given how significantly a verdict like this one can reshape a carrier's view of a shared risk.
