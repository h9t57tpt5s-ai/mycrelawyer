# Wells Fargo Moves to Auction Rockpoint's $806M Boston Tower, Testing the Bottom of the Office Market

### A 32-story Financial District trophy bought at the market's peak is headed to a foreclosure auction that will put a hard number on how far downtown Boston office values have actually fallen.

Wells Fargo has scheduled an October 20, 2026 foreclosure auction for 100 Summer Street, a 32-story, roughly 1.3-million-square-foot office tower in Boston's Financial District owned by Rockpoint Group, according to an auction notice published by Sullivan & Sullivan Auctioneers and reported by the Boston Globe, The Real Deal, Banker & Tradesman, and other outlets this week. The sale, run under Massachusetts' non-judicial foreclosure-by-power-of-sale procedure rather than through a court proceeding, is being described by market participants as the largest Boston office foreclosure auction in more than a decade, eclipsing last year's sale of the 1.1-million-square-foot One Lincoln Street.

The numbers illustrate the scale of the reset. Rockpoint, a Boston-based real estate private equity firm, paid $806 million for the tower in October 2019, financing the purchase with a $470 million mortgage from Wells Fargo. That deal closed just months before the pandemic upended office demand. The building, developed in 1980 and now roughly 72% leased, carries a current municipal assessment of approximately $425 million — nearly half of what Rockpoint paid seven years ago and comfortably below the outstanding loan balance. For a lender or CMBS certificate holder measuring recovery prospects, that gap between the original purchase price, the loan balance, and today's assessed value is the entire story: the collateral is worth meaningfully less than what was borrowed against it, a pattern that has become familiar across gateway-city office towers financed in the years preceding the 2022 rate cycle.

Massachusetts is one of a number of states that permits foreclosure of commercial mortgages by power of sale, meaning a lender with the requisite contractual authority can proceed directly to a public auction after providing statutorily required notice, without first obtaining a judicial foreclosure judgment. That procedural posture is significant for owners and lenders elsewhere to understand: it compresses the timeline between a loan default (or, more precisely here, the lender's decision to enforce against the collateral) and an actual change of ownership, relative to judicial-foreclosure states like New York or Illinois where a foreclosure lawsuit, a receivership fight, and a court-supervised sale process can stretch on for a year or more. A bidder at the October 20 auction will need a $10 million deposit in escrow the day before the sale — itself a signal of the scale of capital this transaction is expected to attract, even at a fraction of the tower's original purchase price.

For office owners and lenders more broadly, the auction is a live data point in an argument that has mostly been fought over appraisals and index numbers: what is a large, partially-leased, class-A tower in a gateway financial district actually worth in an arm's-length transaction today. Foreclosure auctions of this size are relatively rare, and when they do happen, the clearing price becomes an immediate reference point for appraisers, special servicers, and borrowers negotiating loan modifications on comparable assets nationwide. If the tower sells at or near its $425 million assessed value, or below it, that outcome will harden comparable-sales arguments for the next round of maturing office CMBS loans; if a strategic or opportunistic buyer pays materially more, it could support the more optimistic camp's view that quality assets can still find capital even as loans against them fail. Either way, the sale price will matter well beyond 100 Summer Street itself.

The transaction also underscores a structural point for private equity sponsors that acquired trophy assets on aggressive leverage just before a market dislocation: a $470 million loan against an $806 million purchase price was, at origination, a conservative roughly 58% loan-to-value structure by conventional underwriting standards. That the collateral has nonetheless fallen far enough to make the loan effectively unrecoverable in full illustrates how severely office valuations have moved, independent of how prudently a given deal was leveraged at the outset. Owners holding similarly structured loans on office assets originated in 2018-2021 should not assume that a historically conservative loan-to-value ratio at origination provides meaningful protection against a maturity default or a lender's decision to foreclose once current market value has fallen this far.

## Practical takeaways

- Non-judicial power-of-sale foreclosure, available in Massachusetts and several other states, can move from default to a completed auction far faster than judicial foreclosure — owners and lenders operating across multiple jurisdictions should track which procedural regime governs each asset in their portfolio.
- A large, publicly noticed foreclosure auction produces a real transaction price that appraisers, servicers, and opposing counsel will cite in every subsequent office-loan workout or valuation dispute — sponsors negotiating a modification on a comparable asset should anticipate this auction's outcome being raised against them.
- Conservative loan-to-value ratios at origination are not, by themselves, protection against a maturity or valuation-driven default years later if the underlying market experiences a large enough repricing.
- Bidders and observers should watch the October 20 clearing price as an early read on institutional appetite for large, partially-leased class-A office towers in gateway central business districts.
