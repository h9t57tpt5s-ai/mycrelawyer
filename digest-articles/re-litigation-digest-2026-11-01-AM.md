# Bank Wins $46M Final Judgment, Moves to Force Sheriff's Sale of Stalled Mission Gateway Site

### A Kansas bank's three-year foreclosure fight over a vacant former mall site has produced a final judgment and a request to put the property up for public auction

After more than three years of litigation and two decades of failed redevelopment attempts, the long-vacant Mission Gateway site in Mission, Kansas — formerly home to Mission Mall — is a step closer to changing hands. On October 28, 2026, Metropolitan Commercial Bank's attorneys filed papers with the Johnson County, Kansas district court clerk asking the court to order a sheriff's sale of the property, after the owner, Aryeh Realty LLC, failed to pay off a final judgment of more than $46 million by an October 16 deadline.

The judgment itself, entered October 2, 2026 by Johnson County District Court Judge Robert Wonnell, is the formal culmination of a case Metropolitan Commercial Bank filed back in April 2023. The bank, through counsel, sued Aryeh Realty — a subsidiary of the New York-based Cameron Group, which has owned the roughly 20-acre Mission Gateway parcel for years — alleging default on a loan originally made in 2019. According to reporting on the judgment, Aryeh now owes the bank approximately $24 million in unpaid principal plus more than $20 million in accrued interest, for a total judgment north of $46 million on what was originally a loan in the range of $24 to $26 million. Judge Wonnell had already ruled from the bench in December 2025 that the bank could proceed with a foreclosure sale; last month's written order made that ruling final and started the clock on Aryeh's opportunity to redeem the debt before the bank could move to a forced sale.

That opportunity has now lapsed. With the October 16 payoff deadline passed, the bank's foreclosure counsel has asked the Johnson County Sheriff to schedule a public sale of the property, which under Kansas foreclosure procedure must be open to any bidder and ultimately confirmed by the court before a new owner can take title. No sale date has yet been set.

The case is a reminder of how slowly a stalled commercial redevelopment site can move through the courts even once a lender has secured every procedural victory along the way: judgment, sale authorization, and a payoff deadline each took months to work through, and the sheriff's sale itself — and any post-sale confirmation fight — still lies ahead. It is also a cautionary tale for municipalities and prospective developers who stake redevelopment plans on a property still working its way through someone else's foreclosure docket. The City of Mission disclosed in early October that it had reached an agreement contemplating a sale of the site to a locally based firm, R.H. Johnson Company, but the city has been careful to caveat that any such deal depends entirely on how the foreclosure resolves — including who ultimately buys the property at sheriff's sale and on what terms.

For lenders holding distressed loans secured by long-vacant or functionally obsolete commercial sites, the Mission Gateway case illustrates both the leverage and the limits of a foreclosure judgment: a lender can eventually force a sale, but a borrower's inaction (or an underlying ownership structure uninterested in redeeming the debt) can let a property sit in limbo through a full judgment-to-sale cycle measured in years, not months. For a municipality or a would-be buyer circling a distressed asset, the lesson cuts the other way — a letter of intent or a redevelopment agreement signed before a sheriff's sale is, at best, conditional, since the sale process itself (and any priority dispute among the lender, guarantors, and contractors who may have liens of their own against the property) can still upend the assumed outcome. Johnson County's Mission Gateway saga, twenty years in the making, is not over yet, but the October judgment and the bank's subsequent sheriff's-sale request mark its clearest movement toward resolution in years.

**Practical takeaways:**
- A foreclosure final judgment is not the end of the collection process: the borrower's redemption window, a sheriff's-sale request, the auction itself, and court confirmation of the winning bid are each separate steps that can each take additional time.
- Redevelopment agreements or acquisition interest expressed by a third party (here, a city and a prospective local buyer) remain contingent until the underlying foreclosure sale actually closes and is confirmed by the court — municipalities should treat such interest as provisional, not as a settled transaction.
- Guarantors, affiliated holding entities, and any contractors or subcontractors who recorded liens against a long-stalled project can complicate the priority of claims to sale proceeds, and parties monitoring a foreclosure should track whether a priority/disbursement dispute is likely once a sale closes.
- Lenders financing long-term redevelopment sites should build loan covenants and maturity terms around the realistic possibility that a borrower may simply let a property sit through default and foreclosure rather than redeem, extending the effective timeline for recovery well beyond a loan's stated maturity.
