# Boston's "Add-Back" Assessment Practice Faces Mounting Proof in Commercial Tax Retaliation Suit

### Newly disclosed city records confirm assessments rose on 150 buildings after their owners appealed — deepening a class action that tests how far a municipality can go in valuing property under the shadow of litigation

A class action accusing the City of Boston of punishing commercial property owners for appealing their tax bills has moved from allegation to documentation. In discovery produced this month, the city's own Assessing Department records confirm that assessments increased on 150 commercial properties — in 182 separate instances — after their owners filed appeals with the state Appellate Tax Board (ATB) between fiscal years 2023 and 2025, according to data reported by Banker & Tradesman on August 18. The disputed increases total roughly $19 million in additional taxes, the first hard confirmation of a pattern the plaintiffs first alleged in a Suffolk Superior Court complaint filed last December.

The case, brought by the owner of 148 State Street — an 11-story office building in Boston's Financial District — with backing from the Pioneer New England Legal Foundation (PNELF) and counsel from Sullivan & Worcester, targets what the complaint calls the city's "Add-Back Policy." According to the suit, Boston's Assessing Department systematically overrode its own valuation software, known as CAMS, for properties with an open ATB appeal, manually reinserting the higher prior-year assessed value through a "discretionary adjustment" field rather than accepting the lower value the software otherwise generated. The practical effect, plaintiffs argue, was to penalize owners financially in the same fiscal year they exercised their statutory right to challenge a valuation — precisely the years an appeal was pending.

The complaint asserts four claims: that the city failed to determine "fair cash value" as required under Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 59, Section 2A(a); that the practice violates the First Amendment by retaliating against owners for petitioning a government tribunal; that it denies free access to the courts under Articles XI and XVI of the Massachusetts Declaration of Rights; and that it breaches the state constitution's requirement that property within a class be taxed proportionally and equally. The suit seeks a declaration that the Add-Back Policy is unlawful, an injunction barring its continued use, and repayment of the excess taxes collected in fiscal years 2024 and 2025.

The city moved to dismiss, arguing the plaintiffs had not exhausted their administrative remedies before the ATB. In a ruling issued in May, Business Litigation Session Judge Peter B. Krupp of the Massachusetts Superior Court rejected that argument and allowed the case to proceed to limited discovery on the "existence and extent" of the alleged policy. Krupp's order was notably pointed: the mere existence of an Add-Back Policy, he wrote, "plausibly chills all commercial property owners from exercising their right to seek an abatement" — a finding that treats the deterrent effect on the broader ownership class, not just the named plaintiff, as legally cognizable harm. Weeks later, Nicholas Ariniello, the city's Commissioner of Assessing for the past seven years and a 20-year veteran of the department, announced his departure from City Hall, with the transition landing squarely amid the litigation.

For commercial owners and their counsel, the case is significant because it has now cleared the pleading stage on constitutional theories — First Amendment petition-clause retaliation and the state's uniformity-of-taxation guarantee — that could migrate well beyond Massachusetts. Many jurisdictions nationally are facing waves of commercial valuation appeals driven by post-pandemic office vacancy and softening values, and assessors under fiscal pressure have incentives to resist erosion of the tax base. A judicially sanctioned theory that punitive re-assessment following an appeal is independently actionable — not merely evidence in the underlying valuation dispute — gives owners a second front, distinct from the abatement proceeding itself, in jurisdictions with comparable constitutional protections. PNELF has separately pressed the Massachusetts Department of Revenue to investigate Boston's valuation practices; the Commissioner of Revenue has so far declined, saying his office could not substantiate the claims and does not advocate for individual taxpayers — leaving the Superior Court litigation as the primary vehicle for scrutiny.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial owners pursuing property tax appeals should preserve documentation — assessor notices, property record cards, and correspondence — that ties any post-appeal valuation change to the timing of their ATB or board filing, since it was aggregated, discoverable data showing a citywide pattern, not one owner's anecdote, that carried this case past dismissal. Assessing departments and their counsel elsewhere should audit whether informal "override" or "discretionary adjustment" fields in valuation software create a paper trail that could support an inference of retaliatory intent, even where individual adjustments were made in good faith. Owners and REITs holding property across the roughly 150 identified buildings — and any similarly situated owner in a jurisdiction with an open ATB or equivalent appeal — should watch the case's discovery period, which in a typical Business Litigation Session matter runs eight months to a year, and any subsequent class-certification ruling, since those milestones will determine whether real recovery follows the finding that the policy is actionable. Finally, in-house real estate and tax teams should treat the constitutional theories here as portable: a state's uniformity or equal-taxation clause, paired with a First Amendment retaliation claim, is a template that could be raised against comparable practices in other high-value urban office markets.
