# CMBS Trustee Hits West Loop's 540 W. Madison With $310 Million Foreclosure Suit

### A bondholder trustee moved to foreclose on a nearly fully leased, 1.15-million-square-foot Chicago office tower after its senior loan matured unpaid in September, underscoring how even cash-flowing office assets are being tested by maturity walls at today's valuations.

A trustee representing bondholders on the senior mortgage loan secured by 540 W. Madison Street, a 1.15-million-square-foot office tower in Chicago's West Loop, filed a $310 million foreclosure lawsuit in the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, against the ownership entity, 540 West Madison Owner LLC. The suit, reported October 27, 2026 by Crain's Chicago Business and The Real Deal, says the loan was not repaid when it reached its stated maturity in September 2026, triggering the default underlying the foreclosure action.

What makes the filing notable is that it targets a building that, by most conventional measures, is performing. The tower is roughly 81% leased, and according to loan-servicing figures cited in the reporting, the property generated more than $18 million in net cash flow against a $5.3 million annual debt-service obligation on the senior loan — a debt-service coverage ratio most lenders would consider healthy. The default here is not a cash-flow default but a maturity default: the loan simply came due and the ownership group was unable, or unwilling, to refinance or pay it off at the stated amount.

The building has a well-documented capital history. A group of New York investors bought the property from Bank of America in December 2012 for roughly $350 million, and the ownership, led by Boca Raton-based Third Millennium Group, refinanced the asset with a $325 million loan in 2016 that was reportedly underwritten against an appraised value of around $591 million at the time. Crain's reporting on the new foreclosure suit notes that recent sales of comparable downtown Chicago office towers suggest the building's current market value has fallen well short of that 2016 appraisal — a gap that, if accurate, would leave the ownership entity underwater on the loan even though the building itself continues to throw off substantial cash flow from its existing tenant roster.

That dynamic — a well-leased, cash-generating asset nonetheless facing foreclosure because it cannot refinance a legacy loan sized to a since-evaporated valuation — has become one of the defining patterns of the current office-lending cycle. Lenders and special servicers are increasingly willing to pursue foreclosure on maturity defaults even where a borrower can continue servicing debt at the old loan's interest rate, because the real question is whether the borrower can refinance or repay the full principal balance at maturity. Office towers financed at the market peak of the early-to-mid 2020s are proving especially exposed, since replacement financing today is typically available only at a materially lower loan-to-value ratio than the original note assumed.

For owners, lenders, and asset managers tracking the office-distress cycle, the 540 W. Madison filing is a reminder that occupancy and operating performance are no longer reliable predictors of default risk. A building can be meaningfully leased and cash-flow positive and still face a lender's foreclosure complaint if it cannot clear a maturity hurdle sized to pre-reset valuations. That reality is reshaping how special servicers and CMBS trustees approach workout negotiations: a borrower's argument that "the building is performing" carries less weight than it once did when the real impediment is a valuation gap between what a loan assumed and what the asset is worth today.

The case also illustrates the layered capital stack risk facing office sponsors who refinanced at the top of the market. A $325 million loan underwritten against a $591 million valuation implied roughly 55% leverage at origination — a level that would ordinarily be considered conservative. If current market pricing has in fact fallen well below that appraisal, as the foreclosure filing and surrounding reporting suggest, the loan-to-value ratio today could be approaching or exceeding 100%, leaving the sponsor with little practical incentive to contribute fresh equity to cure the default rather than negotiate a deed-in-lieu or let the lender take the keys through foreclosure.

Commercial property owners, developers, and their counsel should treat this filing as a data point in an ongoing, market-wide recalibration: a maturity default on a performing asset is not a sign of mismanagement so much as a sign of how far office valuations have moved since the mid-2020s refinancing wave, and owners approaching their own loan maturities should stress-test refinancing feasibility well before the maturity date rather than assume continued occupancy and cash flow will be enough to carry a loan across the finish line. Lenders, in turn, should expect more of these filings on assets that look healthy on an operating basis but were financed against valuations the market no longer supports, and should prepare receivership and foreclosure strategies accordingly rather than assume workout negotiations alone will resolve the gap. Sponsors facing a similar maturity wall should begin refinancing or recapitalization discussions with existing and prospective lenders well in advance of a loan's maturity date, document the asset's operating performance thoroughly to support any workout negotiation, and recognize that a lender's willingness to foreclose on a cash-flowing building has measurably increased in this cycle.
