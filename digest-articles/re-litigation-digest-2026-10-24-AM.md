# Yardi's Rent-Pricing Algorithm Survives Summary Judgment in Multifamily Antitrust Suit

### A federal judge in Seattle has denied Yardi Systems' bid to end a proposed class action alleging its revenue-management software let competing landlords coordinate rents, sending the three-year-old case into a fuller round of discovery.

*Prepared as a legal news update, October 24, 2026.*

A federal judge in the Western District of Washington has denied Yardi Systems, Inc.'s motion for summary judgment in a proposed class action accusing the property-management software giant of enabling an illegal rent-fixing scheme among multifamily landlords, clearing the way for the case to proceed through a broader round of discovery. The October 13 ruling, issued by U.S. District Judge Robert S. Lasnik in *Duffy v. Yardi Systems, Inc.*, No. 2:23-cv-01391 (W.D. Wash.), is the latest in a string of setbacks for Yardi since the suit was filed in September 2023, and it arrives as algorithmic rent-pricing litigation against property-management software vendors continues to spread across the multifamily sector.

The case, brought by named plaintiffs McKenna Duffy and Michael Brett on behalf of a proposed nationwide class of renters, centers on Yardi's RENTmaximizer product, later rebranded as Revenue IQ. Plaintiffs allege that Yardi's software ingested non-public pricing, occupancy, and lease-term data from competing landlords who licensed the platform, then used that pooled information to recommend rents back to each landlord-client — effectively functioning as a hub for horizontal price coordination among competitors who would otherwise set rents independently. The theory echoes the algorithmic-pricing antitrust claims that have proliferated against RealPage, Yardi's larger rival in the revenue-management software market, in a separate multidistrict litigation pending in the Middle District of Tennessee.

Judge Lasnik's October 13 order is procedural rather than a merits verdict, but it is significant precisely because of what it declines to do: end the case before a jury or further fact-finding can test the plaintiffs' theory. In denying summary judgment, the court found that segmented discovery — an earlier arrangement that paused broader fact-finding against the dismissed landlord-defendants while the parties developed the record specific to Yardi — did not eliminate the need for a fuller discovery record before the remaining claims could be resolved. The same order also sealed certain Yardi confidential business information from the public docket and denied both sides' competing motions to exclude expert testimony, leaving those experts' opinions in play for trial or a later summary-judgment attempt.

The ruling builds on two earlier defeats for Yardi. In December 2024, Judge Lasnik denied the company's motion to dismiss outright, holding that the plaintiffs had plausibly alleged a per se unlawful antitrust conspiracy — a notable holding because per se treatment dispenses with the more exacting burden plaintiffs otherwise face under the antitrust "rule of reason," and it places Yardi's case within an emerging, contested body of law on how courts should categorize algorithmic pricing-recommendation tools. In March 2026, the court trimmed the case on jurisdictional grounds, dismissing ten out-of-state landlord co-defendants — among them Banyan Living Ohio LLC, Envolve Communities LLC, Grubb Properties LLC, Habitat Co. LLC, and McWhinney Property Management LLC — for lack of personal jurisdiction in Washington, leaving Yardi as the central remaining defendant along with any landlord-defendants with sufficient Washington contacts.

For commercial real estate owners and operators, the case is a reminder that the legal exposure created by algorithmic revenue-management software does not run only to the handful of companies, such as RealPage, that have drawn the most public attention. Any multifamily owner or manager that licenses a platform relying on pooled, non-public competitor data to generate pricing recommendations faces the same basic antitrust theory, regardless of which vendor's product it uses. The December 2024 per se ruling in this case, if it holds up through further proceedings, would make that theory meaningfully easier for plaintiffs to prove than a traditional rule-of-reason antitrust claim, since plaintiffs would not need to show the challenged conduct's anticompetitive effects outweigh its procompetitive justifications.

The case also illustrates how quickly algorithmic-pricing antitrust theories can expand once a court accepts the underlying legal framework. What began as a single vendor-specific dispute has now generated a growing docket of parallel and follow-on suits — including local-ordinance-based claims in jurisdictions such as Philadelphia that have separately enacted algorithmic rent-fixing bans naming Yardi as a co-defendant alongside landlord operators. Owners, asset managers, and in-house real estate counsel should treat this as an active and evolving risk area: audit existing revenue-management vendor contracts and data-sharing arrangements for the degree to which pricing recommendations rely on pooled competitor data, track the growing patchwork of state and local algorithmic-pricing ordinances in markets where the company operates, and watch how the per se versus rule-of-reason question is resolved on the merits, since that determination will shape exposure far beyond this particular case.

**Practical takeaways:**
- Any owner or manager licensing algorithmic revenue-management software — not only RealPage — faces the same antitrust theory tested in this case, and should audit how its vendor's pricing recommendations use pooled competitor data.
- Judge Lasnik's December 2024 holding that the claims plausibly allege a per se antitrust violation, if it survives further proceedings, substantially lowers plaintiffs' burden compared with a rule-of-reason claim.
- Personal-jurisdiction defenses succeeded for ten out-of-state landlord co-defendants, underscoring that where and how a company does business in the forum state can determine whether it remains in this kind of suit at all.
- Expect the discovery record now being built in this case to shape not only its own trajectory but the broader wave of algorithmic-pricing litigation against other revenue-management vendors.
