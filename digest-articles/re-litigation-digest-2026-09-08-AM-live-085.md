# Appellate Court Reminds Lenders: CPLR 3215(c)'s One-Year Clock Is Mandatory, Not a Suggestion

### New York's Second Department wipes out a U.S. Bank foreclosure claim against a defaulted mortgagor because the bank waited nearly three years to ask for a default judgment

A commercial mortgage foreclosure action that began in 2017 ended this summer not with a judgment of foreclosure and sale, but with a dismissal — one that turned entirely on the calendar rather than the merits. On August 26, 2026, the Appellate Division, Second Department, modified a Supreme Court, Queens County order and held that U.S. Bank National Association had abandoned its foreclosure claim against defendant Black Plaza Management, LLC by waiting far too long to move for a default judgment after Black Plaza failed to answer the complaint. The ruling is a procedural one, not a substantive one, but for commercial mortgage lenders, loan servicers, and the counsel who manage foreclosure dockets on their behalf, it is exactly the kind of decision that deserves attention: a claim with an otherwise valid mortgage and an undisputed default was thrown out solely because the plaintiff let a filing deadline lapse.

U.S. Bank commenced the underlying action in March 2017, seeking to foreclose a mortgage encumbering real property in Jackson Heights, Queens. Black Plaza Management, one of several named defendants, never answered the complaint or otherwise appeared in the litigation, except later to contest the very motion practice at issue in this appeal. Under New York's CPLR 3215(c), once a defendant defaults, a plaintiff has exactly one year from that default to take proceedings toward entry of judgment. If the plaintiff fails to do so, and cannot show "sufficient cause" for the delay, the court is required to dismiss the claim as abandoned. The statute uses mandatory language — courts "shall" dismiss — and New York's appellate courts have consistently treated it that way, applying the rule strictly even against institutional plaintiffs pursuing large secured claims.

That is precisely what tripped up U.S. Bank here. According to the Second Department's decision, the bank did not move for a default judgment against Black Plaza until December 2019, well over two years after Black Plaza's default and roughly a year and a half beyond CPLR 3215(c)'s one-year window. A four-judge panel of the Second Department sided with Black Plaza, finding that the bank had failed to demonstrate it took any timely proceedings toward judgment within the statutory year, and that its proffered explanations for the delay did not amount to the "sufficient cause" the statute requires. The panel modified the Supreme Court, Queens County order to dismiss the foreclosure claim against Black Plaza as abandoned and awarded costs to the company.

The decision is unremarkable doctrinally — CPLR 3215(c) has generated a substantial and fairly consistent body of Second Department case law over the years, much of it arising in the residential foreclosure context where large mortgage servicers manage enormous dockets and inevitably let some files languish. What makes this ruling worth flagging for a commercial and institutional audience is the reminder that the same mandatory, no-exceptions rule applies with equal force outside the residential space. A defaulted commercial mortgagor, an LLC holding a single investment property, benefits from exactly the same one-year fuse as a homeowner facing foreclosure on a primary residence. Loan servicers and foreclosure counsel who treat commercial files as lower-priority than high-volume residential dockets, or who assume that a strong underlying claim will excuse administrative delay, are working against a statute that does not distinguish between the two.

Courts applying CPLR 3215(c) have recognized only a narrow set of excuses as "sufficient cause" for missing the one-year deadline — law office failure alone is generally insufficient, and generic assertions about docket volume or servicing transfers tend to fare no better. Plaintiffs who can point to genuine, documented obstacles, such as an intervening bankruptcy stay, active settlement negotiations reflected in the record, or a defendant's own conduct that delayed proceedings, have occasionally survived dismissal motions. But the default rule assumes abandonment once the clock runs out, and the burden sits with the plaintiff to rebut that presumption with specifics, not generalities.

For CMBS trustees, portfolio lenders, and the special servicers and law firms who manage foreclosure pipelines on their behalf, the practical takeaways are straightforward. Foreclosure counsel should calendar the CPLR 3215(c) deadline the moment a defendant's default is established, not when convenient, and should build in an internal alert well before the one-year mark to confirm that a default judgment motion, or some other qualifying proceeding, has actually been filed. Lenders should periodically audit stalled or inactive foreclosure files across their servicing portfolios, particularly matters that predate a servicing transfer or a change of outside counsel, since institutional memory of a filing deadline is exactly what tends to get lost in those handoffs. And where a delay is unavoidable, whether from a bankruptcy stay, ongoing loss-mitigation talks, or litigation over lien priority, counsel should contemporaneously document the reason in the file, because a court applying CPLR 3215(c) will ask for specifics years later, and a thin record is what turned a fully securitized mortgage claim into a dismissed one here.
