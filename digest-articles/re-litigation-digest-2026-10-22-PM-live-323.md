# Ohio Appeals Court Reverses Itself, Revives Westlake's Challenge to Crocker Park's Right of First Refusal

### On reconsideration, the Eighth District vacates its own July decision and holds Crocker Park may have waited too long to exercise a contractual right against its municipal co-owner

The Ohio Court of Appeals for the Eighth Appellate District has taken the unusual step of vacating its own, three-month-old decision and issuing a new one that hands the City of Westlake a win it did not get the first time around. In *Crocker Park, L.L.C. v. City of Westlake, Ohio* (8th Dist. Cuyahoga No. 115356, 2026-Ohio-4138), the court held on reconsideration that the trial court erred when it declared Crocker Park had not waived its right of first refusal over a parcel within the Crocker Park development — reversing that portion of the judgment and sending the issue back to the Cuyahoga County Court of Common Pleas, even as it left most of the rest of the trial court's rulings intact.

Crocker Park is a roughly 75-acre mixed-use development in Westlake, Ohio, combining retail, restaurants, offices, apartments, and green space across multiple contiguous parcels. The project's various owners are bound by a 2003 "Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, Restrictions and Easements," which designates Crocker Park, L.L.C. as the "Declarant" responsible for maintaining shared Common Areas and allocating Common Expenses among the parcel owners. In March 2014, Crocker Park conveyed roughly two acres within the development to the City of Westlake at no cost, in exchange for the City's agreement to build a public gathering space — "Market Square" — at its own expense. Critically, the City accepted the land subject to the Declaration's existing covenants, making Westlake an "Owner" within the development's governance structure like any private landholder.

Market Square opened in 2015, and Crocker Park Management, L.L.C. — a Crocker Park subsidiary acting as the project's exclusive property manager and leasing agent for Stark Enterprises, Inc. — operated and maintained it for the City under a separate Operation and Maintenance Agreement, fronting out-of-pocket costs when Market Square's own revenues fell short. The City terminated that arrangement in December 2023. What followed was a lawsuit covering several intertwined disputes: how Common Area expenses should be allocated between Crocker Park and the City as a fellow Owner, counterclaims by Westlake alleging slander of title and abuse of process against Crocker Park and its manager, and a fight over whether Crocker Park properly preserved — or instead let lapse — a contractual right of first refusal tied to the development.

The trial court had granted Crocker Park and Crocker Park Management partial summary judgment on multiple fronts, including defeating Westlake's slander-of-title and abuse-of-process counterclaims and declaring that Crocker Park had not waived its right of first refusal. Westlake appealed, and in July 2026 the Eighth District issued its first decision affirming in part and reversing in part. But the panel then reconsidered. In the opinion it released October 22, 2026 — authored by Presiding Judge Eileen T. Gallagher and expressly vacating the July 30 decision — the court reaffirmed the rulings in Crocker Park's favor on the slander-of-title and abuse-of-process counterclaims, agreeing the trial court properly granted summary judgment to Crocker Park and its manager on those claims. But on the right of first refusal, the panel changed course: it held that Crocker Park failed to exercise the right within a reasonable time, and that the trial court therefore erred in declaring Crocker Park had not waived it. That piece of the case now returns to the trial court for further proceedings.

The reversal on reconsideration is itself worth noting for anyone litigating commercial real estate covenant disputes in Ohio's appellate courts: a released opinion is not necessarily the last word, and a party that appears to have won outright at the appellate level can still see a key ruling flipped weeks or months later if the losing side successfully moves for reconsideration.

More broadly, the case is a useful illustration of how master-planned, multi-parcel commercial developments function — and malfunction — once a municipality becomes a co-owner inside a private CC&R structure rather than merely a regulator standing outside it. Once Westlake accepted the Market Square parcel subject to the Declaration, it took on the same contractual rights and obligations as any private Owner, including the ability to bring — and defend against — ordinary common-law claims like slander of title and abuse of process, while also becoming subject to the Declarant's contractual rights, like the right of first refusal now back in play.

Practical takeaways for developers, municipal real estate counsel, and asset managers: rights of first refusal and similar contractual options frequently carry an implied "reasonable time" requirement for exercise even without an express deadline in the governing document, and delay can waive the right regardless of good intentions. Governments that accept conveyed land inside a master-planned development should expect to step fully into the shoes of a private Owner under the controlling CC&Rs, gaining enforcement rights but also assuming cost-sharing and procedural obligations. Declarants and development managers overseeing multi-owner common-area arrangements should document the timing of every option exercise and maintenance decision carefully, since ambiguity about "when" a right was or wasn't exercised is precisely the kind of dispute that can survive years of litigation and even outlive a seemingly final appellate ruling. And parties who win at the Court of Appeals should not treat that win as final until the window for reconsideration has closed, since this case shows a panel can and will revisit its own decision when persuaded it got a discrete issue wrong.
