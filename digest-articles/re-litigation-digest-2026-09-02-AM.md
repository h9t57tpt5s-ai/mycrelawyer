# Colorado Springs' Two Tallest Towers Head Toward Foreclosure as $59M Loan Balance Goes Unpaid

### Non-judicial foreclosure notices filed against the entities behind the Wells Fargo Tower and FirstBank Building show post-pandemic office distress reaching trophy addresses well outside the largest CMBS-heavy gateway markets.

Downtown Colorado Springs' two tallest and most recognizable office buildings — the Wells Fargo Tower at 90 S. Cascade Avenue and the neighboring FirstBank Building, together forming the Palmer Center complex — are now the subject of foreclosure proceedings after their ownership group failed to keep current on a loan with an unpaid principal balance of roughly $59 million against an original $74 million facility. El Paso County Public Trustee's Office records show foreclosure notices filed against three affiliated Delaware limited liability companies — RPalmer LLC, Overland Palmer LLC, and Palmer Center Owner LLC — which are controlled by private real estate development, investment, and management companies headquartered in California. Several smaller business-suite properties reachable through either tower or the adjacent, separately owned Antlers hotel are also named in the filings.

The proceeding is unfolding under Colorado's public trustee foreclosure statute, a non-judicial process that differs meaningfully from the court-supervised CMBS foreclosure litigation making headlines in gateway markets like New York and Miami. Rather than a lender filing a complaint and litigating to judgment before a sale can occur, Colorado allows the holder of a defaulted deed of trust to record a notice of election and demand with the county public trustee, triggering a statutory timeline toward a trustee's sale unless the borrower cures the default, reinstates the loan, or otherwise resolves the matter — including through bankruptcy, a negotiated modification, or a deed in lieu. That structure gives distressed borrowers less procedural runway than a judicial foreclosure state would, but it also means the case will generate a public administrative record rather than a docketed lawsuit, at least unless a borrower moves to enjoin the sale in district court, as owners of distressed Colorado office towers — including Denver's Republic Plaza — have done in comparable situations this year.

The Palmer Center default is not an outlier so much as a data point in a broader downtown Colorado Springs office story. Second-quarter 2026 figures put downtown vacancy at 10.4%, with roughly 589,500 square feet of available space, against a citywide office vacancy rate near 12% and about 4.2 million square feet available market-wide. Trophy towers with long operating histories and blue-chip anchor tenants — the Wells Fargo and FirstBank names both reflect long-term banking tenancies rather than mere branding — have generally weathered the post-pandemic office downturn better than commodity suburban product. That a marquee downtown asset like Palmer Center is nonetheless now in default on debt service suggests the erosion in debt service coverage tied to elevated interest rates, softer renewal demand, and compressed net operating income has moved beyond the most obviously troubled assets and into buildings that anchor a city's skyline and downtown identity.

For CRE lenders, special servicers, and borrowers, the filing underscores several recurring dynamics of the current cycle. A loan does not need to be underwater on a mark-to-market valuation basis to default — an inability to refinance or pay down principal at maturity, or a shortfall in ongoing debt service coverage, can trigger a default well before an appraisal reflects meaningful value impairment, particularly for loans originated in a lower-rate environment now facing refinancing at materially higher costs. The public trustee process itself is worth understanding on its own terms: in non-judicial foreclosure states, borrowers often have materially less time and fewer procedural levers to contest a filing than they would in a judicial foreclosure jurisdiction, making early engagement with the lender or special servicer — well before a notice of election and demand is recorded — considerably more valuable than after the clock starts running. Ownership structures matter too: because the foreclosure here reaches multiple affiliated single-purpose entities and ancillary business-suite parcels tied to the same complex, lenders and borrowers alike should expect that a default on a complex's primary loan can sweep in adjacent, separately titled parcels financed under cross-collateralized or cross-defaulted loan documents, a structure common in mixed-use downtown developments assembled parcel by parcel over time. Finally, the case is a reminder that not every entity sharing an address or a marketing identity shares in a default: the Antlers hotel, though physically connected to the Palmer Center towers and often marketed alongside them, is separately owned and is not named in these foreclosure filings, underscoring the importance of precise title and lien diligence before drawing conclusions about a complex's overall financial health from a single component's distress.

### Practical Takeaways

Owners and asset managers holding loans nearing maturity in secondary downtown office markets should treat this filing as confirmation that trophy status and long-tenured anchor tenants no longer guarantee refinancing success in the current rate environment, and should engage lenders on workout or extension terms well before a maturity date arrives rather than after. Because Colorado's public trustee process moves on a statutory clock with limited procedural stops, borrowers facing default in non-judicial foreclosure states should treat the recording of a notice of election and demand as a hard deadline requiring immediate counsel engagement, not an opening bid in a negotiation. Lenders and buyers evaluating distressed mixed-use complexes should confirm which specific parcels and entities are cross-collateralized or cross-defaulted under the loan documents, since a default on one component of a multi-building complex can — or, as here with the ancillary business suites, evidently does — extend to affiliated parcels even where a physically connected building, like the Antlers hotel, remains outside the loan's collateral pool entirely.
