# Fifth Circuit Slams the Door on Arbitration for Domestic Insurers in Louisiana Surplus Lines Property Disputes

### A September 3 ruling in *Transportation Consultants, Inc. v. Certain Underwriters at Lloyd's, London* confirms that Louisiana policyholders can keep their domestic surplus lines insurers in court, even when the same policy sends foreign underwriters to arbitration

Commercial property owners who buy surplus lines coverage rarely stop to ask what happens if their policy is split across a dozen different insurers, some based in the United States and some overseas, and a hurricane forces a coverage fight. On September 3, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit answered that question for Louisiana in *Transportation Consultants, Incorporated v. Certain Underwriters at Lloyd's, London, et al.*, No. 25-30372, holding that an arbitration clause in a surplus lines commercial property policy is unenforceable against the domestic insurers on the policy, even though the identical clause remains enforceable against the foreign insurers subscribing to the very same contract. For any commercial property owner, REIT, or lender relying on a layered or syndicated property insurance program with Louisiana exposure, the decision is a clean roadmap for where a coverage dispute will actually be litigated, and it is a reminder that a single insurance policy can now split into two very different procedural tracks depending on where each underwriter is domiciled.

The underlying facts are unremarkable for coastal Louisiana: Transportation Consultants, Inc. owned commercial property insured under a surplus lines policy subscribed by nine domestic insurance companies and two foreign underwriters at Lloyd's of London. The policy contained a broad arbitration clause requiring virtually any disagreement over the insurance to be resolved outside of court, along with a contract allocation endorsement stating that the policy should be treated as a series of separate bilateral contracts between the policyholder and each individual subscribing insurer, rather than as one contract among many co-insurers. When Hurricane Ida damaged the insured property, a dispute over the scope of coverage followed, and Transportation Consultants sued all eleven insurers together in Louisiana state court. The insurers removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana, invoking the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, commonly known as the New York Convention, as the basis for federal jurisdiction over what was, in substance, an international arbitration dispute because of the Lloyd's syndicates' involvement.

The district court's path to the Fifth Circuit's ruling illustrates just how fact-specific this area of law has become. The court initially granted the insurers' motion to compel arbitration across the board and stayed the entire case. On reconsideration, however, the court reversed course as to the nine domestic insurers, holding that Louisiana Revised Statutes 22:868, which generally voids policy provisions depriving Louisiana courts of jurisdiction over suits against an insurer, barred enforcement of the arbitration clause against insurers domiciled in the United States. The court lifted the litigation stay for those nine defendants while leaving the arbitration order intact for the two Lloyd's underwriters, whose contracts fell within the New York Convention's separate, preemptive framework for international arbitration agreements.

On appeal, the Fifth Circuit affirmed that split result. Following its own recent precedents in *Town of Vinton v. Indian Harbor Insurance Co.* and *Crescent City Surgical Operating Co. v. Interstate Fire & Casualty Co.*, the panel held that the Convention's preemptive reach over arbitration agreements extends only to the contracts between the policyholder and the foreign insurers, not to the parallel contracts, created by the very same policy language and the contract allocation endorsement, between the policyholder and the domestic insurers. Because those domestic contracts are governed by ordinary Louisiana law rather than the Convention, Section 22:868's anti-arbitration rule applies to them in full force. The court also rejected the domestic insurers' fallback argument that equitable estoppel should let them ride along with the enforceable arbitration obligations of their foreign co-subscribers simply because all eleven insurers signed the same policy form, reasoning that estoppel cannot be used to import an arbitration right that Louisiana law affirmatively voids.

The practical significance for commercial real estate owners and their brokers is direct. Louisiana remains one of the few states with a statute that flatly bars mandatory arbitration clauses in insurance contracts, and the Fifth Circuit has now made clear that layering a foreign reinsurer or Lloyd's syndicate into a placement does not launder that protection away for the domestic insurers on the same slip. Any surplus lines program covering Louisiana property that mixes domestic and foreign capacity should be understood, from day one, as creating two separate dispute-resolution tracks that will diverge the moment a claim is contested, one heading to state or federal court against the domestic carriers and one heading to arbitration against the foreign carriers, even though the underlying coverage grant and factual dispute are identical. That bifurcation carries real litigation-management costs, since a policyholder may need to prosecute the same coverage theory in two forums simultaneously, and it gives sophisticated insureds a reason to press their brokers, at the placement stage, for clarity on which subscribing markets are domestic versus foreign before a storm turns the distinction from boilerplate into a jurisdictional battleground.

## Practical Takeaways

- In Louisiana, a surplus lines property policy's arbitration clause is enforceable only against the foreign insurers on the risk; domestic insurers subscribing to the identical policy language remain subject to Louisiana Revised Statutes 22:868's bar on mandatory arbitration, per the Fifth Circuit's September 3, 2026 decision in *Transportation Consultants v. Certain Underwriters at Lloyd's, London*.
- A contract allocation endorsement that treats a syndicated policy as separate bilateral contracts between the insured and each underwriter is exactly what allows this domestic/foreign split to occur, so owners should review that endorsement language closely at renewal.
- Equitable estoppel will not let domestic insurers piggyback on their foreign co-subscribers' arbitration rights merely because they share the same policy form; expect that argument to keep failing in the Fifth Circuit after *Town of Vinton* and *Crescent City Surgical*.
- Owners, asset managers, and their brokers placing layered or syndicated commercial property coverage with Louisiana exposure should confirm, before binding, which subscribing markets are domestic and which are foreign, since a future claim dispute may have to be litigated on two separate tracks at once.
