# Faneuil Hall's New Landlord Presses $2 Million Rent Collection Campaign Against Marketplace Tenants

### A wave of Suffolk Superior Court filings shows how aggressively an incoming owner can move to collect on inherited leases — and what it costs a marketplace's occupancy along the way

When J. Safra Real Estate took over management of Boston's historic Faneuil Hall Marketplace at the start of 2024, it inherited more than a collection of century-old retail buildings — it inherited a set of leases with tenants who, in several cases, had already fallen behind on rent. Over the past several months, that inheritance has turned into litigation: J. Safra has filed a series of breach-of-lease suits in Suffolk County Superior Court seeking more than $2 million in unpaid rent, interest, and attorneys' fees from seven marketplace tenants, the most recent and largest of which targets candy and novelty retailer Sugar Factory for more than $930,000.

The Sugar Factory suit, filed July 10, 2026, is illustrative of the pattern across all seven cases. Sugar Factory's Boston location entered into its lease for roughly 9,000 square feet in the marketplace's South Market Building in 2021, under Faneuil Hall's previous owner, New York-based Ashkenazy Acquisition Corp. According to J. Safra's complaint, the landlord notified Sugar Factory's franchise owner, Lloyd Sugarman, in January 2026 that the tenant owed approximately $913,000 in back rent, and warned that failure to pay the full balance within ten days would constitute a default under the lease. J. Safra alleges Sugar Factory failed to cure within that window, triggering the default and the landlord's right to accelerate the remaining rent obligation, interest, and collection costs — pushing the total demand past $930,000.

The other six suits, filed over roughly the past year, name Imagine Exhibitions Inc., Boston Halal, Popular Properties Realty Inc., Lovepop, and Ghirardelli Chocolate Co. as defendants, together accounting for just under $1 million in additional claimed rent. The cases have not proceeded uniformly: the claim against gift retailer Lovepop was dismissed in April 2026, and Popular Properties Realty has disputed J. Safra's allegations, telling the court it vacated its space because of unresolved property maintenance issues — a defense that, if credited, could complicate the landlord's theory that nonpayment alone justifies the amounts sought. Most of the named tenants have since closed their Faneuil Hall locations; Sugar Factory is the only one still operating in the marketplace as of this writing.

**The legal issue.** On their face, these are conventional commercial lease-enforcement actions: a landlord alleging nonpayment, issuing a notice-and-cure demand consistent with the lease's default provisions, and suing for the accelerated balance once the cure period lapses. What makes the cluster notable is less any individual legal theory than the scale and coordination of the enforcement effort — seven suits against a historic marketplace's roster of small and mid-sized retail tenants, filed by an ownership group that took over the property mid-lease-term and is now systematically working through inherited defaults. For general counsel advising either landlords or tenants in similar multi-tenant retail or mixed-use properties, the litigation underscores several recurring issues: how notice-and-cure provisions get triggered and enforced after a change in ownership, whether a tenant's property-condition grievances can be raised defensively against a straightforward nonpayment claim, and how quickly accelerated-rent exposure can compound once a formal default is declared.

**Practical implications.** For commercial landlords acquiring occupied retail properties, the Faneuil Hall litigation is a reminder to conduct rigorous lease and rent-roll diligence before closing, since incoming owners typically take assignment of existing leases — defaults, disputes, and all — without the benefit of a clean slate. For tenants, particularly those in older or transitioning retail centers, the case is a caution that a change in ownership can bring materially more aggressive collection practices than a prior landlord may have used, and that property-condition complaints should be documented and raised formally, not held in reserve as an informal justification for withholding rent. The marketplace-wide vacancy pattern accompanying these suits also illustrates a broader dynamic in legacy retail destinations: aggressive rent collection can accelerate departures, leaving a landlord holding judgments against tenants who have already left rather than a stabilized, paying tenant base.

**Practical takeaways:**
- Buyers of leased commercial and retail properties should audit rent rolls and tenant payment histories pre-closing, since assigned leases carry existing defaults and disputes forward to the new owner.
- Landlords enforcing notice-and-cure provisions should ensure default notices are unambiguous and properly served, given that acceleration and fee-shifting claims typically hinge on strict compliance with the lease's own cure mechanics.
- Tenants facing rent-collection actions should raise property-condition or landlord-performance defenses formally and early, rather than relying on them informally to justify withholding payment.
- Aggressive post-acquisition rent enforcement can accelerate tenant turnover, a tradeoff owners of legacy retail destinations should weigh against the value of a stable, occupied marketplace.
