# A Fargo Developer's Watertown, South Dakota Apartment Portfolio Unravels Across Two Courts and Two States

### A $22 million loan-default fight between a Minnesota community bank and a multifamily developer, now tangled with a Chapter 7 bankruptcy sale that collapsed mid-transaction, shows how quickly a regional CRE workout can spread across multiple forums.

A multifamily development dispute rooted in Watertown, South Dakota has grown into a sprawling, multi-forum legal fight spanning state foreclosure litigation, a removed-and-remanded federal case, and a Chapter 7 bankruptcy proceeding in neighboring North Dakota — culminating, on August 17, 2026, in a bankruptcy court order that stripped a local developer of a downtown redevelopment site after his own winning bid to reclaim it fell apart. The dispute offers commercial lenders and workout counsel a real-time illustration of how a mid-size regional loan default can metastasize into parallel proceedings that each move on their own timeline and their own rules.

The underlying dispute traces back to a series of promissory notes, totaling more than $22 million, that Red River State Bank of Halstad, Minnesota extended to finance three Watertown apartment developments launched by Fargo-based developer Jesse Craig: Parkside Place, Generations on 1st, and a downtown adaptive-reuse project known as The Ruins. Red River State Bank filed foreclosure lawsuits in South Dakota's Third Judicial Circuit Court in Codington County, naming Generations on 1st, LLC, Craig Holdings, LLC, Watertown Development Company, and Jesse and Mulinda Craig individually as defendants, and alleging that Generations on 1st and Mulinda Craig had defaulted on the notes. In response, the Craigs filed counterclaims against the bank alleging breach of contract, breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and both actual and constructive fraud — allegations that, if credited, would shift at least some of the blame for the projects' distress from the developer back onto the lender's own conduct during origination and servicing.

While the state-court foreclosure and fraud counterclaims have continued to work their way through Codington County (with a related proceeding removed to, and then remanded back from, federal court in South Dakota), a parallel and ultimately more consequential process was unfolding in bankruptcy court. The Ruins, the downtown Watertown project, ended up in a Chapter 7 bankruptcy sale process, with attorney Erik Ahlgren serving as trustee. Craig's own company initially won that sale with a credit bid of roughly $11.265 million, outbidding the next-highest bidder by more than $5 million under a sale order the bankruptcy court entered on June 4, 2026. But the win did not hold. On July 7, 2026, Trustee Ahlgren moved to amend that sale order, seeking to have the court explicitly enumerate sixteen separate mortgages, mechanics' liens, and other claims against the property in order to satisfy South Dakota title-insurance requirements — a sign that the property's lien stack was more tangled than the original order had accounted for. That motion set off a chain of events that ultimately unwound Craig's winning bid entirely.

On August 17, 2026, U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Shon Hastings, Chief Judge of the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of North Dakota, signed a final order authorizing Trustee Ahlgren to convey The Ruins property, located at 315 E. Kemp Avenue in Watertown, to a backup bidder, Archer Land Co., LLC, for approximately $6.1 million — roughly half of what Craig's entity had originally bid. As part of the deal's collapse, Craig forfeited more than $1.1 million in earnest money that had been tied to his company's original winning bid.

The case is instructive precisely because of its procedural sprawl. A single distressed multifamily portfolio generated at least three formally distinct tracks: a state-court foreclosure action testing whether the borrower actually defaulted and whether the lender's own conduct excuses or offsets that default; a related federal removal-and-remand fight over which court gets to decide threshold issues; and a bankruptcy sale process in an entirely different state's federal bankruptcy court, driven by the separate imperative of clearing marketable title through a Chapter 7 trustee rather than by the merits of the underlying loan dispute. For lenders and workout counsel, the bankruptcy track's outcome is a reminder that a credit bid win in a Chapter 7 sale is not final until the trustee and the court have actually cleared title — a sale order entered without a complete lien inventory can be reopened, and a winning bidder who has already begun celebrating can still lose the property to a backup bidder months later.

The fraud and good-faith counterclaims the Craigs have raised against Red River State Bank also deserve attention independent of the bankruptcy sale's outcome. Borrower counterclaims alleging that a lender's own conduct — including, potentially, decisions about further advances, inspections, or default notices — contributed to or caused a project's failure are a recurring feature of CRE loan-default litigation in a higher-rate environment, and they can complicate what might otherwise look like a straightforward foreclosure.

**Practical takeaways:** Regional and community bank lenders financing multi-project developer relationships should confirm, before consenting to any credit-bid sale in a borrower's bankruptcy, that the trustee has a complete and title-insurable lien inventory — an incomplete lien schedule can force a sale order to be reopened months after it is entered. Borrowers and their counsel should treat a Chapter 7 credit-bid win as provisional until title actually clears, and should structure any earnest-money or deposit terms with that risk in mind given how much Craig ultimately forfeited when his bid unwound. Workout counsel on any multi-property developer relationship should map out every forum a dispute could land in at the outset — state foreclosure court, potential federal removal, and bankruptcy court for any project that reaches Chapter 7 — since each forum can move on an independent timeline and produce outcomes that affect the others. Finally, lenders facing borrower counterclaims alleging fraud or bad-faith conduct in loan origination or servicing should preserve underwriting and servicing files early, since those counterclaims can survive and complicate a foreclosure even where the underlying default itself is not seriously disputed.
