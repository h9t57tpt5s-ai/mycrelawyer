# Kaanapali Land Subsidiary Moves to Foreclose Lien on Maui Land Over Unpaid Infrastructure Costs

### KLMC says Newport Hospital Corporation defaulted on a 2014 agreement to help fund roads, sewer, and electrical work tied to the sale of 14.9 acres in West Maui

A long-running infrastructure-cost dispute tied to a 2014 West Maui land sale has turned into an active foreclosure fight, according to a Form 10-Q that publicly traded land company Kaanapali Land, LLC (OTC: KANP) filed with the SEC on November 12, 2026. The filing discloses that the company's subsidiary, Kaanapali Land Management Corp. ("KLMC"), initiated foreclosure proceedings in Maui on September 29, 2026 against Newport Hospital Corporation ("NHC") after NHC failed to pay a receivable owed under a shared infrastructure agreement. NHC responded by moving to stay or dismiss the foreclosure proceeding on November 5, 2026, and the company says there is no assurance KLMC will ultimately prevail or collect what it is owed.

## A Decade-Old Deal Comes Back Into Dispute

The underlying transaction dates to 2014, when KLMC sold NHC approximately 14.9 acres of land in West Maui under a Property and Option Purchase Agreement. As part of that deal, the parties entered into an Infrastructure Improvement Agreement, later amended, under which KLMC committed to fund a share of off-site infrastructure work — roads, sewer, and electrical improvements — serving the broader area, with the company's exposure capped depending on several variables. The agreement in turn created reciprocal contribution obligations, and it is NHC's side of that bargain that is now at the center of the dispute: according to the 10-Q, NHC owes KLMC a receivable tied to the infrastructure work, secured by what the filing calls the "NHC Contribution Lien" against NHC's land.

The company's filing states that after NHC failed and refused to pay the receivable, KLMC gave NHC written notice of default under the Infrastructure Improvement Agreement on September 4, 2026, along with a demand for payment. When NHC did not cure the default, KLMC exercised its contractual right to foreclose on the Contribution Lien and filed a foreclosure complaint in Maui on September 29, 2026. NHC did not simply let the foreclosure proceed; on November 5, 2026, it filed a motion seeking to stay or dismiss the proceeding, which remains pending. Kaanapali Land's filing is candid about the uncertainty ahead, cautioning that there is no assurance the company will prevail in the foreclosure proceeding or that a win would actually result in collecting the receivable and accrued interest.

## Part of a Larger Maui Master Development

The dispute is not an isolated commercial transaction for Kaanapali Land. The company's filing notes that it retains certain approval rights over the uses and design of the NHC parcel under the Infrastructure Improvement Agreement, intended to keep development on the site consistent with Kaanapali's broader master-planned development for the surrounding region — the company says it is in the planning stages for a 295-acre parcel mauka of (upslope from) its Kaanapali Coffee Farms community. That context matters for how the foreclosure fight is likely to play out: a lien foreclosure against a parcel embedded inside a larger master development gives the landowner-plaintiff leverage beyond simply recovering a receivable, since an unresolved dispute over the NHC parcel's infrastructure contributions and permitted uses could complicate planning and permitting decisions across the adjoining acreage.

## Practical Takeaways

Developers who structure a land sale around a shared infrastructure-cost agreement, rather than requiring the buyer's contribution up front, should recognize that enforcing that obligation years later — after the roads, sewer, and electrical work are already built — can mean litigating a contested foreclosure rather than simply collecting a bill, particularly once the buyer disputes the underlying default. A contribution lien is only as useful as the foreclosure remedy behind it, and a motion to stay or dismiss, as NHC has now filed, can extend the collection timeline well past the original notice-and-cure period contemplated in the agreement. For master-plan developers retaining design and use-approval rights over parcels they have already sold off, an unresolved infrastructure-cost dispute with one buyer is worth flagging early in connection with planning and entitlement work on adjacent, still-undeveloped acreage, since the two are not legally separate from a practical standpoint even when they are separate parcels and separate contracts. Counsel drafting infrastructure-cost-sharing or contribution-lien provisions in land sale agreements should also build in realistic assumptions about how long collection could take if a buyer later disputes the amount owed, rather than assuming a lien alone guarantees a straightforward recovery.
