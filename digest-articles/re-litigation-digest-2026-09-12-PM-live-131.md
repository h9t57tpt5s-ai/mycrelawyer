# Credit Union's $17.1M Foreclosure Suit Targets Chicago Office Landlord — and Him Personally

### A South Loop building's loss of its sole government tenant, on the heels of dozens of unresolved code violations, has triggered a foreclosure action that reaches past the LLC and straight at its owner's personal balance sheet.

Jovia Financial Federal Credit Union has filed a $17.1 million foreclosure lawsuit in the Circuit Court of Cook County against S Indiana Avenue LLC, an entity controlled by Lakewood, New Jersey investor Reuven Y. Rivlin, over a 129,200-square-foot office building in Chicago's South Loop neighborhood. The September 4, 2026 complaint, filed in the court's Chancery Division Mortgage Foreclosure Section, arrives after the property's dominant tenant — a state agency housing the Illinois Department of Children and Family Services — terminated its lease for cause, and after a three-month forbearance agreement the credit union had extended to Rivlin lapsed without a resolution in June. For commercial mortgage lenders and borrowers alike, the case is a compact illustration of how a single tenant's exit, layered on top of unresolved deferred maintenance, can convert a performing loan into a foreclosure and personal-guaranty claim within a matter of months.

The property at 1911–1921 South Indiana Avenue is a ten-story building constructed in 1996. Rivlin's venture acquired it on December 31, 2021 for $14 million and, days later, on January 5, 2022, borrowed $17.5 million from Jovia — a Westbury, New York-based credit union — secured by a mortgage on the building. That the loan amount exceeded the purchase price suggests the financing included a cash-out component layered on top of acquisition costs, a structure that left little cushion once the building's finances came under stress. As a condition of the loan, Rivlin personally signed an unlimited guaranty, meaning Jovia's complaint is not confined to recovering the collateral; it seeks to hold Rivlin liable for the full $17.1 million balance regardless of what the building itself ultimately sells for in foreclosure.

The building's fortunes turned on its anchor tenant. The Illinois Department of Central Management Services had leased 129,126 square feet — effectively the entire building — on behalf of DCFS, making the state, in practical terms, the property's only material source of rental income. According to reporting on the case, the building accumulated more than three dozen open municipal code violations in 2024 and failed multiple city inspections, and CMS stopped paying rent after December 2025 before terminating the lease outright, citing severe code violations and structural maintenance deficiencies. With its primary income source gone, S Indiana Avenue LLC was unable to service the debt, and a forbearance arrangement Jovia extended while the parties attempted to work out a resolution expired in June 2026 without a cure, clearing the way for the foreclosure filing three months later.

The case sits squarely within a broader pattern reshaping Chicago's office market: single-tenant or government-anchored office buildings that lose their occupant amid a market where vacancy in the city's central business district has climbed sharply, leaving landlords with few readily available replacement tenants and lenders newly exposed on loans that looked well-covered at origination. What distinguishes this filing from a garden-variety foreclosure is the personal guaranty. Many commercial mortgage borrowers structure ownership through single-purpose LLCs specifically to wall off personal assets from a failed real estate investment, but lenders increasingly insist on personal guaranties, particularly on loans that already carry meaningful leverage relative to a property's in-place value, as this one apparently did. Once a borrower signs an unlimited guaranty, that insulation disappears: the lender can pursue a deficiency judgment against the individual's other assets even if the foreclosed property, once sold, fails to cover the outstanding balance.

The dispute also underscores an underappreciated risk for owners of buildings leased to government tenants. Public-sector leases are often prized for their perceived stability, but government tenants can and do exercise "termination for cause" provisions tied to a landlord's maintenance obligations more readily than commercial lessees who face higher relocation costs and less institutional appetite for prolonged litigation over code compliance. A pattern of unresolved building-code violations, in other words, is not merely a municipal enforcement headache; it can become the tenant's contractual exit ramp, and from there, the trigger for a lender's declaration of default.

Owners of single-tenant office assets, particularly those leased to public agencies, should treat outstanding code violations as a live threat to the lease itself rather than a routine cost of doing business, since an accumulation of unresolved citations can hand a well-resourced government tenant contractual grounds to walk away entirely. Borrowers negotiating acquisition financing should scrutinize whether a lender is requiring an unlimited personal guaranty, weigh that risk against the leverage the loan provides, and understand that such a guaranty survives even a full loss of the underlying collateral. And lenders financing single-tenant, government-leased office properties should build ongoing property-condition monitoring into loan servicing, since a landlord's deferred maintenance can just as easily cost a lender its income stream as it can cost the borrower the building.
