# Connecticut Investor Says Joint-Venture Partner's Forged Settlement Erased Its Stake in UConn-Area Development

### JZ Investments accuses Chen Ji of misappropriating federal investor-program funds and signing an unauthorized settlement that handed its equity to a Belpointe-affiliated opportunity zone fund

A Connecticut investor's lawsuit over a stalled 262-unit housing development next to the University of Connecticut's main campus illustrates a recurring risk in joint-venture real estate deals funded partly through foreign investor capital: what happens when the managing partner who controls the paperwork turns out, the other side alleges, not to be trustworthy with it.

JZ Investments, Inc., a Connecticut corporation, filed a complaint on April 24, 2026 in Connecticut Superior Court in Stamford against Chen Ji, an individual who managed a real estate joint venture with JZ, and against three affiliated entities of Belpointe PREP, LLC, a publicly reporting qualified opportunity fund (Nasdaq-adjacent OTC ticker: OZ) that develops multifamily and mixed-use projects: Belpointe Prep LLC, Belpointe Prep OC LLC, and BPOZ Storrs Holding, LLC. JZ brought the suit both individually and derivatively on behalf of CMC Storrs SPV, LLC, the Delaware entity that owns roughly 60 acres at 497-501 Middle Turnpike in Mansfield, Connecticut — the site of a planned 262-unit residential project immediately adjacent to UConn's Storrs campus.

According to Belpointe's own SEC disclosures and reporting by the Hartford Business Journal and Law360, JZ Investments says it put approximately $1.6 million into the venture and held a preferred equity interest of nearly 30% in CMC Storrs SPV. JZ's complaint alleges that Chen, over a period of years, misappropriated funds tied to a federal immigrant investor program (commonly known as EB-5 financing) connected to the project, and that in 2023 Chen signed a settlement agreement purporting to resolve disputes over the venture — without JZ's authorization — that had the effect of stripping JZ of its ownership interest and leaving Belpointe as CMC's sole owner. Against Chen individually, the complaint alleges fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, and forgery. Against the Belpointe entities, it alleges negligence, fraudulent transfer, unjust enrichment, and violations of the Connecticut Unfair Trade Practices Act, arguing that Belpointe benefited from a settlement it knew or should have known was unauthorized. JZ is seeking restoration of its equity interest in CMC, declaratory relief, compensatory damages, and attorneys' fees and costs.

Belpointe, for its part, disputes any liability in its own SEC filings, stating that it intends to vigorously defend the litigation, and an attorney representing the Belpointe defendants told the Hartford Business Journal that his clients deny the allegations against them, are not responsible for Chen's conduct, and have separately sued Chen themselves. As of Belpointe's most recent quarterly disclosure, filed November 4, 2026, the parties were still litigating preliminary motions, with Belpointe awaiting a hearing date on its motion to dismiss. The Storrs dispute is not Chen's only entanglement with Belpointe in litigation: the company's filings also disclose a related, earlier-filed suit by a separate creditor, the Galinn Fund LLC, seeking to foreclose on a $3 million mortgage note Chen executed on CMC's behalf, as well as a 2025 derivative suit against Chen that was dismissed in February 2026.

For a development that has already sat stalled for years as these disputes played out, the litigation is a reminder that institutional investors underwriting joint ventures with foreign-capital components need more than a trusted relationship with the managing partner — they need independent visibility into that partner's authority to sign settlements and amendments on the venture's behalf.

## Practical takeaways

- Passive or minority investors in a real estate joint venture should insist on contractual notice-and-consent rights before any settlement, release, or equity restructuring affecting the investment vehicle becomes binding, rather than relying on a managing member's good faith.
- Deals funded in part through EB-5 or other federal immigrant-investor capital carry an additional layer of fiduciary and regulatory exposure, since allegations of fund misappropriation can trigger both civil fraud claims and scrutiny of the underlying visa-linked investment structure.
- A publicly reporting sponsor's SEC filings are often the first place a dispute like this becomes visible to the market; counsel for both sponsors and investors should expect opposing litigation postures (sponsor disclaiming responsibility for a rogue partner, investor alleging the sponsor benefited from the partner's conduct) to play out in parallel with the underlying litigation.
- Multiple, overlapping suits arising from the same underlying joint-venture breakdown — a mortgage foreclosure claim from one creditor, a derivative suit from another investor, and now a direct fraud claim — are a signal of deeper governance problems at the project level that counterparties and lenders should factor into any workout or refinancing discussion.
