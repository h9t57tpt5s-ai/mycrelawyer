# New Jersey Appeals Court Upholds Zoning Approval for Historic Building's Hotel-Restaurant-Spa Conversion

### A neighboring commercial property owner's second attempt to block redevelopment of the Gottlieb Building in Point Pleasant Beach fails on appeal, reinforcing the deference New Jersey courts give to detailed zoning board records

**October 30, 2026** — The Appellate Division of the Superior Court of New Jersey has affirmed a trial court ruling that upheld a municipal zoning board's approval of use variances allowing a 1906 commercial building in downtown Point Pleasant Beach to be converted into a ground-floor restaurant and spa with a seven-room boutique hotel on the second floor. The decision in *637 Arnold, LLC v. Arnold Shores, LLC*, issued October 30, 2026, closes out the second round of litigation a neighboring property owner has brought over redevelopment plans for the same building, and it offers commercial developers a useful data point on how much weight New Jersey courts will give a zoning board's own factual findings when a project survives a contested, multi-session public hearing process.

## What Happened

The dispute centers on the Gottlieb Building, a two-story structure at 641 Arnold Avenue in Point Pleasant Beach's downtown commercial district. In 2020, the building's then-owners obtained a use variance from the Borough's Zoning Board of Adjustment to convert the building's upper floor into a hotel — a use not otherwise permitted in that commercial zone — while retaining retail space below. Plaintiff 637 Arnold, LLC, which owns neighboring property, unsuccessfully challenged that approval in a prior prerogative-writs action. The former owners never built out the approved project and, in 2023, sold the building to Arnold Shores, LLC, an entity managed by Hollston Hills, LLC and affiliated with Hollston Realty Group, LLC (together, the Gottlieb defendants).

The new owners returned to the Board in 2024 with an expanded application: they proposed keeping the previously approved hotel use on the second floor while adding a restaurant (a permitted use) and a spa (not a permitted use) on the ground floor. After three public hearings spanning March through May 2024, during which 637 Arnold appeared as an objector and the Gottlieb defendants presented testimony from their property manager, architect, and a licensed planner, the Board unanimously approved the integrated hotel-restaurant-spa plan. Its resolution found the project "particularly suitable" for the site, concluded it would add "vitality and convenience" to the downtown without a "substantial negative impact" on surrounding properties, and held that the relief sought did not conflict with the Borough's master plan or zoning ordinance.

637 Arnold filed a complaint in lieu of prerogative writs seeking to overturn the approval, arguing that the public notice describing the application was legally deficient, that the Board failed to properly analyze the variance criteria for the newly integrated multi-use project, that the site plan relied on an outdated survey, and that the proceedings were tainted by bias. A Superior Court judge rejected each of these arguments following a March 2025 trial and dismissed the complaint with prejudice, issuing a lengthy written amplification of his reasoning. The Appellate Division — a two-judge panel sitting per curiam — affirmed, holding that it saw no basis to disturb the trial court's findings.

## The Legal Backdrop

New Jersey's Municipal Land Use Law, N.J.S.A. 40:55D-70(d), authorizes "(d) variances" for uses not ordinarily permitted in a given zone, but conditions that relief on a showing that the site is particularly suited to the proposed use and that the variance can be granted without substantial detriment to the public good or impairment of the municipality's master plan and zoning ordinance. Boards of adjustment are given considerable latitude in weighing conflicting expert testimony — here, the Board expressly credited the applicant's planner over any objections raised by 637 Arnold — and reviewing courts generally will not second-guess a board's factual findings where they are supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The case also illustrates a recurring feature of New Jersey land-use litigation: a neighboring owner can object to successive applications for the same property over multiple years, but each new round of review resets the factual record the board (and ultimately the court) will rely on. Here, the Board's 2024 approval incorporated an updated boundary survey reflecting a 2021 court-ordered correction to the property line between the two parcels — a dispute that had itself previously been litigated between the same neighbors — which undercut 637 Arnold's argument that the site plan rested on invalid survey data.

## Practical Takeaways

For commercial property owners, developers, and their counsel navigating contested zoning approvals, several points from this decision are worth keeping in mind:

- **A well-developed hearing record matters more than the number of hearings an objector forces.** The Board's multi-session process, which generated detailed testimony from the applicant's planner, architect, and ownership, gave the reviewing courts a substantial evidentiary basis to defer to — precisely the kind of record that tends to survive prerogative-writs review.
- **Reusing elements of a previously approved plan can streamline, not complicate, a new application.** Because the second-floor hotel use mirrored what an earlier board had already approved for the same building (and that approval had itself survived a court challenge), the new application needed to justify primarily the ground-floor restaurant-and-spa component, narrowing the scope of what objectors could realistically contest.
- **Resolve boundary and title issues before they become zoning-litigation leverage.** The Board's incorporation of a court-ordered boundary correction from earlier litigation between the same parties foreclosed one of the objector's main procedural arguments; developers facing a contentious neighbor should consider whether unresolved property-line or survey disputes could later be weaponized against a pending land-use application.
- **Public notice challenges require more than a generalized claim of deficiency.** The panel's affirmance reflects the trial court's finding that the notice adequately described the nature of the relief sought, underscoring that a notice challenge needs to show the specific ways in which affected parties lacked a meaningful opportunity to participate.
