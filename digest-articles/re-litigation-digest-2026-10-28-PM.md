# No-Show at Trial Costs Eminent Domain Defendant Its Shot at More Compensation

### Arkansas Court of Appeals affirms a directed verdict capping a condemned property owner's recovery at the city's pre-trial deposit, holding the owner waived its objections by raising them only after losing at trial

A commercial property owner that skipped its own eminent domain trial has lost its bid on appeal to recover more than the government's pre-trial deposit for the taking. In *BFT Rental, LLC v. City of Fayetteville*, the Arkansas Court of Appeals on October 28, 2026, affirmed a Washington County Circuit Court order granting the City of Fayetteville a directed verdict after BFT Rental, LLC failed to appear for the jury trial set to determine how much compensation it was owed for a condemned easement. The ruling leaves BFT's recovery capped at the $112,180 the City had already deposited with the court — and serves as a pointed reminder that a condemnee's right to a jury's independent assessment of just compensation can be forfeited by default just as surely as any other civil claim.

### A condemnation case undone by counsel turnover and a missed trial date

The City filed its condemnation action against BFT on November 8, 2023, seeking to acquire an easement across BFT-owned real property in Fayetteville for construction of a water-transmission main. Acting under Arkansas's quick-take statute, Ark. Code Ann. § 18-15-303, the City deposited $112,180 into the court registry and obtained an order of immediate possession on November 16, 2023 — a mechanism that lets a condemning authority take possession and begin public works while the ultimate compensation question is litigated. BFT moved to withdraw the deposited funds, which the court granted, and the case proceeded toward a jury trial on the amount of just compensation.

What followed was a run of attorney transitions that proved consequential. BFT's original counsel withdrew in the spring of 2024; replacement counsel Tona DeMers entered an appearance that July. The circuit court set a one-day jury trial for April 30, 2025. On January 31, 2025 — roughly three months before trial — DeMers moved to withdraw as well. The City objected to any resulting continuance, noting the case had already been pending for well over a year, its witnesses were prepared to proceed, and DeMers was BFT's second attorney to seek withdrawal during the litigation. The circuit court granted the withdrawal on February 27, 2025, but denied the continuance.

BFT did not retain new counsel until after the trial date had passed, and critically, never objected to the February 27 ruling at the time. Instead, BFT simply did not appear for the April 30 jury trial. With no appearance, no witness list, and no exhibit list from the defense, the City moved for a directed verdict, which the circuit court granted, affirming the City's ownership of the easement and fixing just compensation at the $112,180 already on deposit. The court later explained in its written order that, absent any evidence from BFT, "a reasonable jury would not have a sufficient evidentiary basis to award [BFT] just compensation in excess of that previously deposited."

### The appeal: an objection raised too late

On appeal, BFT argued that the circuit court abused its discretion by allowing its second attorney to withdraw three months before trial while denying a continuance, and that this error tainted both the directed verdict and the court's later denial of BFT's motion for a new trial. The Court of Appeals never reached the merits of that argument. Under well-settled Arkansas preservation doctrine — the panel cited *Tate-Smith v. Cupples* and *Nash v. Nash* — an objection raised for the first time in a motion for new trial is not timely and cannot be considered on appeal. The court explained that this rule exists precisely to prevent a litigant from "sandbagging": waiting to see whether a gamble pays off before raising an available objection. Because BFT voiced no complaint about the February 27 ruling until two months later, after it had already lost at trial, the panel held the issue was unpreserved. The court likewise rejected BFT's separate challenge to the denial of its new-trial motion, again for lack of a timely, specific objection below, and noted that BFT's briefing never actually argued why the directed verdict itself was substantively wrong.

### Why this matters for commercial property owners

Eminent domain compensation disputes are almost always about the numbers: appraisals, replacement-cost analyses, business-loss testimony, and the gap between what a government deposits and what the taken property is actually worth. This case is a reminder that all of that evidence is worthless if it never reaches the jury. A condemnee that loses its lawyer close to trial faces a genuine dilemma, but the remedy is an immediate, on-the-record objection and a formal continuance motion before the trial date — not a wait-and-see approach that raises the issue only after an adverse verdict. For commercial landlords, developers, and other property owners facing condemnation actions, the case also underscores that a government's statutory deposit, once undisputed by competent evidence at trial, can become the ceiling on recovery as a matter of law, not just a floor.

**Practical takeaways:**
- When a condemnation case loses counsel close to trial, object to any adverse scheduling ruling immediately and on the record — raising it for the first time in a post-trial motion forfeits appellate review under Arkansas's preservation rules.
- A quick-take deposit under statutes like Ark. Code Ann. § 18-15-303 is not merely an interim payment; if the condemnee fails to put on evidence of greater value at trial, the deposit can become the final, binding measure of just compensation.
- Default at trial in an eminent domain case carries the same consequences as default in any other civil action — courts will not revisit the underlying merits on appeal absent a properly preserved objection.
- Owners facing condemnation should treat trial dates as immovable absent a timely, documented continuance motion, particularly when counsel transitions are already a point of friction with opposing counsel.
