# ZOM Holding Files for Chapter 11 to Fund Appeal of $308.6 Million Arizona Judgment

### A rare non-circumvention verdict against a national multifamily developer is now playing out in bankruptcy court, with implications for how developers document informal joint-venture sourcing arrangements.

ZOM Holding, Inc., the parent of Florida-based multifamily developer ZOM Living, filed for Chapter 11 protection on October 30, 2026, in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Middle District of Florida, together with two affiliates, ZOM Associates, Inc. and ZH Development I, LLC. The filing follows a roughly $308.6 million Arizona state-court judgment entered against ZOM Holding earlier this year in favor of Gray Development Group, a now-defunct Phoenix-area development firm that accused ZOM of freezing it out of a multifamily and commercial pipeline the two companies had planned to build together. For commercial developers who rely on informal information-sharing with prospective joint-venture partners before signing binding deal documents, the case is a pointed reminder that courts will enforce non-circumvention and good-faith obligations even without a fully executed partnership agreement.

The underlying dispute traces back to 2019, when Gray says it shared proprietary market research and site information with ZOM in connection with a proposed joint venture to develop a pipeline of luxury multifamily and commercial projects in the Phoenix area. According to Gray's allegations, ZOM spent roughly the next ten months continuing to solicit additional information and analysis from Gray about the planned projects before quietly sidelining the smaller firm and moving forward with the developments on its own. Gray contended the Phoenix projects were conceived and scoped by Gray before ZOM ever became involved, and that ZOM's subsequent dealings with it were never made in good faith. Gray ceased operations in 2020, not long after the relationship fell apart.

A Maricopa County jury agreed with Gray's version of events. In a verdict reported in mid-to-late May 2026, the jury found ZOM liable for breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing arising out of the parties' dealings over the Phoenix pipeline. The trial court subsequently entered judgment against ZOM Holding for approximately $308.6 million in compensatory damages — a figure Gray's counsel described as closer to $323 million once post-judgment interest is factored in. ZOM has maintained on appeal that it was never the actual purchaser of the parcels at issue and was not part of the purchasing entities' organizational structure, disputing both liability and the scope of the damages award.

Rather than satisfy or bond the judgment while the appeal proceeds, ZOM Holding and its two affiliated debtors turned to Chapter 11. According to the bankruptcy filings, the debtors' stated purpose is to preserve and fund ZOM Holding's pending appeal of the Arizona judgment while the automatic stay halts collection efforts, and to sell substantially all of the debtors' assets — including their claims and causes of action — with a non-debtor affiliate, ZOM Holding, L.P., lined up as a stalking-horse bidder and proposed debtor-in-possession lender. That non-debtor affiliate, which holds ZOM's newer development projects, is itself not part of the bankruptcy filing, a structuring choice that keeps the company's active pipeline insulated from the litigation even as the entities tied to the Gray judgment reorganize around it. Court filings describe ZOM Holding's own balance sheet as badly mismatched to the judgment, with assets in the single-digit millions against liabilities reported in the hundreds of millions, and total unsecured debt across the three debtors cited at roughly $541 million. Before the petition date, Gray had already moved to domesticate and enforce the Arizona judgment by recording it in Seminole County, Florida, though reports indicate it had not yet matured into an enforceable lien there when ZOM filed.

For owners, developers, and lenders who routinely explore multifamily and mixed-use pipelines through informal teaming arrangements before a joint-venture agreement is signed, the ZOM litigation underscores several practical risks. A prospective partner's claim to have originated or substantially developed a project can survive the absence of a final JV agreement if a jury finds the parties' course of dealing created an implied duty of good faith, and damages in such cases can reach far beyond any single deal's economics once a jury values an entire pipeline. The case also illustrates how Chapter 11 is increasingly used not merely to shed debt but as an offensive tool to fund a contested appeal while shielding a going-concern business from immediate collection — a strategy that shifts leverage back toward a judgment debtor even after a jury has ruled against it, and one that creditors and their counsel should anticipate when negotiating development-stage confidentiality and non-circumvention terms.
