# Fifth Circuit: Apartment Owner's Hail-Damage Claim Died the Day the Insurer Closed the File

### A San Antonio multifamily owner waited too long to sue over a denied hailstorm claim, and the Fifth Circuit's October 20 ruling shows exactly which date started the clock

A federal appeals court has handed commercial property insurers a clean win on one of the most common — and most commonly mishandled — defenses in first-party property litigation: the statute of limitations. In an unpublished opinion issued October 20, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit affirmed summary judgment against NFI Apartment Management, L.L.C., holding that its breach-of-contract and Texas Insurance Code claims against Third Coast Insurance Company over hailstorm damage to a San Antonio apartment complex were filed too late. The case, *NFI Apartment Management, L.L.C. v. Third Coast Insurance Company*, No. 25-50953, is a reminder that in first-party property coverage disputes, the clock typically starts running when the insurer denies or closes the claim — not whenever the policyholder eventually decides to sue.

**What happened.** A May 2020 hailstorm struck an apartment complex owned by NFI Apartment Management in San Antonio. NFI submitted a claim to its property insurer, Third Coast Insurance Company. Third Coast investigated and, in a letter dated July 21, 2020, told NFI it had identified roughly $37,058.29 in covered damage — well short of the policy's $359,856.60 deductible. Because the estimated loss fell below the deductible, Third Coast paid nothing on the claim. NFI pushed back, and the parties conducted a joint reinspection of the property. Third Coast's consultant revised the damage estimate upward, to roughly $61,839.72 — still nowhere near the deductible threshold. Third Coast formally closed the claim file on July 28, 2021.

NFI did not file suit until 2024, asserting breach of contract and violations of the Texas Insurance Code and the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act. Third Coast removed the case to the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas and moved for summary judgment on the ground that NFI's claims were barred by both the policy's own contractual limitations provision and the applicable Texas statutory limitations periods. The district court agreed, holding that NFI's claims accrued no later than July 21, 2020 — the date of Third Coast's denial letter — and granted summary judgment for the insurer, mooting NFI's pending discovery motion in the process.

**The appeal.** NFI's central argument on appeal was that its claims were not, in fact, time-barred — essentially re-litigating the accrual-date question the district court had already resolved against it. Reviewing the summary judgment ruling de novo, the Fifth Circuit panel was unpersuaded. The court's unpublished, per curiam opinion affirmed across the board, agreeing that the limitations clock began running when Third Coast denied coverage (or, at the latest, when it closed the file after the joint reinspection), not when NFI finally got around to filing suit roughly three to four years later.

**Why it matters for commercial property owners.** This is not a dispute about whether the hail damage was real or whether Third Coast's damage estimate was fair — those questions never reached the merits, because NFI's claims were extinguished by the calendar before a court ever had to weigh in on the adequacy of the payout. That procedural posture is exactly what makes the case useful as a cautionary tale rather than as a substantive coverage precedent. Multifamily owners, like any other commercial insureds, face contractual limitations periods (commonly two years from the date of loss or denial under many Texas property forms) layered on top of statutory limitations periods under the Texas Insurance Code and DTPA. When an insurer sends a denial or below-deductible determination letter, or formally closes a claim file after a reinspection, that communication is very likely to be the operative trigger for the limitations period — regardless of how much informal back-and-forth, reinspection, or internal deliberation continues afterward.

The case also illustrates a trap specific to below-deductible determinations: because the insurer paid nothing, there was no claim payment, denial letter boilerplate, or formal "claim denied" language to anchor a policyholder's calendar — only a letter stating an estimate below the deductible. Property owners and their counsel should treat that kind of communication with the same urgency as an outright denial, because courts plainly will.

**Practical takeaways.** Commercial and multifamily property owners should calendar limitations deadlines from the date of the insurer's first adverse determination — whether that is an outright denial or, as here, an estimate that falls below the deductible — rather than waiting for a "final" resolution that may never formally arrive. Any subsequent reinspection, revised estimate, or renewed correspondence with the insurer should be assumed not to restart the limitations clock absent clear contractual language or a written tolling agreement, so owners should not treat an insurer's willingness to keep talking as implicit permission to delay filing suit. Finally, because many commercial property policies shorten the limitations period by contract below what statute would otherwise allow, owners facing a denied or below-deductible claim should have counsel confirm the applicable contractual limitations provision — not just the general statutory period — at the first sign of a dispute, well before the file is formally closed.
