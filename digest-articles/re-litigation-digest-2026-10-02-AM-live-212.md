# Federal Court Keeps Real Estate Industry's Preemption Challenge to Denver and Colorado Building-Emissions Mandates Alive

### A ruling out of the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado lets apartment, hotel, and developer trade groups continue to argue that Energize Denver and the state's Regulation 28 are preempted by a 1975 federal appliance-efficiency law -- with the U.S. Department of Justice now weighing in on the plaintiffs' side.

Commercial building owners across Colorado's largest city have spent the better part of two years watching a single piece of federal litigation that could determine whether some of the most aggressive municipal building-decarbonization rules in the country can stand. On the first order of October 2026, U.S. District Judge Regina M. Rodriguez gave them a partial answer: the real estate industry's core preemption theory survives a renewed motion to dismiss and may proceed toward the merits, even as a related claim about consumer appliances was tossed as untimely.

The case, *Colorado Apartment Association v. Ryan*, was filed in April 2024 by four trade associations with members who own or manage large buildings in Colorado -- the Colorado Apartment Association, the Apartment Association of Metro Denver, the Colorado Hotel and Lodging Association, and the NAIOP Colorado Chapter, the commercial real estate development trade group. Their target is a pair of linked regulatory programs: Denver's Energize Denver ordinance and the state's parallel Regulation 28, both of which require owners of large buildings to cut energy use or greenhouse gas emissions by specified amounts on a fixed timetable, with financial penalties for buildings that miss their targets.

The plaintiffs' theory is not that building decarbonization is unlawful in the abstract, but that the specific mechanism Denver and Colorado chose runs headlong into the Energy Policy and Conservation Act of 1975. EPCA sets uniform federal efficiency standards for a defined list of "covered products" -- furnaces, water heaters, central air conditioners, and similar equipment -- and expressly bars states and localities from imposing their own, different efficiency or energy-use standards on those same products. The trade groups argue that because many buildings can only hit their Energize Denver and Regulation 28 targets by replacing covered equipment with units that exceed the federal standard, the local rules function as exactly the kind of product-specific mandate EPCA forecloses, even though the ordinances are framed in building-level, not appliance-specific, terms.

The litigation has already traveled a bruising procedural road. The district court dismissed the plaintiffs' original complaint without prejudice in March 2025, holding that the four associations had not adequately pleaded that their individual members had standing to sue. The plaintiffs regrouped, filed an amended complaint in June 2025, and the state and city defendants -- along with intervenor-defendants defending the programs -- moved to dismiss again. That motion drew an unusual degree of federal attention: on September 15, 2026, the U.S. Department of Justice filed a statement of interest telling Judge Rodriguez that Energize Denver and Regulation 28 "plausibly are preempted" by EPCA, a position that does not make the United States a party but signals how the federal government's own lawyers read the preemption question.

Judge Rodriguez's ruling tracks that view on the central claim while narrowing the case elsewhere. The building-performance preemption theory -- that Energize Denver and Regulation 28 effectively dictate the energy use of covered appliances through building-level targets -- survives dismissal and moves toward discovery and the merits. A separate claim challenging the rules' treatment of consumer appliances more directly was dismissed as untimely, reflecting the limitations problems that have dogged this litigation since its 2024 filing.

For commercial real estate owners, the significance extends well beyond Denver's city limits. Energize Denver and Regulation 28 are among the most closely watched building-performance standards in the country, and a growing number of other jurisdictions -- from New York's Local Law 97 to building electrification ordinances on the West Coast -- rest on similar legal architecture: emissions or energy-use targets set at the building level rather than naming specific equipment. A final ruling that EPCA preempts Colorado's approach would hand owners, developers, and lenders underwriting large-building acquisitions a powerful argument against similar mandates nationally; a loss would remove one of the industry's few remaining federal theories for resisting building-performance compliance costs outright.

The practical reality for Denver and statewide Colorado building owners right now is that compliance obligations under Energize Denver and Regulation 28 remain in force while the litigation continues -- this ruling keeps the lawsuit alive, it does not enjoin the programs. Owners underwriting capital improvements, equipment replacements, or acquisitions of large buildings in Denver should budget for near-term compliance costs under the existing rules rather than assume the litigation will resolve before their own compliance deadlines arrive, while also tracking the case closely given the DOJ's unusual intervention and the stakes for building-performance mandates nationally. Lenders and buyers underwriting large commercial and multifamily assets in jurisdictions with comparable building-performance ordinances should treat this case as the leading indicator of how far EPCA preemption arguments can realistically travel, and should expect compliance-cost disclosures and energy-retrofit capital plans to become a more routine part of diligence regardless of how the preemption question is ultimately resolved.
