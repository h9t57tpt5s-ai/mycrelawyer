# Illinois Center's Twin Towers Head to Sheriff's Sale After $260M CMBS Foreclosure Judgment

### A Cook County judge's September ruling against an AmTrust RE affiliate clears the way for a November 12 sheriff's sale of the Mies van der Rohe-designed Chicago complex, now appraised at a fraction of its 2015 value.

A Cook County judge has cleared the way for a special servicer to take title to Illinois Center, the twin-tower office complex at 111 E. Wacker Drive and 233 N. Michigan Avenue in Chicago's East Loop, after ruling against the AmTrust RE affiliate that has owned the roughly 2.1-million-square-foot property since 2015. The ruling sets up a Cook County sheriff's sale on November 12, 2026, and marks one of the more dramatic value collapses yet documented in Chicago's distressed office market.

Cook County Associate Judge Edward Robles ruled against the AmTrust RE affiliate in September 2026, entering a judgment of foreclosure on the $260 million commercial mortgage-backed securities loan secured by the complex. According to The Real Deal, Bisnow, and other trade-press accounts published October 19 and 20, 2026, AmTrust had stopped actively contesting the case over the summer, declining to oppose the lender's motion for summary judgment after workout talks with special servicer LNR Partners, LLC broke down. With the judgment entered, the Cook County sheriff's office has scheduled a public auction of the property for November 12, and LNR Partners — acting on behalf of the CMBS trust's bondholders — is positioned to submit the winning credit bid and take title.

The loan dates to 2015, when the AmTrust RE affiliate acquired the Illinois Center towers and financed the purchase with the $260 million CMBS loan, then underwritten against a $390 million valuation. The borrower fell behind on payments, and LNR Partners filed a foreclosure suit in the Circuit Court of Cook County's Chancery Division. By the time Judge Robles entered judgment, the numbers behind the loan had deteriorated sharply: Morningstar Credit data cited in the reporting puts the property's most recent appraisal at just $66 million — an 83% drop from the 2015 origination value and a 22% decline from an already-reduced 2024 appraisal. Occupancy at the complex had fallen to roughly 33% as of the end of 2025, according to servicer commentary described in the coverage.

For a property once counted among the signature assets of Chicago's East Loop skyline — part of the Illinois Center development built in the architectural idiom associated with Mies van der Rohe — the scale of the write-down illustrates how far the city's office downturn has progressed for aging, undercapitalized towers built on pre-pandemic rent and occupancy assumptions. The case also follows a now-familiar pattern for CMBS special servicers handling distressed Chicago office loans: rather than pursue an extended workout or loan modification, LNR Partners appears set to take the property into REO (real estate owned) status directly through the sheriff's sale process, giving the servicer and bondholders direct control over any future repositioning, lease-up, or disposition strategy rather than continuing to negotiate with a borrower that has effectively walked away.

For other Chicago-area office owners facing CMBS loans originated in the mid-2010s at valuations that no longer reflect current market realities, the Illinois Center matter is a useful data point on how special servicers are approaching these situations as 2026 progresses. A borrower who stops contesting foreclosure once it becomes clear that remaining equity has been wiped out — as AmTrust appears to have done here — can move a case to judgment and sale relatively quickly once summary judgment is unopposed. Lenders and servicers, in turn, appear increasingly willing to take title outright rather than extend further forbearance on assets whose appraised values have fallen this far below outstanding debt.

**Practical takeaways**

- Borrowers on legacy CMBS loans secured by severely undervalued office assets should evaluate early whether continued litigation makes economic sense once occupancy and appraised value have fallen far enough that no realistic refinancing or sale could cover the debt; an unopposed summary judgment motion, as in this case, can accelerate the path to a sheriff's sale.
- Special servicers and CMBS bondholders should treat a credit bid and direct REO takeover as a live option for deeply underwater assets rather than defaulting to further loan modifications, particularly where occupancy has fallen into the 30% range.
- Owners and lenders alike should watch Cook County sheriff's sale calendars and Morningstar/Trepp servicer commentary for a read on how quickly comparable downtown Chicago office towers are moving from default to disposition, since the timeline here — foreclosure suit in late 2024 to judgment and sale by November 2026 — may be a useful benchmark.
