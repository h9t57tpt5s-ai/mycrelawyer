# D.C. Attorney General Sues Ward 5 Landlord Over "Extreme and Pervasive" Disrepair at Delta Pearl

### A 166-unit affordable housing tower with dead elevators, no air conditioning, and nearly 200 open code violations has drawn a consumer-protection and receivership suit from the District's top law enforcement officer — a warning shot for any owner treating deferred maintenance as a line item rather than a legal exposure.

District of Columbia Attorney General Brian L. Schwalb filed suit on September 1, 2026 against the owner, manager, and developer of Delta Pearl, a 10-story, 166-unit apartment building at 1400 Florida Avenue NE in the District's Ward 5, alleging that months of unrepaired conditions have left tenants living without air conditioning, without functioning elevators, and amid what the complaint describes as trash, urine, and feces accumulating in common stairwells. The suit, filed in the Superior Court of the District of Columbia, names Delta 2 Owner LLC as the record owner, DP Management LLC — doing business as Faria Management — as the property manager, and developer Buwa Binitie individually as defendants.

Delta Pearl was originally constructed in 1979 as affordable senior housing and was acquired by Binitie in 2022, who redeveloped the property; roughly 51 percent of its units remain designated as affordable housing for tenants earning below 80 percent of the area median income. According to the Office of the Attorney General, city inspectors have documented nearly 200 unresolved housing code violations at the building, including rodent and roach infestations, standing water damage, exposed electrical wiring, inoperable fire-safety equipment, and unrepaired structural damage from a fire that struck the building in February 2026. Air conditioning and elevator service have both been out since the beginning of July, according to the complaint, meaning tenants — many of them elderly or mobility-limited residents in a building historically marketed to seniors — have gone through the height of a Washington summer without either.

The lawsuit invokes two separate D.C. statutory frameworks: the District's Consumer Protection Procedures Act, which prohibits unfair and deceptive trade practices in the offering of housing, and its Tenant Receivership Act, which allows a court to strip an owner of control over a building and appoint an independent receiver to manage repairs when conditions pose an ongoing threat to tenant health and safety. The Attorney General's office is seeking an emergency court order compelling immediate repairs to the elevators and cooling systems, a broader preliminary injunction requiring the defendants to bring the building into code compliance, restitution for affected tenants, and civil penalties under the consumer-protection statute.

For owners and managers of affordable and rent-restricted housing, the Delta Pearl case illustrates a pattern regulators are increasingly willing to escalate quickly: rather than relying solely on the slower mechanism of individual housing-code citations working through an administrative process, the District is combining a receivership request — which can functionally remove an owner from operational control of a property — with a consumer-protection claim carrying its own civil penalty exposure. That combination raises the stakes considerably above a conventional code-enforcement action, and it signals that a building's affordable-housing designation does not insulate an owner from aggressive government intervention when conditions deteriorate; if anything, the vulnerability of the tenant population appears to be sharpening the Attorney General's response. The timing is also notable: a receivership motion filed while conditions are actively documented and ongoing — rather than after the fact — gives a court a stronger evidentiary basis to act on an emergency footing, and defendants facing a receivership motion typically have little practical opportunity to cure the underlying violations before a hearing occurs.

The case is at an early stage, with the defendants not yet having filed a public response to the allegations, and no hearing date has been announced as of this writing. But institutional owners, lenders, and asset managers with affordable-housing components in their portfolios should treat the filing as a reminder that deferred maintenance at a property serving a vulnerable population can convert into fast-moving litigation risk well before it becomes a routine code-enforcement matter, that a receivership request can strip day-to-day control of an asset from its owner on relatively short notice once a court finds an ongoing threat to health and safety, that documented violation counts and repair timelines are being treated by regulators as evidence of a pattern rather than isolated maintenance lapses, and that maintaining current, auditable repair logs and vendor payment records for critical building systems — elevators and HVAC chief among them — is now a basic risk-management necessity rather than a paperwork formality, particularly for any property carrying an affordable-housing designation that draws heightened regulatory scrutiny.
