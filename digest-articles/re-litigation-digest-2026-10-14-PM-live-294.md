# Massachusetts Appeals Court: 800,000-Square-Foot Holliston Warehouse Is Not a Permitted-as-of-Right Use

### CRG Acquisition's win in the Land Court unravels on appeal, leaving a logistics developer's special-permit denial as the last word on its project

The Massachusetts Appeals Court on October 14, 2026 reversed a Land Court judgment that had cleared the way for CRG Acquisition, LLC to build an approximately 800,000-square-foot warehouse and distribution facility in Holliston without a special permit. In *CRG Acquisition, LLC v. Planning Board of Holliston*, the panel held that even though the planning board was owed no special deference on the specific legal question presented, the board's underlying position was correct: a facility of this scale does not fall within the zoning bylaw's as-of-right warehouse category, but instead within a separate, size-based general-industrial-use category that requires a special permit. That permit was denied back in 2021, and the reversal leaves the project without a zoning path forward in its current form.

CRG is the buyer under a pending purchase and sale agreement for roughly seventy-two acres of land from New Hopping Brook Realty Trust, sited in Holliston's industrial zoning district. In 2019, CRG applied to the planning board for site plan approval to build a warehouse and distribution facility that would include 423 trailer truck spaces, 170 loading docks and 129 passenger vehicle parking spaces. The planning board approved the site plan in March 2020 with thirty-one conditions, though the town's building inspector flagged a concern even then that the use might ultimately require a special permit for general industrial uses rather than qualifying under the bylaw's warehouse category.

That concern proved prescient. In June 2020, CRG applied for a special permit while maintaining that none was actually required, and separately sought approval of a modified site plan with fewer trailer spaces and loading docks. After eleven public hearings, the planning board denied both the special permit and the modified site plan in July 2021, citing concerns about traffic, noise and the project's impact on the town's historical and agricultural character. CRG responded by suing in the Land Court on two tracks: a direct appeal of the board's denials under G. L. c. 40A, Section 17, and a separate declaratory judgment claim under G. L. c. 240, Section 14A, arguing that its warehouse was a use permitted by right under the bylaw's Section III.G.1, covering "wholesale office or showroom, including warehouse facilities," so that no special permit was needed in the first place.

A Land Court judge agreed with CRG on the declaratory judgment count, reasoning that the bylaw's specific use of the word "warehouse" in Section III.G.1, and nowhere else in its table of uses, meant the principal use of a property as a warehouse was allowed as of right in the industrial district. A judgment entered declaring that CRG needed no special permit and remanding the separate site-plan dispute to the planning board for further review. The town and planning board appealed, and the Appeals Court reversed. The panel agreed with CRG that no deference was owed to the board's interpretation of the use tables for purposes of the declaratory judgment claim, since that statute does not involve review of an existing board decision in the way a direct zoning appeal does. But reviewing the bylaw independently, the panel sided with the town on the merits: given the scale of CRG's proposed facility, the project falls under the bylaw's separate category for general industrial uses exceeding 15,000 square feet of floor area, not the as-of-right warehouse category CRG had invoked, so a special permit is required after all.

The practical upshot for logistics and warehouse developers is that a zoning bylaw's use table cannot be read by matching a keyword like "warehouse" to a permitted-as-of-right category without accounting for separate, size-based use categories the same bylaw may impose on larger facilities. A roughly 800,000-square-foot distribution center with hundreds of trailer spaces and loading docks can fall outside a town's ordinary warehouse use classification even when that classification exists elsewhere in the same bylaw, pushing the project into a discretionary special-permit process that neighbors and local officials can use to raise traffic, noise and character objections. Developers assembling large industrial sites should confirm, before signing a purchase agreement, whether local bylaws tie use classification to project size or intensity, and should not assume that a favorable declaratory judgment on that question at the trial level will survive an appeal once the underlying discretionary permit denial is already final.

Practical takeaways: (1) a town's generic "warehouse" use listing does not guarantee as-of-right status for a large-scale distribution facility if the same bylaw has a separate, size-triggered general-industrial category; (2) courts reviewing a declaratory judgment claim about a bylaw's meaning may owe the local board no deference at all, yet still reach the same result the board did on independent review; (3) winning a declaratory judgment at the trial level does not insulate a project from reversal on appeal, particularly where a discretionary special-permit denial already stands on its own, independent grounds; and (4) developers should map a bylaw's full use-table structure, including any size or intensity thresholds, against a proposed project's actual scale before relying on a single use-table entry during due diligence.
