# New York Appellate Court Clarifies That "Good Guy" Guaranty Liability Ends When a Tenant Physically Vacates, Even Amid Conflicting Lease Language

### The Appellate Division, First Department, applied the Court of Appeals' recent decision in 1995 CAM LLC v. West Side Advisors to release individual guarantors from more than $300,000 in claimed rent and damages, resolving a drafting conflict between a lease's "vacate" release clause and its "Surrender Date" provision in the tenant's favor.

New York's Appellate Division, First Department, has affirmed the dismissal of a SoHo landlord's claims against the individual guarantors of a retail lease, holding that the guarantors' liability ended when the tenant physically vacated the premises -- regardless of a separate lease provision purporting to extend liability until a later, formally defined "Surrender Date." The October 1, 2026 decision in 62 Wooster, LLC v. TML N.Y. Inc. is the First Department's first significant application of the Court of Appeals' 2025 ruling in 1995 CAM LLC v. West Side Advisors, and it gives commercial landlords and their counsel a clearer, if sobering, picture of how "good guy" guaranty release language will be construed when a lease's own provisions conflict.

### What Happened

The case concerns a retail lease at 476 Broome Street in Manhattan, owned by the plaintiff landlord. Two individuals each executed substantively identical personal "good guy" guaranties in connection with the tenant's lease of the space -- a standard risk-mitigation device in New York City commercial leasing that typically limits a principal's personal exposure to the period before the tenant vacates, in exchange for the tenant's promise to leave the space broom clean and give timely notice.

The guaranty documents here, however, contained two provisions in tension with one another. A surrender clause ended the guarantors' liability for rent accruing after the tenant "surrendered legal possession" of the premises, but the guaranties never defined that phrase. A separate "good guy" provision purported to keep the guarantors on the hook through a defined "Surrender Date" -- a formal concept tied to the broader lease documents rather than the simple fact of vacatur. According to the record, the tenant vacated the premises by tendering keys to the landlord's agent and delivering the space broom clean in the fall of 2020. The landlord nonetheless sued the individual guarantors, seeking roughly $307,577.08 in unpaid rent for July through October 2021, along with liquidated damages measured by the shortfall between the lease rent and what the landlord collected upon reletting through the original lease's expiration date, plus its costs of enforcing the guaranty.

Supreme Court, New York County (Alexander M. Tisch, J.), granted the guarantors' motion to dismiss the landlord's claims for unpaid rent, liquidated damages, and enforcement costs against them individually. The landlord appealed.

### The Court's Reasoning

The First Department affirmed, without costs, relying on the Court of Appeals' intervening decision in 1995 CAM LLC v. West Side Advisors, which construed the term "surrender" in a commercial guaranty to mean the tenant's physical relinquishment of possession and control over the premises -- not a landlord's subsequent acceptance of that surrender, and not some later-defined contractual milestone layered on top of the physical fact of vacatur. Applying that rule, the panel held that the guarantors' liability ended when the tenant relinquished physical possession in 2020, notwithstanding the lease documents' separate "Surrender Date" language that the landlord argued had never been triggered. In effect, where a guaranty's plain surrender-based release clause conflicts with a more technical, landlord-favorable trigger drawn from the underlying lease, the tenant-protective, vacatur-based reading controls.

The practical consequence for the landlord was substantial: the claims for unpaid rent covering mid-2021, the liquidated-damages measure tied to the full remaining lease term, and the associated enforcement costs were all foreclosed as against the individual guarantors, even though the landlord's theory -- that a formally defined "Surrender Date" had not yet occurred under the lease's own terms -- found some textual support in the guaranty documents themselves.

### Why It Matters

Good guy guaranties are ubiquitous in New York City retail and commercial leasing precisely because they are supposed to offer predictability: a tenant principal's personal exposure ends at a clear, ascertainable point tied to vacating the space, in exchange for cooperation on move-out condition and notice. This decision, read together with 1995 CAM LLC, signals that courts will resolve ambiguity or internal conflict in guaranty release language in favor of that simple, vacatur-based understanding, even where a landlord's drafting attempted to preserve liability through a more technical defined term. Landlords relying on multi-layered defined terms to extend guarantor liability beyond the date of actual vacatur should not assume those provisions will be enforced as written if they conflict, even implicitly, with a more straightforward surrender-based release elsewhere in the same guaranty.

### Practical Takeaways

Landlords drafting good guy guaranties should ensure that any provision intended to extend guarantor liability beyond the date of physical vacatur is reconciled explicitly with the guaranty's surrender or release clause, rather than left as a separate, potentially conflicting defined term. Tenants and guarantors should document the date and manner of vacatur carefully -- delivery of keys, broom-clean condition, and written notice to the landlord's agent -- since that evidentiary record is now squarely what determines when guarantor liability ends under this line of cases. Landlords seeking to recover post-vacatur rent, reletting shortfalls, or enforcement costs from a guarantor should expect courts to scrutinize whether the guaranty's own terms unambiguously extend liability past the tenant's physical surrender, and should consider renegotiating guaranty language going forward to close that gap before it becomes litigation.
