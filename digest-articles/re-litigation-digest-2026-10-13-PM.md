# Industrial Landlord Sues AI Robotics Tenant Over Holdover as Anthropic Waits in the Wings

### A San Francisco warehouse lease fight shows how fast the city's AI-driven industrial squeeze is turning into litigation

The owners of a 98,000-square-foot industrial building in San Francisco's Mission District sued their tenant, robotics startup Physical Intelligence, in San Francisco Superior Court last week, alleging the company has refused to vacate the space since its lease expired and is now accruing holdover damages of more than $13,000 a day. The timing is pointed: according to the complaint, a new tenant is ready to move in as soon as the space is cleared, and sources have identified that incoming tenant as Anthropic, which is reportedly eyeing the building for a research-and-development lab.

The property at 2000 Folsom Street had sat largely vacant as a former food-manufacturing site before the recent wave of AI and robotics companies began competing for San Francisco industrial space. Physical Intelligence signed a lease in January covering roughly 29,000 square feet on the ground floor, at a reported rate of $60,000 a month. According to the lease terms attached to the complaint, the term ran from February through May, with an option for the tenant to continue on a month-to-month basis after expiration. The landlords allege that Physical Intelligence exercised that rollover but never properly terminated it, and that the company compounded the problem by expanding into additional, unleased areas of the building and making improvements there without the owners' consent.

Physical Intelligence had not filed a response to the complaint as of early October, and neither the startup nor Anthropic responded to press inquiries about the dispute or the reported handoff of the space. The landlords' own attorney likewise declined to comment.

## The legal mechanics of a holdover

Commercial holdover disputes like this one turn on contract language that is easy to overlook until a landlord has a replacement tenant lined up. A month-to-month rollover clause lets a tenant stay past a fixed lease term without immediately becoming a trespasser, but it does not give the tenant an indefinite option to remain: California law and most commercial leases allow the landlord to terminate a month-to-month tenancy on notice, after which continued occupancy becomes an unlawful holdover subject to damages, often calculated as a multiple of the prior rent or, as alleged here, a stated daily rate far exceeding the original rent.

The complaint's allegation that Physical Intelligence also occupied unleased portions of the building without consent adds a second, independent theory of liability. Expanding a tenant's footprint beyond the leased premises, even informally, can expose a tenant to damages for the reasonable value of that additional space and can undercut any argument that the landlord tacitly consented to an extended or expanded tenancy. Courts examining these disputes typically look at concrete facts, continued payment or non-payment of rent, whether the landlord accepted rent for the expanded space, and whether either side gave notice, rather than at the parties' informal understanding of the arrangement.

For a landlord with a signed or near-signed deal with a new tenant waiting on delivery of the space, the daily holdover-damages clock is also a practical lever beyond just making the holdover tenant whole: it is designed to make delay expensive enough that the existing tenant has a strong incentive to vacate quickly rather than litigate the underlying lease terms. That incentive structure explains why these suits are often filed, and frequently resolve, within a matter of weeks.

## Why this matters beyond one Mission District warehouse

San Francisco's industrial and flex-space market has tightened considerably as AI labs and robotics companies compete for ground-floor space suited to hardware testing, fabrication and lab work, inventory that the city's office-heavy stock does not supply in quantity. That scarcity is precisely what turns an ordinary end-of-lease holdover into a contested, fast-moving lawsuit: with a specific, well-capitalized replacement tenant waiting on a specific building, the cost of delay to the landlord, and the leverage available to the outgoing tenant, both rise sharply compared to a holdover in a soft market with no backup deal in hand.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial tenants negotiating month-to-month rollover rights should treat the termination mechanics, not just the right to stay, as the operative language, since a landlord with a lined-up replacement tenant has every incentive to terminate on the shortest notice the lease allows. Landlords sitting on a signed deal contingent on vacant possession should document the termination notice and any unauthorized expansion into adjacent space contemporaneously, since both are what converts a slow-moving holdover into a quickly enforceable damages claim. And in tight submarkets where a handful of tenants are chasing the same inventory, both sides should expect that a holdover dispute will move at the pace of the waiting deal, not the pace of ordinary litigation.
