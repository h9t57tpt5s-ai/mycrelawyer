# NY Appellate Court Revives $1M Sublease Fight, Finds Landlord's Own Utility Lapse Created a Fact Question

### A sublessor's summary-judgment win on unpaid rent was wiped out because its own tenant swore the sublessor never paid its share of the building's utilities

A New York appellate panel on October 21, 2026 reversed a $1,004,426.20 judgment that a Brooklyn commercial sublessor had won against its sublessee for unpaid rent, holding that the sublessee raised a triable issue of fact by alleging the sublessor itself breached the sublease — by failing to pay its share of utilities and by having the gas service to the premises disconnected. The decision, *VK Dental Group, Inc. v. Tender Touch Physical Therapy, PLLC*, from the Appellate Division, Second Department, is a reminder that a commercial landlord or sublessor cannot assume summary judgment on a rent-nonpayment claim is automatic just because the tenant's failure to pay is undisputed.

### What happened

The dispute traces back to a chain of leasing transactions involving commercial space, apparently medical and professional office space, in Brooklyn. In January 2017, Metrotech Smile Dental, P.C. leased the property from nonparty landlord 350 Fulton Realty, LLC. Two months later, Metrotech subleased the premises to Tender Touch Physical Therapy, PLLC and other sublessees. In April 2018, Metrotech assigned its prime lease to VK Dental Group, Inc., which stepped into Metrotech's shoes as sublessor to the Tender Touch defendants.

In May 2022, VK Dental sued the sublessees for breach of the sublease agreements, alleging they had stopped paying rent and that the nonpayment ultimately forced VK Dental to surrender possession of the leased premises back to the prime landlord. VK Dental moved for summary judgment on its breach-of-contract claim. The Supreme Court, Kings County (Robin K. Sheares, J.), held a hearing — at which it heard live testimony from a witness for VK Dental — and in an order dated October 21, 2024, granted summary judgment in VK Dental's favor. A judgment followed on November 21, 2024, awarding VK Dental $1,004,426.20 against the defendants. The sublessees appealed.

### The appellate ruling

The Second Department first dismissed the direct appeal from the underlying order, since the right to appeal it had merged into the final judgment, but it reviewed the order's substance as part of the appeal from the judgment itself. On the merits, the panel — Justices Mark C. Dillon, Paul Wooten, Laurence L. Love and Elena Goldberg Velazquez — applied New York's familiar four-part test for a breach-of-contract claim: the existence of a contract, the plaintiff's own performance, the defendant's breach, and resulting damages.

The court agreed that VK Dental had met its initial, prima facie burden on summary judgment simply by showing the sublessees had not paid rent for certain months — regardless of whether the trial court should have held a hearing with live testimony to reach that conclusion. But the panel held that the sublessees defeated summary judgment in opposition by submitting the affidavit of their principal, Svetlana Kibrik, who testified that VK Dental itself had failed to comply with its own obligations under the sublease: specifically, to pay 50% of the premises' utility costs, and that VK Dental had the gas service to the subleased space disconnected. Because a plaintiff suing for breach of contract must show its own performance under the agreement, that evidence was enough to raise a genuine, triable issue of fact as to whether VK Dental had itself breached the sublease — a question a jury, not a judge on a paper record, must resolve. The Second Department therefore held that the trial court should have denied summary judgment outright, reversed the $1 million-plus judgment, and awarded costs to the sublessees.

### Why it matters for commercial landlords and sublessors

The ruling is a useful corrective for commercial property owners and sublessors who treat an unpaid-rent case as a straightforward, nearly automatic path to summary judgment. New York courts have long required a breach-of-contract plaintiff to establish its own performance as part of its prima facie case, and this decision shows that requirement has real teeth even in a garden-variety rent-collection action: a tenant's sworn claim that the landlord failed on a comparatively modest ancillary obligation — splitting a utility bill — was enough to defeat summary judgment on a six-figure rent claim, without the tenant needing to prove its allegation at the summary-judgment stage, merely raise it credibly.

For sublessors and master tenants managing multi-tenant commercial buildings, the case underscores the importance of maintaining clean records of utility payments, service continuity, and any other ancillary obligations a sublease imposes on the landlord side of the relationship — not just rent collection on the tenant side. A disconnected gas line or an unpaid utility invoice can become the centerpiece of a tenant's defense years later, regardless of how clear-cut the nonpayment of rent itself may appear.

### Practical takeaways

- A tenant's admitted nonpayment of rent does not guarantee a landlord or sublessor summary judgment; courts will scrutinize whether the landlord met its own obligations under the lease, including ancillary duties like utility cost-sharing.
- An affidavit from a tenant's principal alleging the landlord's own breach — even without documentary proof at the summary-judgment stage — can be enough to create a triable issue of fact and send a rent case to trial.
- Commercial landlords and sublessors should document compliance with every lease obligation they owe, not just rent collection, including utility payments and continuity of building services, before moving for summary judgment on a tenant's nonpayment.
- A procedural win at the trial level, including a hearing with live testimony, offers no insulation on appeal if the court improperly resolved a genuine factual dispute rather than sending it to a jury.
