# Minority Owners Take a Below-Market Land Sale to Trial — and the Fight Could Reshape How Commercial Development Partners Police Self-Dealing

### A bench trial over a $130 million tract on the edge of West Des Moines tests how far a managing member can go when selling jointly owned land to his own affiliate

A closely watched Iowa bench trial over the sale of roughly 150 acres of prime West Des Moines-area development land wrapped up its evidentiary phase in early September 2026, with post-trial briefs due October 9 in *Eagle Nest Investments, LLC, et al. v. Spencer, et al.*, pending in the Iowa District Court for Polk County. The case, brought by minority owners of Pavilion Park LC against the entity's own manager and his affiliated companies, centers on a pattern increasingly familiar to commercial real estate lawyers nationally: a controlling member selling jointly held land to an entity he also controls, at what the minority owners allege was a steep discount to fair market value.

Pavilion Park LC holds a large, fast-appreciating tract in the western Des Moines suburbs, straddling land suited for both residential and commercial development. William Spencer manages Pavilion Park and also controls TJK Investments LC, a majority owner of the entity, as well as Landmark Brokerage Inc., a real estate brokerage. The plaintiffs — Eagle Nest Investments LLC, Gratias Properties LLC, and Thomas J. Gratias, all minority owners in Pavilion Park — allege that Spencer and TJK arranged for Pavilion Park to sell two parcels to another Spencer-controlled entity, Wendover Reserve LLC, over the minority owners' express objection and at prices well below what the plaintiffs say independent appraisals supported.

According to the complaint, a 25.52-acre parcel slated for residential development sold to Wendover for $1.6 million against an alleged fair market value of roughly $1.99 million, while a much larger, 126.52-acre parcel intended for commercial development sold to Wendover for $11.4 million against an alleged fair market value of at least $18.34 million — a claimed shortfall of nearly $8.7 million between the two transactions combined. The plaintiffs also allege that TJK and Spencer separately arranged for Pavilion Park to pay Landmark Brokerage an unauthorized and excessive commission on the sales, compounding the harm to the minority interest. The suit frames the entire sequence as a coordinated scheme in which Wendover Reserve, TJK, and Spencer worked together with the object of benefiting Wendover at Pavilion Park's expense, breaching the duties of loyalty and care Spencer owed the entity and its minority owners as manager.

The case proceeded to a bench trial rather than a jury trial, meaning the presiding judge — not a jury — will weigh the competing valuation evidence and issue findings on whether the sale prices reflected genuine fair market value or an insider-favorable discount, and on whether the commission paid to Landmark was authorized under Pavilion Park's own governing documents. With the evidentiary record now closed and post-trial briefing due October 9, a ruling could follow in the weeks after.

For commercial real estate practitioners, the dispute is a clean illustration of the litigation risk inherent in any LLC or joint venture structure where a managing member also controls the buyer on the other side of a transaction. Iowa law, like that of most states, imposes fiduciary duties of loyalty and care on an LLC manager, and self-interested transactions — even ones nominally approved through the entity's own governance procedures — remain vulnerable to challenge when the manager sits on both sides of the deal and the minority ownership did not consent. The gap the plaintiffs allege here, nearly 30% below claimed fair market value on the commercial parcel alone, is large enough that a factfinder could plausibly view it as evidence of self-dealing rather than an ordinary difference of appraisal opinion, though the ultimate outcome will turn on how the court weighs dueling valuation testimony neither side has yet had adjudicated.

The case is also a reminder that appraisal methodology and process matter as much as the topline number in these disputes: how a valuation was commissioned, whether it reflected the property's highest-and-best development use, and whether minority owners had a genuine opportunity to object before the transaction closed are all likely to factor into the court's eventual ruling on both the fiduciary-duty and civil-conspiracy theories.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Any LLC or joint-venture manager contemplating a sale to an affiliated entity should obtain a contemporaneous, independent appraisal and document that minority owners had a real opportunity to object before the transaction closes, not after.
- Minority owners in closely held real estate entities should treat objection letters and internal correspondence as important evidence, since a documented objection strengthens a later fiduciary-duty claim far more than an after-the-fact challenge.
- Brokerage commissions paid to an affiliate of the managing member deserve the same scrutiny as the underlying sale price, since an excessive or unauthorized commission can independently support a breach-of-duty claim even where the sale price itself is defensible.
- Structuring around related-party transactions — through an independent committee, a third-party fairness opinion, or unanimous-consent provisions in the operating agreement — remains the most reliable way to avoid this exact category of dispute.
