# Tenant Forecloses on Landlord: Soros Fund Management Takes Title to Billionaires Row Office Building

### A $158.7 million foreclosure judgment culminates in a rare role reversal, with the building's own anchor tenant ending up as its owner

George Soros's family office has completed one of the more unusual foreclosure stories in recent Manhattan commercial real estate history: after suing its own landlord for defaulting on a $145 million mortgage, Soros Fund Management took title to 224 West 57th Street, the ten-story Midtown office building that houses Soros's philanthropic arm, Open Society Foundations, at a September 1, 2026 foreclosure auction. The result leaves the tenant effectively owning the building it occupies, a scenario that underscores both the opportunistic value of distressed-debt acquisitions and the risks facing over-leveraged office owners as legacy loans come due in a still-difficult refinancing market.

The dispute traces back to 2018, when Eretz Group, the New York-based developer that has owned 224 West 57th Street since 2015, borrowed $145 million from lender Aareal Capital Corp. to refinance the roughly 107,000-square-foot building, a former auto showroom on the block widely known as Billionaires Row. The loan carried a maturity date of June 2023, and Eretz obtained two subsequent extensions before ultimately failing to repay the principal. Rather than continue carrying the increasingly troubled loan itself, Aareal sold the debt to Soros Fund Management, which stepped into the lender's shoes and, in April 2024, filed a foreclosure action against Eretz in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, New York County.

What makes the case distinctive is the identity of the plaintiff-lender: Open Society Foundations, the philanthropic organization founded and funded by George Soros, occupies the majority of the office space at 224 West 57th Street as Eretz's tenant. When Soros Fund Management purchased the distressed mortgage, it effectively positioned an entity tied to the building's own occupant to potentially become its landlord, a dynamic that played out in full at the September 1 auction. As courts, litigants, and now the commercial marketplace have observed with growing frequency, the acquisition of distressed CRE debt at a discount has become one of the more reliable strategies for well-capitalized investors to gain control of trophy or high-visibility assets without competing in a conventional sale process.

The foreclosure judgment ultimately entered against Eretz totaled $158.7 million, reflecting the original $145 million principal plus accrued interest, fees, and other carrying costs that piled up over the more than two years the case wound through litigation. At the September 1 auction, held in Lower Manhattan, the building returned to the lender's side of the ledger rather than selling to a third party, consistent with what typically happens when a credit bid from the foreclosing lender exceeds what the market is willing to pay in a challenging office-financing environment. The Soros-linked auction was reported alongside another Midtown foreclosure sale the same day, involving the former headquarters of the Chetrit Group, reflecting a broader wave of legacy office loans reaching their breaking point across the borough this cycle.

For owners and lenders navigating New York's still-recovering office market, the Eretz foreclosure illustrates several practical dynamics at once. First, maturity defaults on legacy loans originated in more favorable rate environments continue to surface even years after the initial stress began, particularly where extensions merely delayed rather than resolved an underlying valuation gap. Second, the sale of distressed debt by an original lender to a new, better-capitalized noteholder is increasingly common, and borrowers should not assume a change in lender signals flexibility; in many cases, as here, it signals the opposite. Third, tenants with the balance sheet to do so are increasingly willing to acquire the debt behind their own leased space, either defensively, to secure occupancy certainty, or opportunistically, to capture the building's underlying value once a foreclosure becomes likely.

Practical takeaways: Commercial property owners carrying loans originated in the pre-2022 rate environment should proactively assess whether extensions have addressed the underlying valuation and cash-flow gap or merely deferred it, since repeated maturity extensions without a structural fix frequently end in foreclosure rather than resolution. Landlords should also be aware that a loan sale to a new noteholder, especially one affiliated with an existing tenant or other stakeholder in the property, can change the practical calculus of a workout far more than a sale to an unrelated distressed-debt fund. Finally, tenants and other market participants with knowledge of a building's financial distress should recognize that purchasing the underlying debt, rather than waiting to negotiate a new lease after a change in ownership, can be a more direct and cost-effective path to securing long-term occupancy or control of a strategically important asset.
