# Equity Residential's $56M RealPage Settlement Pushes Nationwide Rent-Fixing Accord Past $359M

### The Chicago-based multifamily REIT joins Camden Property Trust and Mid-America Apartment Communities in resolving claims that landlords used RealPage's revenue-management software to coordinate rents, as the sprawling MDL heads toward an October fairness hearing.

Equity Residential, one of the nation's largest publicly traded multifamily REITs, has agreed to pay $56 million to resolve its portion of the multidistrict antitrust litigation accusing large apartment owners of using RealPage, Inc.'s revenue-management software to coordinate and inflate rents across the country. The settlement, disclosed in an April 13, 2026 SEC filing, is one of 37 individual settlements reached to date in In re: RealPage, Inc., Rental Software Antitrust Litigation (No. II), MDL No. 3071, pending before U.S. District Judge Waverly D. Crenshaw Jr. in the Middle District of Tennessee — pushing the combined value of settlements in the case past $359.9 million, with a final fairness hearing now scheduled for October 15, 2026.

The underlying claims, first filed in late 2022 and 2023 and later consolidated into the Nashville MDL, allege that Equity Residential and dozens of other multifamily owners and operators violated Section 1 of the Sherman Act by delegating pricing decisions to RealPage's algorithmic revenue-management software, which the complaints say pooled competitors' non-public rent, occupancy, and lease-renewal data to recommend coordinated pricing across horizontally competing properties — the same "hub-and-spoke" theory of liability that has since spread into DOJ enforcement action and a wave of parallel suits against individual landlords in cities with their own algorithmic-pricing bans. Equity Residential's settlement follows a similar pattern to those reached by fellow REITs Camden Property Trust and Mid-America Apartment Communities, which each agreed to pay approximately $53 million, suggesting settlement values across the largest publicly traded multifamily defendants are converging in the same general range even as individual portfolio sizes and market footprints vary considerably.

In its SEC disclosure, Equity Residential was careful to frame the settlement as a business decision rather than an admission of liability, stating that it continues to believe it has "numerous defenses, both factual and legal" to the underlying allegations but concluded that resolving the case now avoids the cost, distraction, and legal uncertainty of continuing to litigate a complex antitrust matter through trial. That framing is now standard across the RealPage MDL's growing settlement roster: defendants are choosing to pay eight- or nine-figure sums to exit the litigation while explicitly preserving their position that the underlying algorithmic-pricing conduct did not, in fact, violate the antitrust laws — a posture that leaves the core legal question of whether software-mediated pricing coordination constitutes an unlawful horizontal conspiracy formally undecided for any defendant that settles rather than litigates to judgment.

The settlement's mechanics track the structure the court has now approved twice, first for an initial batch of settlements in November 2025 and again for eleven additional agreements, including Equity Residential's, presented for preliminary approval in May 2026. Under that structure, class members — renters at properties owned by settling defendants during the relevant class period — will be eligible for cash payments scaled to how much rent they paid, funded out of the aggregate settlement pool once the court grants final approval. That approval process reaches its critical juncture on October 15, 2026, when Judge Crenshaw will hold a fairness hearing in Nashville to determine whether to grant final approval to the settlements reached to date; distribution to class members will not begin until after final approval is granted and any appeals are resolved, meaning the practical payout to renters remains months away even as the legal exposure for settling landlords is now largely fixed.

For REITs and other institutional multifamily owners that have not yet resolved their own RealPage-related exposure, the growing settlement roster offers a rough valuation benchmark — but only a rough one, since settlement amounts appear to track each defendant's portfolio size and market concentration in cities where algorithmic pricing has drawn the most regulatory and litigation attention, rather than a uniform per-unit or percentage-of-revenue formula. Owners still facing individual or MDL exposure should also bear in mind that a national settlement of the antitrust theory does nothing to resolve the separate, and in some ways more durable, exposure created by city- and state-level algorithmic-pricing bans now in effect in jurisdictions including Philadelphia, San Diego, and New York, which impose standalone statutory liability regardless of any proof of a multi-landlord conspiracy.

**Practical takeaways:** Multifamily owners and REITs still facing RealPage-related antitrust exposure should treat the growing settlement roster — now including Equity Residential, Camden, and Mid-America — as an emerging (if imprecise) valuation benchmark tied to portfolio size and market concentration, while recognizing that no settling defendant has conceded liability on the underlying hub-and-spoke theory. Asset managers should confirm whether their properties fall within the MDL's settlement class and calendar the October 15, 2026 fairness hearing, since final approval there determines when class-member payouts actually begin. And because a national antitrust settlement resolves conspiracy-based claims only, not the separate statutory liability created by municipal and state algorithmic-pricing bans, owners operating in jurisdictions with such bans should continue treating vendor-contract and data-sharing practices as a live compliance issue even after resolving MDL exposure.
