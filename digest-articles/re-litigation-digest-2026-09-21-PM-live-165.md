# Overhue v. Benton County: A Second Missouri Data Center Developer Fights a County Moratorium in Court

**A packed Benton County, Missouri courtroom heard arguments this month over whether a six-month moratorium on data center development can be enforced against a developer who had committed to buy land for a project — but had not yet closed on it — when the moratorium took effect.**

Developer Trent Overhue and his company NSI6 LLC asked a Missouri judge to issue a temporary restraining order against Benton County after county commissioners adopted a six-month moratorium on new data center development effective August 10, 2026. The request, argued in early September before a courtroom that filled with roughly 100 spectators, is the second of at least three distinct data-center-related suits now moving through Missouri courts, underscoring how quickly a local government's attempt to pause a fast-growing industry can turn into contested litigation once real projects and real money are on the line.

The timeline is central to the dispute. According to reporting on the courtroom arguments, Overhue committed on July 23, 2026 to purchase property in Benton County for a proposed data center development, with a closing scheduled for September 9, 2026 — roughly a month after the county's moratorium took effect. Because Overhue had not yet closed on the land and construction had not started, counsel for Benton County argued the moratorium validly applies to his project, noting that the ordinance was drafted to allow projects already under construction to proceed while pausing new ones. Overhue's side pressed the court for a restraining order that would preserve his ability to move forward with the purchase and eventual development while the underlying legality of the moratorium is litigated.

The Benton County fight is emerging alongside a parallel dispute the same developer has brought against the City of Springfield, roughly ninety miles away, where NSI, A.1 LLC sued on September 17, 2026 to block a separate city-level data center moratorium from applying to a project the city had previously confirmed was permitted in writing. A third, unrelated Missouri matter — a citizen advocacy group's challenge to a Montgomery County data center incentive package — recently cleared a motion to dismiss in Cole County Circuit Court. None of the three cases are the same underlying dispute; each involves a different local government, a different moratorium ordinance, and in the Montgomery County matter, an entirely different plaintiff. But together they reflect a single, fast-developing trend: Missouri counties and cities reaching for temporary moratoria as data center proposals multiply, and developers with real capital already committed pushing back in court almost as quickly.

For commercial real estate investors and developers watching the data center sector nationally, the Benton County dispute illustrates a recurring vulnerability in project timing. A developer who has only a purchase commitment, rather than closed title, and who has not yet broken ground, typically has a much weaker footing to argue that a moratorium cannot be applied to a specific project, since most moratorium ordinances are deliberately drafted to grandfather in-progress construction while sweeping in everything still on the drawing board. That timing gap — a signed purchase agreement but an unclosed deal — is precisely where Overhue's Benton County project sits, in contrast to the stronger factual position he has in the Springfield case, where the city had issued written zoning verification specific to his project well before adopting its own moratorium.

The broader lesson for developers pursuing data center or other capital-intensive projects in jurisdictions that have not yet adopted formal siting rules is to treat the period between signing a purchase contract and closing as a period of real regulatory exposure, not a formality. A moratorium adopted during that window, even one enacted in apparent good faith to allow the jurisdiction time to study infrastructure impacts, can lawfully reach a project that has not yet closed or broken ground, and a court asked to block it on an emergency basis will weigh both the developer's reliance interest and the county's police-power justification for pausing new development. Developers negotiating land contracts for projects likely to draw local scrutiny — data centers chief among them at the moment — should build moratorium risk into their diligence and closing timelines, and local governments considering a pause should expect that any moratorium adopted after a developer has already committed capital, even without closing, will be litigated by a developer with genuine sunk costs and little to lose from testing the ordinance in court.
