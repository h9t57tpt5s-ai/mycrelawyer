# New Securities Suit Accuses Arbor Realty Trust of Hiding Scope of Troubled Loan Book

### A shareholder complaint filed in federal court in Brooklyn says the mortgage REIT's reassurances about resolving problem assets masked a pile of bad multifamily bridge loans that kept growing through 2026

Arbor Realty Trust, Inc. (NYSE: ABR), one of the nation's largest bridge and agency lenders to multifamily owners, is facing a fresh securities fraud class action, filed November 9, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York. The complaint accuses the mortgage REIT and two of its senior executives of misleading investors about the company's ability to work through more than $1.25 billion in problem loans and foreclosed properties, even as that pile of distressed assets kept expanding rather than shrinking.

The new suit lands against a backdrop of sustained investor and regulatory scrutiny of Arbor's loan book. An earlier securities class action against the company, covering purchasers of Arbor stock between May 2021 and July 2024, was dismissed by a federal judge in the same district in March 2026 after the court found the plaintiffs had failed to adequately plead how the company's statements misled investors; that case closed in May 2026 after no amended complaint was filed. Separately, Arbor has faced a wave of borrower-side litigation, including a $175 million suit by former owners of a Georgia apartment complex alleging the REIT strung them along on a bridge-loan refinancing before foreclosing and buying the property through an affiliate, amid reported federal scrutiny of Arbor's broader lending practices.

## What the New Complaint Alleges

According to reporting on the complaint, the new suit asserts claims under Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and SEC Rule 10b-5, alleging that Arbor and two senior executives made materially misleading statements about the company's progress in resolving its nonperforming loans and real-estate-owned properties. The complaint points to a March 11, 2026 investor presentation in which the company projected it would reduce its problem-asset pile, and alleges that subsequent earnings calls and quarterly filings continued to reassure investors even as new delinquencies in Arbor's bridge-loan portfolio accumulated faster than the company could work them out. The two senior executives are also named under Section 20(a) of the Exchange Act as alleged controlling persons of the company. No court has yet ruled on the claims, and Arbor has not been reported as having responded to the complaint.

## Why This Keeps Happening to Bridge Lenders

Arbor's business model — originating short-term, higher-interest bridge loans to multifamily owners with the expectation that many will convert into long-term, lower-cost agency financing through Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac — has come under particular stress as higher interest rates and softer multifamily fundamentals have left more borrowers unable to refinance out of their bridge loans on schedule. When a bridge loan does not convert as planned, the lender is left holding a nonperforming asset or forced into foreclosure, and the REIT's quarterly disclosures about the size and trend of that nonperforming pile become the central battleground in exactly the kind of securities suit Arbor now faces for a second time. The prior, dismissed class action and this new one share a common structural theory: that a lender's own public confidence about resolving its troubled book outpaced the underlying reality disclosed, however gradually, in later periods.

## Practical Takeaways

For publicly traded mortgage REITs with large bridge-loan books, the Arbor litigation underscores the risk of issuing specific, date-stamped projections about resolving problem assets — a projection that does not pan out on schedule becomes the natural anchor for a follow-on securities claim once the gap between forecast and actual performance becomes visible in later quarters. REIT boards and management teams should expect that each quarterly disclosure of nonperforming-loan trends will be measured against prior guidance by plaintiffs' counsel, not just by analysts, and should calibrate forward-looking statements about workout timelines accordingly. Directors and officers of lenders with meaningful multifamily bridge-loan exposure should also note that a previously dismissed securities suit covering an earlier class period does not immunize the company against a new suit covering a later period and a different set of disclosures; each quarter's statements are assessed on their own. Finally, the overlap between this securities suit and Arbor's separate borrower-side litigation is a reminder that a lender's foreclosure and workout practices and its public disclosures about those same practices are increasingly litigated on parallel tracks, with developments in one often cited as context in the other.
