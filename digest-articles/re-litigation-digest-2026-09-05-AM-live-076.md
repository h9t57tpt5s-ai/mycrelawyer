# $17.7 Million Foreclosure Suit Hits Loop Retail Condo as CMBS Special Servicer Moves on State Street Landlord

### Rialto Capital's action against Jenel's 36 South State Street retail condo underscores the maturity-default wave still working through downtown Chicago retail debt

A CMBS special servicer has filed suit to foreclose on a two-floor retail condominium at the base of a 21-story tower on Chicago's State Street, the latest sign that loan maturity defaults continue to work their way through the Loop's distressed commercial debt even in property types, ground-floor retail at a trophy corner, that once looked more resilient than office. Rialto Capital Advisors, acting as special servicer for a securitized commercial mortgage loan trust, filed the foreclosure action on April 1, 2026 in the Circuit Court of Cook County, Illinois, against Jenel Management Corp. and affiliated Jenel Real Estate entities, the New York-based landlord that owns the roughly 20,211-square-foot retail condominium occupying the first two floors of the tower at 36 South State Street, at the corner of State and Monroe.

The dispute traces back to a 2018 transaction in which Jenel purchased the retail condominium from Thor Equities, the New York investment firm led by Joe Sitt, for approximately $26 million, assuming roughly $16.8 million of existing mortgage debt in the process. According to Rialto's complaint, that loan reached its scheduled maturity date and went unpaid, triggering the default now at issue. Rialto's filing puts the total amount owed at approximately $17.7 million, a figure that reflects not just the original principal balance but also default interest, late fees, and liquidation costs that accrue once a CMBS loan is transferred to special servicing and enforcement begins. Cook County's circuit court has scheduled a hearing in the matter for September 28, 2026, which will be an early test of how quickly the foreclosure can proceed and whether Jenel intends to contest the default or negotiate a resolution before judgment.

The case is procedurally a straightforward commercial mortgage foreclosure, but its context matters more than its mechanics. Retail condominiums at high-profile downtown corners were, for much of the past decade, treated as a relatively defensive CRE asset class: unlike office towers grappling with a structural shift toward remote and hybrid work, ground-floor retail in a dense urban core depends primarily on foot traffic and tenancy rather than corporate space decisions. The State Street corridor, however, has weathered its own version of the broader Loop's struggles, with elevated retail vacancy, a slower-than-hoped-for recovery in downtown pedestrian volume, and financing that was underwritten against pre-pandemic rent and occupancy assumptions now colliding with maturities in a materially higher interest-rate environment. When a loan originated years ago comes due today, refinancing it often means qualifying for a smaller loan amount at a meaningfully higher rate, a gap that can be difficult to bridge without fresh equity, and one that pushes an increasing number of otherwise performing properties into technical default simply because the maturity balloon cannot be refinanced on comparable terms.

For landlords and lenders alike, the Rialto-Jenel dispute is a reminder that special servicers move deliberately once a maturity default occurs. CMBS pooling and servicing agreements generally require the servicer to act in the collective interest of certificate holders, which in practice means pursuing foreclosure, receivership, or a negotiated workout on a timeline dictated by the trust's governing documents rather than by any particular borrower's preferences. A borrower facing an approaching maturity wall has meaningfully more leverage, and more workout options, before a default occurs and a loan transfers to special servicing than after a servicer has already filed suit. Once litigation begins, the practical range of outcomes narrows toward a negotiated forbearance or discounted payoff struck under the pressure of a pending court date, a receivership that displaces the borrower from day-to-day control of the asset, or a foreclosure sale that extinguishes the borrower's equity entirely.

The matter is also a useful data point for anyone underwriting or acquiring downtown Chicago retail debt or equity today. A loan secured by a well-located, seemingly stable retail condominium can still default on maturity even without a precipitating tenant bankruptcy or a sudden drop in occupancy, simply because the debt-service coverage and loan-to-value assumptions embedded in a 2018-vintage loan no longer clear the market's current underwriting bar. Buyers evaluating distressed or maturing CRE debt in the Loop should treat a "clean" performance history, no missed payments, no tenant defaults, as necessary but no longer sufficient assurance that a loan will refinance on its own terms, and should model maturity risk independently of a property's operating performance.

**Practical takeaways:** Commercial borrowers with loans approaching maturity, particularly those originated at pre-2022 rate and valuation assumptions, should engage lenders on extension or refinancing terms well before the maturity date rather than waiting for a monetary default to force the conversation. Owners of ground-floor retail condominiums in mixed-use towers should recognize that even resilient asset types are exposed to the same maturity-wall dynamics driving office foreclosures, and should stress-test refinancing assumptions against current rather than historical debt markets. Once a CMBS loan transfers to special servicing, borrowers should expect litigation to proceed on the trust's timeline and should retain workout counsel promptly, since negotiating leverage narrows considerably after a foreclosure complaint is filed.
