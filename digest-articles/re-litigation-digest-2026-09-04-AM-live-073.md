# Data Center Developer's Suit Over Kentucky Moratorium Turns on a Threshold Annexation Fight

### Kentucky Industrial Alliance's challenge to Cave City's year-long data-center moratorium has been consolidated and reframed around whether the developer's land was ever properly annexed into the city at all

A zoning dispute over a proposed gigawatt-scale data center campus near Mammoth Cave National Park has become one of the more closely watched test cases in the fast-growing wave of local moratoria aimed at data center development, and a procedural ruling handed down in August 2026 has reshaped how it will be decided. In Barren Circuit Court, Judge John T. Alexander agreed to consolidate two lawsuits filed by Kentucky Industrial Alliance LLC against the City of Cave City, its council, and the Cave City–Barren County Joint Planning Commission, and to resolve a threshold question — whether the developer's land was ever properly annexed into city limits — before reaching the merits of whether Cave City's 12-month data-center moratorium can lawfully block the project.

## What Happened

Kentucky Industrial Alliance LLC owns more than 600 acres in and around Cave City, a small tourism-dependent city in south-central Kentucky best known as a gateway to Mammoth Cave. On May 11, 2026, the company submitted an application to the Cave City–Barren County Joint Planning Commission proposing a large-scale data center project on roughly 380 acres along Doyle Avenue. Cave City's council responded by convening two special meetings, on May 18 and May 20, and enacting a 12-month moratorium on new data-center development within city limits, citing the need to study the infrastructure, water, power, and land-use implications of large computing facilities before permitting more of them.

Kentucky Industrial Alliance filed suit on June 8, arguing that its May 11 application predated the moratorium and should therefore be reviewed under the zoning regulations in effect at the time of filing — a vested-rights theory common in land-use litigation, under which a landowner who submits a complete, compliant application before a new restriction takes effect is entitled to have that application judged under the old rules. The company asked the court to declare the moratorium unlawful as applied to its project. Cave City moved to dismiss, countering that Kentucky Industrial Alliance's May 11 submission was only a two-page form lacking the development plan, engineering drawings, and other materials the planning ordinance requires for an application to be considered complete — meaning, in the city's view, no genuine "application" existed before the moratorium took effect.

A second, related suit followed, and in August the parties agreed to consolidate both cases before Judge Alexander and to litigate the annexation question first: whether the Doyle Avenue property was ever validly brought within Cave City's municipal boundaries in the first place. If it was not, the moratorium — a city ordinance — would not reach the property regardless of how the vested-rights dispute over the application's completeness is ultimately resolved, making annexation a potentially case-dispositive threshold issue. Discovery on that question is now underway, with a status conference set for November 2026 to determine whether the parties are ready to set a briefing schedule for Judge Alexander to decide it.

## Why It Matters for Commercial Real Estate

Cave City is a small jurisdiction, but the legal mechanics being tested here are the same ones playing out in dozens of communities nationwide as data center developers race to lock up land and power capacity ahead of a wave of local moratoria and restrictive zoning ordinances. The vested-rights question — whether a project submitted before a moratorium is grandfathered under prior zoning — is emerging as the central battleground in this litigation wave, and how courts define what counts as a "complete" application before a cutoff date will materially affect how developers structure and time their entitlement filings going forward. The annexation twist adds a second, largely independent layer of risk: a project's underlying jurisdictional status — whether particular parcels are validly within a city's regulatory reach at all — can determine the outcome of a zoning fight even before the vested-rights merits are reached, an issue diligence teams do not always scrutinize with the same rigor as zoning classification itself.

For developers, lenders, and infrastructure investors pursuing large-footprint projects — data centers prominently among them, but the same dynamics apply to industrial and logistics campuses — the case is a reminder that municipalities facing community pushback over large developments are increasingly turning to emergency moratoria as a stopgap, and that the timing and completeness of a pre-moratorium application filing can become dispositive litigation evidence rather than a mere formality.

## Practical Takeaways

- Developers filing land-use applications ahead of anticipated moratoria should ensure the submission meets every element of a "complete application" under the local ordinance, since incompleteness is the most common basis municipalities use to argue vested rights never attached.
- Before relying on a municipality's zoning authority over a parcel, diligence teams should independently confirm the property's annexation history and current jurisdictional status rather than assuming municipal boundaries as depicted are legally sound.
- The rise of local data-center moratoria nationwide means developers in this sector should build entitlement-timeline risk, including the possibility of litigation over vested rights, into project underwriting and financing milestones.
- Because threshold procedural issues like annexation can resolve a zoning case without ever reaching the merits, parties to comparable disputes should assess jurisdictional and procedural defenses early rather than focusing litigation strategy solely on substantive zoning arguments.
