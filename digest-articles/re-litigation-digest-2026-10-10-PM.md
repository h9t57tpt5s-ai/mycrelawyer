# Deutsche Bank Moves to Foreclose on Brookfield's Pembroke Lakes Mall as $260M CMBS Loan Craters

### A severely underwater mortgage, a receiver request, and a mall valuation that has fallen 73% since 2013 frame the latest high-profile CMBS foreclosure fight in South Florida retail.

Deutsche Bank National Trust Company has filed suit in Broward County Circuit Court to foreclose on Pembroke Lakes Mall, a roughly 750,000-square-foot regional mall in Pembroke Pines, Florida owned by an affiliate of Brookfield's real estate platform (BGRE, formerly Brookfield Properties). The suit, reported October 8, 2026, seeks both a foreclosure judgment against borrower Pembroke Lakes Mall LLC and the immediate appointment of a receiver — Carmen Spinoso of Spinoso Real Estate Group — to take control of the property while the case proceeds. For commercial mortgage lenders, special servicers, and mall owners nationally, the filing is the latest data point in a now-familiar pattern: a loan originated at peak retail valuations, pushed past maturity without refinancing, and converted into contested litigation over control of the asset itself.

## What the Complaint Alleges

Deutsche Bank brings the suit as trustee for a 2013-vintage, single-asset, single-borrower commercial mortgage-backed securities trust — GSMS 2013-PEMB, sponsored by Goldman Sachs — that holds the $260 million, 12-year, interest-only, non-recourse loan secured by the mall. The loan matured on March 1, 2025, and the complaint alleges Pembroke Lakes Mall LLC failed to repay it, triggering a maturity default. Deutsche Bank says it notified the borrower of default in April 2025 and again in April 2026, and that the total amount now due — principal, accrued interest, and default interest — stood at approximately $263.8 million as of September 1, 2026.

According to Kroll Bond Rating Agency (KBRA), which rates the GSMS 2013-PEMB securitization and affirmed its ratings as recently as September 30, 2026, BGRE requested a three-year extension of the maturity date, which was not granted. The loan was transferred to special servicing following the 2025 maturity default, a step that typically precedes either a negotiated workout, a forbearance, or — as here — foreclosure litigation when the parties cannot agree on terms.

The valuation collapse underlying the default is stark. The property was appraised at $427 million when the loan closed in 2013. A more recent appraisal, dated April 2026, values the mall at just $112 million — a decline of roughly 73 percent. That gap between the outstanding debt and the collateral's current value leaves the lender meaningfully undersecured, which helps explain both the push for an extension by the sponsor and the lender's decision to pursue foreclosure and receivership rather than continue negotiating.

## The Legal Mechanics: Foreclosure Plus Receivership

Procedurally, the case follows the standard playbook for a defaulted CMBS loan secured by Florida commercial real estate. Florida permits a mortgagee to seek the appointment of a receiver concurrently with a foreclosure action, particularly where the lender can show the collateral's income stream or physical condition is at risk without independent management. By asking the court to install Spinoso Real Estate Group immediately, rather than waiting for a final foreclosure judgment, Deutsche Bank is seeking to secure control over leasing decisions, operating expenses, and tenant relationships at the mall while the underlying foreclosure claim is litigated — insulating the collateral (and ultimately, the CMBS certificate holders) from further deterioration during what could be a multi-month or multi-year process.

This two-track approach — foreclose on the debt, but separately and urgently seek a receiver — has become the default lender strategy in large CMBS defaults nationally, visible in recent high-profile cases involving defaulted office towers and malls alike. It reflects a judgment that control of operations, not just eventual title, is often the more immediate and more valuable prize when a loan craters this far underwater.

## Why This Matters Beyond Pembroke Pines

For mall owners and other retail sponsors carrying 2012-2013-vintage CMBS debt now reaching maturity after a decade-plus of structural retail headwinds, this case illustrates the arithmetic that special servicers and courts are increasingly confronting: when a decade-old appraisal no longer bears any relationship to current value, extension requests become much harder to justify economically, and lenders have strong incentive to move quickly into receivership rather than continue carrying a severely impaired loan. For REITs and institutional owners with similar legacy mall debt approaching or past maturity, Pembroke Lakes Mall is a reminder that special servicers are willing to litigate aggressively — including seeking immediate receivership — rather than extend accommodations once a valuation gap of this magnitude is documented.

### Practical Takeaways

Borrowers facing a maturity default on legacy retail CMBS debt should assume that a wide gap between current appraised value and outstanding loan balance will make servicers and trustees far less receptive to extension requests, and should be prepared for foreclosure and receivership filings to follow quickly once an extension is denied. Owners should also recognize that lenders will often pursue receivership as an independent, faster remedy alongside — not merely following — a foreclosure claim, meaning operational control of a distressed asset can shift away from the sponsor well before any final judgment. Finally, special servicers and their counsel should document maturity-date defaults, notice history, and current valuations carefully and contemporaneously, since those facts (as here) form the evidentiary backbone of both the foreclosure claim and the request for a receiver.
