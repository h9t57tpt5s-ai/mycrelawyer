# A $1 Credit Filing Puts a $20 Billion Data Center's Power Supply in Federal Court

### FERC has preserved the transmission agreement behind Hillwood and PowerHouse's Joliet, Illinois mega-campus, but left the underlying contract fight over a disputed $1 letter of credit to a federal judge in Chicago.

A regulatory dispute over a single dollar has become one of the more closely watched commercial real estate fights tied to the AI data center boom. On September 22, 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected an attempt by Commonwealth Edison Company, the Chicago-area utility subsidiary of Exelon Corporation, to cancel the transmission security agreement underpinning a roughly $20 billion, 1.8-gigawatt data center campus that developer PowerHouse Hillwood Holding LLC is building on nearly 795 acres in Joliet, Illinois. FERC's order keeps the agreement alive for now, but it declined to resolve the contract dispute that triggered ComEd's cancellation notice in the first place, leaving that fight to the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, where litigation between the two sides is already underway.

The dispute centers on credit support. Transmission security agreements of this kind typically require a developer to post financial guarantees covering the costs a utility incurs to build out grid capacity for a large new customer. PowerHouse Hillwood — a joint venture between PowerHouse Data Centers and Hillwood, the Ross Perot Jr.-backed real estate firm behind the project — contends that it satisfied the agreement's initial credit support obligation by posting a letter of credit for $1, reading the contract's terms as not requiring a larger guarantee until power delivery to the site draws closer, with build-out not expected until around 2029. ComEd disagreed, arguing the token filing fails to provide the kind of meaningful financial backstop the agreement was meant to secure, and filed a notice with FERC on July 24, 2026 seeking to cancel the agreement outright. PowerHouse Hillwood moved on August 14 to reject that cancellation, setting up the fight that FERC resolved procedurally, if not substantively, two months later.

FERC's September 22 order did not pick a winner. The commission found that interpreting the disputed contract language was a matter the federal courts were just as well positioned to handle as the agency itself, and declined to assert primary jurisdiction over the question — a move that keeps the transmission agreement technically in force while the interpretive dispute plays out in litigation. At least one commissioner, David LaCerte, used the occasion to question publicly whether a $1 credit posting could ever function as meaningful financial protection for a utility and its ratepayers, signaling that the agency's forbearance on the merits should not be read as an endorsement of the developer's position.

The fight has not stayed confined to FERC. According to a November 2, 2026 report from The Real Deal, a federal judge overseeing the related litigation in Chicago recently denied a request by PowerHouse Hillwood to reinstate a separate, related retail electricity service agreement, ruling that authority over that particular question rests with the Illinois Commerce Commission rather than the federal court. The same report states that the judge, in the course of that ruling, described the underlying contract language as poorly drafted — a comment the outlet suggested could cut in the developer's favor on the central credit-support question still to be decided. That characterization has not yet been corroborated elsewhere and should be treated as provisional pending the court's own written orders.

The underlying project is not new to litigation. The Joliet City Council approved the campus's annexation and rezoning by an 8-1 vote on March 19, 2026, and a separate suit by the nonprofit Joliet Residents for Responsible Growth challenging that approval on public-notice and open-meetings grounds remains pending before a Will County circuit judge. The ComEd dispute is a distinct legal front: rather than questioning whether the project should have been approved, it goes to whether the single largest practical input a hyperscale data center campus needs — a secured, binding commitment of transmission capacity from the regional utility — can be unwound by the utility itself over a disagreement about how much financial security the developer owes and when.

For commercial real estate lenders, equity partners, and other developers chasing data center capacity nationally, the Joliet fight is a preview of a risk category that is likely to recur as utilities across the country negotiate ever-larger interconnection and transmission agreements with hyperscale developers. A contract provision addressing the timing and size of credit support — often treated as boilerplate relative to headline capacity and pricing terms — is proving capable of putting a multibillion-dollar project's power supply, and by extension its financing and construction timeline, into federal court. Counsel structuring similar utility agreements should expect that ambiguity in credit-support language will be tested aggressively by both sides once a project's economics or a utility's risk tolerance shifts, and that a regulator's procedural deference to the courts, as FERC exercised here, does not resolve commercial uncertainty — it only relocates it.
