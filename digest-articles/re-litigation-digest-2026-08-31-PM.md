# Second Circuit Upholds $4.8M Fine Against Roosevelt Field Mall Owner, But a Concurrence Opens a New Fault Line

### A ruling affirming Nassau County's steep penalty for missed financial disclosures also floats a narrower question — whether corporations can invoke the Excessive Fines Clause at all — that owners nationwide should watch

A commercial property owner's bid to strike down one of the steepest municipal reporting penalties in the country has failed, but not without a notable wrinkle. On August 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit affirmed a $4.8 million fine levied against The Retail Property Trust, the entity that holds title to Roosevelt Field, one of the largest shopping malls in the United States, for failing to file mandatory financial disclosures with Nassau County's Department of Assessment. The decision, *The Retail Property Trust v. Nassau County Department of Assessment*, No. 25-907 (2d Cir. Aug. 6, 2026), is a clean win for local tax authorities — but a separate opinion from one judge on the panel raises a constitutional question that could echo well beyond Long Island.

At issue is Nassau County's Annual Statement of Income and Expense Law, known as the ASIE Law, which requires owners of income-producing commercial property to report detailed financial data — rent rolls, operating expenses, and related figures — to the county assessor each year. The stated purpose is straightforward: assessors cannot accurately value large commercial properties, and therefore cannot tax them fairly, without visibility into the income those properties actually generate. Owners who decline to comply do not simply face a flat penalty; the ASIE Law calculates fines as a percentage of the property's market value, an approach designed to make noncompliance costly in direct proportion to what is at stake.

The Retail Property Trust — an entity within the ownership structure controlled by Simon Property Group, the publicly traded mall operator — did not file the required statements for two consecutive years. Nassau County assessed a cumulative fine of $4.8 million. Rather than pay and report, the Trust sued, arguing the ASIE Law's penalty scheme violates the Eighth Amendment's Excessive Fines Clause, the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, and several New York state-law provisions. The case had been working its way through the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York for years before the district court granted summary judgment in the county's favor, teeing up the appeal.

The Second Circuit's core holding tracks a familiar proportionality analysis. Excessive Fines Clause claims turn on whether a penalty is "grossly disproportional" to the gravity of the underlying offense — a standard that looks at both the harm caused and the defendant's culpability. The panel reasoned that a $4.8 million fine, while large in absolute terms, amounts to a small fraction of one percent of the assessed value of a multi-hundred-million-dollar regional mall. Weighed against two straight years of willful noncompliance with a valid, generally applicable reporting law — not a one-time lapse or a good-faith dispute over interpretation — the court found nothing grossly disproportional about the county's response. The Due Process and state-law claims fared no better.

The more consequential thread runs through a separate opinion by one member of the panel, who agreed the fine should stand but declined to join the majority's apparent assumption that the Excessive Fines Clause protects corporate entities in the first place. That question — whether a constitutional guarantee rooted in protecting individuals from punitive government overreach extends with equal force to corporate landowners — has never been squarely resolved by the Supreme Court in this context, and the concurrence's skepticism signals that the issue remains live even where, as here, it did not change the outcome.

For commercial owners, the immediate lesson is procedural rather than constitutional: disclosure mandates like the ASIE Law are not paperwork owners can treat as optional or low-priority, because the penalty structure is built to scale with exactly the kind of high-value assets that draw the least sympathy from reviewing courts. Owners operating in Nassau County, and in the growing number of jurisdictions — including several elsewhere in New York and beyond — that have adopted comparable income-and-expense reporting regimes, should treat annual filing deadlines as fixed compliance obligations tied directly to dollar exposure, not discretionary submissions to weigh against the cost of preparing the underlying data. At the same time, the panel's internal disagreement over corporate standing under the Excessive Fines Clause is worth tracking closely: if that reasoning gains traction in future cases, it could reshape how commercial owners contest not just ASIE-style penalties, but a broader universe of value-linked regulatory fines — code-violation penalties, rent-registration fines, and similar assessments — that municipalities increasingly use as revenue and compliance tools against large corporate property owners.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Treat annual income-and-expense reporting mandates as strict, non-negotiable compliance deadlines; penalties under statutes like the ASIE Law scale with property value and compound with repeated noncompliance, making willful delay far costlier than the burden of timely filing.
- Courts assess "gross disproportionality" against both the property's value and the owner's culpability — a large fine measured as a small percentage of a high-value asset, imposed after repeated willful violations, is unlikely to be struck down on Eighth Amendment grounds.
- Watch for further litigation testing whether corporations can invoke the Excessive Fines Clause at all; this concurrence suggests the question is unsettled and could affect how commercial owners challenge other value-linked regulatory penalties going forward.
- Portfolio owners with holdings across multiple jurisdictions should audit local reporting-compliance calendars now, since disclosure-and-penalty regimes modeled on Nassau County's approach are spreading among tax authorities seeking better visibility into commercial property income.
