# That "Looks Good — Thanks" Email Just Cost Two Property Owners $100,000

### A Massachusetts appeals court holds that settlement terms hashed out between lawyers by email bound the parties to a deal — even after one side tried to walk away before the paperwork was formally signed by every party

When litigators negotiate a settlement by email, they tend to assume the deal isn't real until someone signs a formal agreement. A Massachusetts Appeals Court ruling handed down October 1, 2026, in *Zammuto v. Damianidis*, should disabuse them of that assumption. The court held that two property owners were bound to pay $100,000 under a settlement reached almost entirely through back-and-forth emails between counsel — and that they could not escape that obligation simply by declining to go through with the underlying real estate sale the settlement was built around.

The case began as an ordinary failed development deal. Paul and Irene Damianidis owned two adjacent parcels in Ashland, Massachusetts, each held through a separate realty trust. Charles Zammuto agreed to buy both properties with plans to build an apartment building, and in the course of that deal obtained the special permits needed for the project. Before closing, a title defect surfaced: a 12-foot strip of land sitting between the two parcels had an unresolved ownership question. Zammuto offered to split the cost of clearing up the title problem so the sale could proceed. The Damianidises instead walked away from the transaction entirely, and Zammuto sued.

Litigation produced a three-way settlement structure rather than a simple damages award. The parties agreed that a third entity, Evolution, would step in and purchase the properties from the Damianidises. As part of that arrangement, Zammuto's special permits for the apartment project would transfer to Evolution, and Zammuto would be paid $100,000 out of the sale proceeds at closing in exchange for dismissing and releasing his claims against the sellers. None of this was unusual for a multi-party real estate settlement. What made the case turn out the way it did was how the agreement actually got finalized — and how it then fell apart.

The record shows the deal moving forward almost entirely over email. On November 12, 2021, the attorney for Evolution's principal — referred to in the proceedings as Hanzi — emailed the other lawyers approving the negotiated terms with a three-sentence message that closed with "Looks good — thanks." Four days later, on November 16, the Damianidises signed the settlement agreement and emailed Zammuto and Hanzi to ask how they wanted to handle execution. Zammuto signed in person at Hanzi's office on the morning of November 23; later that same morning, Hanzi's attorney emailed the Damianidises' counsel showing he had already begun preparing the paperwork to transfer the site plan approval, exactly as the settlement contemplated. The one signature still missing was Evolution's own. Before that signature was obtained, the Damianidises pulled out of the sale to Evolution altogether, apparently on the theory that without a fully executed four-party document, nothing had actually been agreed.

The Appeals Court rejected that theory. It held that the email exchanges, taken together, established that the parties had reached a binding settlement agreement — the Damianidises' own signature, their lawyers' email confirming the terms, and the conduct that followed (including preparing the permit-transfer paperwork) were enough to show mutual assent to a deal with definite terms, notwithstanding that Evolution's formal signature had not yet been affixed. The court further found that the Damianidises bore an implied obligation to make a good-faith effort to see the underlying sale through, since the $100,000 payment to Zammuto was structured to come from that sale's proceeds. Having undermined the condition themselves by backing out, the sellers could not then point to the unsatisfied condition — Evolution's completed purchase — as an excuse to avoid paying. The court ordered the $100,000 paid.

For commercial real estate practitioners, the decision is a pointed reminder that courts increasingly treat email exchanges between counsel as capable of forming binding contracts, settlement agreements included, once the core terms have been confirmed and the parties have begun acting consistently with those terms. A side that wants to preserve the right to walk away before a formal signing needs to say so explicitly and contemporaneously — a "subject to execution of a final agreement" or "non-binding until signed by all parties" line in the emails themselves, not an assumption that the formalities will protect it later. The decision also illustrates the risk inherent in multi-party settlements that route a payment through a dependent transaction: a seller who controls whether that transaction closes cannot treat its own decision to kill the deal as a shield against the payment obligation the settlement was built to deliver.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Treat email negotiations over settlement terms as potentially binding the moment the other side confirms agreement, even informally ("looks good — thanks" was enough here); if you need more time or protection, say explicitly that nothing is final until a formal document is signed by every party.
- When a settlement's payment term depends on a related transaction closing (here, a sale to a third-party buyer), expect a court to read in a good-faith obligation to pursue that transaction — don't assume you can control the outcome by simply declining to proceed.
- Document any contingency or walk-away right you want to preserve directly in the negotiating correspondence; silence will be read against the party that later tries to invoke it.
- Multi-party settlements involving a buyer, a seller, and a separate claimant create several points where one party's unilateral action can trigger disputes over the whole structure — build in clear sequencing and consequences for non-performance at the drafting stage, not after someone has already walked.
