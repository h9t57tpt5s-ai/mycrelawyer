# When the Developer Never Leaves: Doral Condo Owners Sue Over Retained Association Control

### A declaratory judgment suit against Grand at Doral I's developer-affiliated unit holders puts a recurring Florida condo-conversion problem back in front of a judge

Four years after Grand at Doral I welcomed its first residents, the 80-unit condominium's owners' association has gone to court to settle a question that has quietly shaped life in the building since it opened: who actually controls it. The association filed a declaratory judgment action in Miami-Dade County Circuit Court against entities affiliated with the building's original developer, the Pablo Jose Valdes Florida Irrevocable Trust, alleging that the developer never fully relinquished control of the property even though construction wrapped in 2022. According to the complaint, the trust transferred 41 of the building's 80 units — a bare majority — to two affiliated companies, Prime Properties at Doral and Premier Properties at Doral, both managed by Lucilla Suero and Niurka Fonte-Esquivel in their capacity as co-trustees of the Valdes trust. That concentration of ownership, the association says, has left the developer's affiliates holding the lion's share of votes at the building years after the point at which control was supposed to pass to independent unit owners.

The dispute is not simply about vote counting. The complaint alleges that when the trust conveyed those units to Prime and Premier, it also granted the two entities a package of "developer rights" that ordinary owners do not have — including the ability to rent out their units freely and an exemption from the leasing restrictions and capital-contribution obligations that apply to everyone else in the building. The association is asking the court to determine, as a matter of law, whether that arrangement is valid: whether Prime and Premier are in fact exempt from the building's unit-leasing restrictions, and how parking spaces tied to an adjacent lot controlled by the Valdes trust should be assigned among owners.

What makes the case more than a routine internal governance dispute is a structural problem the association's own attorneys have flagged: under Grand at Doral I's governing documents, amending the declaration to eliminate Prime and Premier's exemptions would itself require a vote of Prime and Premier, since they control the votes needed to approve any such change. In other words, the only parties capable of approving an amendment to strip the disputed benefits are the very parties who benefit from them. That circularity is precisely the kind of governance lock that Florida courts and legislators have grown increasingly skeptical of in recent years, as condominium associations statewide have pushed back against developers who structure post-completion ownership to preserve outsized influence long after a building is sold out.

Florida law generally requires developer-controlled boards to transition to unit-owner control once a statutory threshold of units has been sold — typically once 90% of units in a condominium have been conveyed to non-developer purchasers, though the mechanics vary by declaration and by how "developer" affiliates are defined. Developers and their counsel have, in a range of matters across South Florida in recent years, structured post-turnover ownership through affiliated LLCs precisely to preserve leverage over amendments, assessments, and leasing policy after the formal transition date has technically passed. Litigation over these structures typically turns on close reading of the declaration's own definitions — whether an entity that is legally distinct from the developer but commonly controlled and originally conveyed units as part of the developer's own allocation should be treated as a developer affiliate for voting and disclosure purposes, or as an ordinary owner entitled to vote its full block.

For Grand at Doral I's independent owners, the practical stakes are straightforward: leasing flexibility, assessment obligations, and control over the building's finances and maintenance priorities all hinge on the outcome. For the broader Florida condominium market — already reshaped by post-Surfside structural reforms that increased financial and disclosure burdens on associations statewide — the case is a reminder that governance battles over developer influence remain very much alive even in buildings with no structural safety issues at all.

## Practical takeaways

Condominium developers structuring post-completion ownership through affiliated entities should expect close judicial scrutiny of arrangements that concentrate voting power or preserve exemptions unavailable to other owners, particularly where those exemptions can only be undone with the affiliate's own consent. Unit owners and newly independent boards evaluating a developer transition should audit the declaration for circular amendment requirements before assuming that a nominal transfer of control has actually shifted governing authority. Commercial owners and asset managers acquiring interests in recently converted condominium buildings should diligence not just unit-count thresholds for turnover but also the identity and voting rights of any developer-affiliated unit holders who remain on the roll after conversion.
