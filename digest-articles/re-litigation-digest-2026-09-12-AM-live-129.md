# Louisville Suburb Ordered to Pay $36.3M After Jury Finds Zoning Denial Was Housing Discrimination

### A Jefferson County jury's verdict against the City of Prospect closes out a nine-year fight over a rejected affordable senior housing project — and hands developers a powerful precedent against zoning boards that use land-use process to block protected housing

A Jefferson County, Kentucky jury has ordered the City of Prospect to pay more than $36.3 million to affordable-housing developer LDG Development, finding that the wealthy Louisville suburb's decade-old rejection of a proposed 198-unit senior apartment complex violated the federal Fair Housing Act and the Equal Protection Clause. The verdict, returned August 26, 2026 after a two-week trial in Jefferson Circuit Court, includes roughly $7.3 million in punitive damages and closes out litigation that began in 2017, when local officials voted down a zoning change needed to build the project. For commercial developers, municipal land-use bodies, and the neighborhood groups that often pressure them, the case is a rare instance of a jury putting a concrete, nine-figure price tag on a zoning denial found to be discriminatory.

The dispute traces to LDG's proposal, first submitted around 2016, to build Prospect Cove, a four-story, 198-unit affordable apartment complex for seniors, including residents with disabilities, on land near the intersection of River Road and Timber Ridge Drive in Prospect. City and county planning staff initially reviewed the application favorably, but the Louisville Metro Council, which held final say over the necessary zoning change, rejected it in a 14-11 vote in 2017. LDG sued that year, naming both Louisville Metro Government and the City of Prospect, and alleging that the denial was not a legitimate land-use decision but a pretext for excluding low-income and, the complaint argued, disproportionately Black tenants from an affluent, largely white suburb. The lawsuit invoked both the Fair Housing Act's prohibition on housing decisions motivated by race or other protected characteristics and the Fourteenth Amendment's equal protection guarantee, arguing that officials had improperly bowed to organized neighborhood opposition rather than evaluating the project on its planning merits.

The nearly decade-long litigation moved in fits and starts, including an early setback for LDG when a trial-level ruling upheld the rejection, before the case eventually proceeded toward trial on claims against both government defendants. Louisville Metro Government broke from the City of Prospect first, agreeing in August 2025 to a $6 million settlement that also committed city officials to legislative changes intended to streamline the approval process for future affordable-housing applications. That left Prospect as the sole remaining defendant when the case went to trial in March 2026 before a Jefferson Circuit Court jury, culminating in the August verdict.

The jury sided with LDG on five of eight counts submitted for its consideration, awarding damages that credited the developer's claims of lost value, delay-related costs, and reputational harm tied to the years-long fight to build the project, along with punitive damages reflecting a finding that Prospect's conduct went beyond ordinary land-use disagreement. LDG's trial counsel, from the Louisville office of Dinsmore & Shohl, characterized the result as a landmark fair-housing verdict, and the developer has said it intends to move forward with construction of the senior housing complex, targeting a groundbreaking in late 2026 or early 2027 -- meaning the project Prospect spent nearly a decade fighting will likely still get built, just years later and considerably more expensively for the city that resisted it.

The case sits at the intersection of two areas of law that commercial real estate practitioners increasingly need to track together: municipal zoning discretion and federal fair-housing liability. Local governments have long enjoyed substantial deference in land-use decisions, and courts are generally reluctant to second-guess a zoning board's judgment about density, traffic, or infrastructure capacity. But that deference evaporates once a plaintiff can show that a facially neutral zoning process was, in substance, a vehicle for excluding a protected class of prospective residents. Discovery in FHA zoning cases frequently turns up exactly the kind of evidence that can convert a routine land-use dispute into a discrimination claim: council members' informal communications with organized opposition groups, unusual or reversed staff recommendations reached without a clear planning rationale, and a record showing that similar projects proposed for other, less affluent, less white areas moved through the same approval process without comparable resistance.

For developers building affordable or income-restricted housing, particularly in higher-income suburban jurisdictions where local opposition to density is common, the Prospect verdict is a signal that a documented pattern of pretextual zoning obstruction can support not just an eventual approval on remand but substantial money damages, including punitive damages, assessed directly against the municipality. For municipal counsel and elected zoning bodies, the lesson cuts the other way: a rejection vote that departs from staff recommendations, follows organized community pressure focused on the type of prospective tenant rather than legitimate land-use criteria, and lacks a documented planning-based rationale is exposed to fair-housing liability that can ultimately cost far more than the political discomfort of approving the project would have.

## Practical Takeaways

- Municipalities and zoning boards should ensure land-use denials are supported by a documented, planning-based rationale consistent with staff recommendations; departures driven by organized opposition focused on prospective residents' income or protected characteristics create substantial Fair Housing Act and Equal Protection exposure.
- Developers facing politically charged zoning denials for affordable or supportive housing should preserve records of the application's planning merits, staff recommendations, and any public comments referencing the type of tenant the project would serve, since that record can later support a discrimination claim.
- A municipality's exposure does not end when it settles or loses at trial: LDG intends to proceed with construction, meaning a losing municipality can face both a nine-figure damages verdict and the very project it fought getting built regardless.
- Co-defendant governmental entities should weigh early settlement against the risk of a joint or several damages verdict at trial; Louisville Metro's $6 million settlement, reached roughly a year before trial, left Prospect to face the full jury verdict alone.
