# Cook County Judge Orders Investor Sean Conlon to Pay $10M+ Over Vacant Former Rainforest Cafe Building

### A breach-of-contract judgment on a personally guaranteed $8.3 million loan shows how a single missed payment on a long-vacant property can snowball into a seven-figure personal liability

A Cook County judge has ordered real estate investor and television personality Sean Conlon to pay more than $10 million to resolve breach-of-contract litigation brought by lender Alecko Capital over the long-vacant former Rainforest Cafe building in Chicago's River North neighborhood. The ruling, issued October 20, 2026 by Judge Jerry A. Esrig of the Circuit Court of Cook County, closes out roughly a year of litigation that began when Alecko sued over a defaulted $8.3 million loan Conlon had personally guaranteed, and it is a pointed illustration of how a dormant, income-producing-in-name-only commercial property can turn a routine loan default into a far larger personal exposure once default interest, fees, and litigation costs accrue.

### What happened

The 22,000-square-foot freestanding building at 605 N. Clark Street has sat empty since August 2020, when the Rainforest Cafe that had anchored the corner since 1997 closed for good amid the pandemic. A venture led by Conlon had bought the property in 2015 for about $13.7 million, betting on River North's continued strength as a dining and tourism corridor. That bet went sideways when the pandemic emptied downtown Chicago of the tourists and office workers the restaurant depended on, and it never reopened.

With the building vacant and generating no rental income, the loan secured against it became increasingly difficult to service. Alecko Capital, the private credit and real estate investment firm led by Alex Pissios, sued in October 2025, alleging Conlon had defaulted on the $8.3 million loan and seeking to enforce his personal guaranty. Conlon has told reporters the dispute traces back to a single late payment and that he was in the process of arranging refinancing when the suit was filed — a familiar refrain in distressed-debt litigation, where borrowers frequently argue a lender moved to enforce a default that could have been cured. Separately, the building's post-closure history has been complicated by a failed plan to lease the space to a cannabis dispensary, which collapsed after Illinois's buffer-zone rules barring a dispensary within 1,500 feet of an existing shop proved to be an obstacle, further delaying any path to re-tenanting the property and restoring cash flow against the debt.

Litigation over the loan proceeded for roughly a year before Judge Esrig's October 20 order, which resolved the matter by requiring Conlon to pay more than $10 million — a figure that reflects not just the original $8.3 million principal but the default interest, fees, and costs that typically accumulate once a commercial loan goes into default and a lender is forced to litigate to collect. Conlon has said publicly that he intends to pay the judgment and to retain ownership of the building rather than surrender it.

### Why it matters for commercial property owners and guarantors

The case is a useful reminder that a personal guaranty on a commercial loan does not become less dangerous just because the underlying default feels, to the borrower, like a technical or temporary lapse. Lenders routinely structure acquisition and bridge loans on commercial property with full-recourse personal guaranties precisely so that a default converts a secured, asset-level risk into unsecured personal liability extending well beyond the property's own value — and the gap between an $8.3 million loan and a $10 million-plus judgment shows how quickly default interest and litigation costs can widen that exposure once a dispute goes to judgment rather than settling early.

It is also a reminder of the particular risk profile of a single-tenant, single-use commercial building that loses its anchor tenant. Once the Rainforest Cafe closed, the property had no income stream to service its debt, and each failed attempt at re-tenanting — including the aborted cannabis dispensary plan — extended the period during which the loan sat unserviced. For lenders and investors alike, a vacant specialty-use building is a materially riskier credit than a diversified, multi-tenant asset, because there is no buffer of other rental income to absorb a single setback.

### Practical takeaways

Commercial borrowers who personally guarantee acquisition or bridge financing should treat any payment default as an immediate priority rather than something to resolve on their own timeline, since lenders are frequently entitled to accelerate the full balance, impose default interest, and add their litigation costs to the amount owed well before a borrower's refinancing plans can materialize. Owners of single-tenant specialty properties, such as a freestanding restaurant building, should build contingency plans for an extended vacancy into their underwriting and loan covenants from the outset, since re-tenanting such a space can take years and can be derailed by unrelated regulatory obstacles like zoning buffer requirements for a replacement use. And for lenders, the case underscores the value of a well-documented, broadly worded personal guaranty: it is frequently the difference between recovering a secured loan's face value and recovering a judgment that also captures the accumulated cost of the default itself.
