# Florida Developer Sues Over 157% Water Impact Fee Hike on Pending Apartment Permit

### A Palm Beach County suit tests a core protection of Florida's Impact Fee Act: whether a city can raise development fees on a project after its permit application is already in the pipeline.

A developer planning a 315-unit apartment complex in Riviera Beach, Florida sued the city's water utility on or about September 18, 2026, accusing the Riviera Beach Utility Special District of illegally applying a 157% increase in water and wastewater impact fees to a project whose permits were already pending before the higher fees took effect. The plaintiff, 7920 Riviera Beach Holdings LLC, says the fee hike added roughly $1.6 million to the cost of its planned development at 7880 North Military Trail, and that applying it to an already-submitted application violates the Florida Impact Fee Act's protection against retroactive fee increases.

The case is a compact but instructive illustration of a recurring flashpoint between fast-growing Florida municipalities financing infrastructure expansion and developers who priced a project's economics around the fee schedule in place when they filed.

## The Fees, the Timing, and the Statutory Fight

According to the complaint, the Riviera Beach Utility Special District's governing board approved the increased water and wastewater impact fees in February 2025, with the new fees taking effect June 1, 2025. The developer says it had already submitted a complete permit application for its 315-unit project by April 24, 2025 — weeks before the new fee schedule took effect — and that the per-unit impact charge nonetheless jumped from $3,940 to $10,126, a 157% increase applied to its pending application.

Florida's Impact Fee Act is designed precisely to prevent this kind of retroactive cost shock: it generally bars a local government from applying a newly adopted or increased impact fee to a permit application that was already pending before the fee's effective date, giving developers a measure of certainty that the fee schedule in place when they file will govern their project's costs. The developer's complaint leans directly on that statutory protection, arguing the timing here is exactly the scenario the statute exists to prevent.

The utility district, notably, is not primarily defending the increase on the merits of its infrastructure costs. Instead, it is reported to be arguing that the charges at issue were never "impact fees" subject to the Impact Fee Act's protections in the first place — a threshold characterization dispute that, if the district wins it, would sidestep the retroactivity question entirely regardless of when the developer's application was filed. That kind of definitional fight is common in impact-fee litigation nationally: municipalities facing a retroactivity challenge frequently argue that a disputed charge is instead a "capacity charge," "connection fee," or similarly labeled cost recovery mechanism outside the statute's scope, since many state impact-fee statutes define their own coverage narrowly by the charge's stated purpose and structure rather than by its practical effect on a developer's budget.

## Why It Matters Beyond Riviera Beach

Florida's population and infrastructure growth have made impact-fee increases a near-constant feature of the state's fastest-growing municipalities, and utility districts in particular have leaned on steep fee increases to fund water and wastewater capacity expansion without raising general rates. For developers, the case is a reminder that the protection Florida law offers against a mid-application fee increase is not self-executing — it has to be affirmatively invoked and litigated, including against a municipality's attempt to recharacterize the disputed charge outside the statute's coverage. For municipalities and their utility districts, the case is a caution that labeling a charge to avoid impact-fee statutory constraints invites exactly the kind of litigation now underway in Palm Beach County, and that the label a local ordinance uses for a fee will be tested against the statute's substance, not just its own terminology.

## Practical Takeaways

- Developers should document the exact date a permit application is deemed complete and retain that record specifically to preserve an Impact Fee Act retroactivity defense against any fee increase adopted afterward.
- A municipality's argument that a disputed charge is not a statutory "impact fee" at all is a common and sometimes successful defense strategy — developers should assess a jurisdiction's fee nomenclature and enabling ordinance early, not just the dollar amount charged.
- Fast-growing municipalities imposing steep utility or infrastructure fee increases should structure and label those charges carefully against applicable state impact-fee statutes before adoption, since a favorable characterization is far easier to establish in a properly drafted ordinance than to defend after the fact in litigation.
- Project underwriting in high-growth jurisdictions should build in contingency for fee escalation risk between application and permit issuance, since litigation to enforce a retroactivity protection can take months or years to resolve even when the developer ultimately prevails.
