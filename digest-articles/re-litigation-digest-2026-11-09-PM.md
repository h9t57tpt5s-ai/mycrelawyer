# Illinois Appeals Court: Commercial Landlords Can Assign Claims for Unpaid Rent

### A new ruling clarifies that an accrued, unpaid-rent claim is a freely transferable asset distinct from the leased property itself — with real consequences for landlords, buyers, and tenants alike.

The Appellate Court of Illinois, First District, ruled on November 9, 2026, that a commercial landlord may assign its claim to collect a tenant's already-accrued, unpaid rent to a third party, reversing a Cook County circuit court dismissal and reviving a rent-collection lawsuit against a Chicago commercial tenant. The decision resolves, as a matter of first impression under modern Illinois law, a question that has quietly unsettled landlords and their counsel for years: once rent has come due and gone unpaid, is the right to collect it tied to the property, or does it belong personally to whoever was owed it — free to sell, assign, or walk away from like any other asset?

The dispute traces to a long-term lease of commercial space on North Clark Street in Chicago. The tenant, a company doing business locally as an interiors supplier, signed a 20-year lease in 2002 obligating it to pay base rent plus a share of the property's real estate taxes and utilities. The property changed hands more than once during the lease term, and by 2021 the landlord of record was a single-asset Illinois LLC organized to hold the building. According to the complaint, while that LLC held the lease, the tenant fell behind on rent, tax, and utility charges owed under the lease. In 2024, the landlord assigned its right to collect that unpaid balance to a third party, who filed a verified complaint in Cook County in January 2025 to collect the arrears as an assignee rather than as the current landlord.

The tenant moved to dismiss, arguing the assignee lacked standing because a claim for rent that accrued to a previous landlord does not travel with an assignment made independently of the property itself. The circuit court agreed, dismissing the suit solely on standing grounds. In doing so, it relied on the Illinois Supreme Court's 1872 decision in *Kennedy v. Kennedy* and a 2018 First District opinion, *1002 E. 87th Street, LLC v. Midway Broadcasting Corp.*, both of which had been read to treat a landlord's accrued-rent claim as a type of interest that does not pass to a stranger to the lease.

The appellate panel reversed. Writing for the court, the presiding justice distinguished between the right to *future* rent — which runs with the property and automatically transfers to a new owner under settled Illinois law — and a claim for rent already due and unpaid, which the court characterized as a distinct "chose in action" held personally by the landlord to whom it was owed. Surveying Illinois Supreme Court precedent on the assignability of claims generally, the panel concluded that nothing in the modern law of assignment bars a landlord from transferring that chose in action to anyone it chooses, including a party with no other connection to the lease or the property. The court held that the circuit court's reliance on *Kennedy* and *Midway Broadcasting* was inconsistent with that modern view, reversed the dismissal, and remanded the case for further proceedings on the merits of the unpaid-rent claim — without yet deciding how much, if anything, the tenant actually owes.

For general counsel advising commercial landlords, lenders, and buyers, the ruling turns an often-overlooked bookkeeping question into a live due-diligence issue. Accrued, uncollected rent is now confirmed to be its own freely transferable asset in Illinois, separate from the real estate that generated it — which means a landlord exiting a troubled tenancy or preparing to sell a building has a genuine option to monetize or farm out stale receivables rather than simply writing them off. It also means that buyers of leased commercial property cannot assume a closing automatically carries with it the right to chase down rent that was already in arrears before the sale; if that receivable is meant to pass to the buyer, the purchase and sale agreement needs to say so in explicit terms, because absent such language, Illinois law now clearly leaves that claim with the seller, or with whomever the seller later assigns it to.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Commercial landlords can sell or assign the right to collect already-accrued, unpaid rent to any third party — a collections specialist, an affiliate, or an individual investor — without losing recourse against the delinquent tenant, and without the assignee having ever been the tenant's landlord.
- Buyers and lenders underwriting a leased commercial property should confirm, as part of diligence, whether pre-closing rent arrears are being assigned along with the deal; silence in the purchase agreement leaves that receivable with the seller under this ruling.
- Tenants facing a stale rent demand from an unfamiliar plaintiff should not assume a standing challenge will succeed merely because the plaintiff was never their landlord; Illinois courts will now look at whether the claim was properly assigned, not simply who originally held it.
