# Deutsche Finance Sues German Pension Giant BVK Over Nearly $2 Billion in US Property Losses, as Munich Corruption Probe Hits Both Sides

### A Delaware contract and defamation suit over who controlled — and who is to blame for — a soured cross-border commercial real estate portfolio has collided with a separate criminal investigation into how the deal was managed.

*Prepared as a legal news update for commercial real estate owners, lenders and asset managers.*

Deutsche Finance America LLC and its German parent, DF Deutsche Finance Holding AG, sued Bayerische Versorgungskammer (BVK), Germany's largest public pension fund, in Delaware Superior Court on September 1, 2026, over responsibility for losses on a nearly $2 billion portfolio of US commercial real estate investments. The case, originally filed as *Deutsche Finance America LLC, et al. v. Bayerische Versorgungskammer*, No. N26C-08-455 KMM CCLD, in Delaware's Complex Commercial Litigation Division, was removed by BVK to the U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware on October 16, 2026, where it is now docketed as No. 1:26-cv-01337.

Deutsche Finance's complaint asserts five claims: breach of contract, defamation, violation of the Delaware Deceptive Trade Practices Act, tortious interference with contract, and tortious interference with prospective business relations. At the center of the dispute is a factual question with obvious relevance to any institutional investor relying on a third-party manager: who actually held authority over the deals that went bad. Deutsche Finance alleges that BVK retained sole discretion to approve budgets, financing and leases across the portfolio, and failed to act as US property markets weakened beginning in 2020 — then turned around and publicly blamed Deutsche Finance for the resulting losses. Deutsche Finance says neither it nor its affiliated entities acted as discretionary target-fund managers with decision-making authority over the underlying assets. BVK has rejected the allegations and says it will defend itself "decisively."

The portfolio at issue was built in significant part alongside New York developer Michael Shvo's firm, SHVO. BVK has disclosed that it invested roughly €1.6 billion (about $1.9 billion) through vehicles tied to Deutsche Finance and Shvo, and has warned that fund-level losses could reach as much as €853 million. By the end of last year, BVK had already written off €392 million of those investments. Separate, related litigation between Shvo-affiliated entities and BVK over fee and profit-sharing disputes, including a dispute over the sale of San Francisco's Transamerica Pyramid, has been proceeding on parallel tracks in California.

The civil case took on a new dimension in early November. Munich prosecutors searched Deutsche Finance's offices on November 3 and BVK's offices on November 5, 2026, as part of a corruption investigation into BVK's US real estate program. Prosecutors say the inquiry involves a current and a former BVK employee in the pension fund's real estate investment department, along with a member of Deutsche Finance Group's management team, over allegedly improper benefits — described as invitations to high-end restaurants in Munich, London, Los Angeles and New York, and stays in a luxury hotel suite in New York. Notably, prosecutors have said that allegations of breach of trust connected to the loss-making real estate investments themselves are not currently part of the criminal probe, which instead centers on the alleged benefits. The investigation reportedly originated when BVK itself flagged potential compliance concerns to prosecutors; two senior BVK real estate managers had already departed after an internal review found compliance violations, including the lavish business travel now under scrutiny. German financial regulator BaFin has also sought information from Deutsche Finance about its US real estate investments.

For commercial real estate owners, lenders and fund sponsors, the case is a pointed illustration of how a governance dispute over decision-making authority in a complex, multi-entity investment structure can metastasize well beyond a single breach-of-contract claim. A structure built to let a sophisticated institutional investor retain ultimate control over major asset-level decisions — budgets, financing, leasing — can just as easily become the basis for that investor to disclaim responsibility for the consequences of those same decisions once the market turns. And once a dispute of this size becomes public, it tends to draw regulatory and even criminal scrutiny into underlying relationships — gifts, travel, hospitality — that might otherwise have gone unexamined.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Investment managers and institutional investors structuring joint ventures or discretionary-mandate arrangements for US commercial real estate should document, in real time, exactly which party held authority over which categories of asset-level decisions (budgets, financing, leasing, capital calls) — ambiguity on this point becomes the central battleground once a portfolio underperforms.
- A breach-of-contract and defamation suit over investment losses can trigger independent regulatory or criminal exposure tied to the parties' business courtesies and hospitality practices, even when those practices are not directly connected to the investment losses themselves; compliance programs around gifts, travel and entertainment deserve the same rigor as programs governing the investment decisions they surround.
- Cross-border institutional real estate investments carry compounding litigation risk: a single soured portfolio can generate parallel proceedings in multiple jurisdictions (here, Delaware civil litigation, a Munich criminal probe, and separate California litigation involving a co-investor), each discoverable by and relevant to the others.
