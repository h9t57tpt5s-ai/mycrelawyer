# Pennsylvania Court Upholds $3.85 Million Confessed Judgment Against Philadelphia Borrower and Guarantors

### The Superior Court of Pennsylvania affirmed denial of a borrower's bid to strike a bank's confessed judgment on a commercial mortgage loan secured by a Spring Garden Street property, even after finding the trial court used the wrong evidentiary record to get there.

The Superior Court of Pennsylvania, in a memorandum opinion by Judge Maria C. McLaughlin filed November 9, 2026, affirmed a Philadelphia trial court's denial of a borrower's and its guarantors' petition to strike or open a confessed judgment entered against them on a $3.85 million commercial mortgage loan. The decision in First Citizens Community Bank v. SA Living Fund LLC underscores how resilient confession-of-judgment clauses remain in Pennsylvania commercial lending, even when a reviewing court finds procedural fault with how the trial court reached its conclusion.

The loan at the center of the case traces to July 2022, when SA Living Fund LLC borrowed $3.85 million for an 18-month term, secured by mortgaged premises at 827 Spring Garden Street in Philadelphia. Individual guarantors Craig L. Grossman and Aaron Z. Cohen each personally guaranteed the loan under a Guaranty and Suretyship Agreement, which, like the loan documents themselves, contained a confession-of-judgment provision allowing the lender to obtain judgment against the borrower and guarantors without a trial if a default occurred. The lender, First Citizens Community Bank, is the successor by merger to Huntingdon Valley Bank, the loan's original lender.

The loan was modified twice. In March 2024, the parties extended the maturity date by one month in exchange for an extension fee, with that first modification likewise containing a confession-of-judgment clause. In November 2024, the parties executed a second modification: SA Living Fund agreed to pay $103,433.33 to bring the loan current and to prepay an additional year of interest, in exchange for which the bank extended the maturity date all the way to October 2029, subject to its right to call the loan sooner. That second modification also included a confession-of-judgment provision. According to the bank, SA Living Fund never fully made good on the payment terms of that second modification — leaving an outstanding balance of $18,463.33 on the modification payment and a further $30,010.55 in principal and interest that came due on January 1, 2025. The bank sent a default letter in January 2025 giving the borrower 15 days to cure, and when it did not, filed a complaint for confessed judgment in March 2025. The Philadelphia County prothonotary entered the confessed judgment as a ministerial act, without any judicial review of its merits — the hallmark of how confession-of-judgment clauses operate in Pennsylvania.

SA Living Fund, Grossman, and Cohen then petitioned to strike or open the judgment, arguing the bank's complaint was facially defective because it alleged default in only conclusory terms — without specifying what payments were missed, when, or in what amount — and that they had a meritorious defense because the bank's own damages calculations were materially erroneous, serious enough that the trial court itself had directed the bank to file a motion to reassess damages. The trial court denied the petition, finding that although the complaint was "not heavy on details," the record as a whole — including a January 2025 default letter introduced at the hearing — showed the borrower had not met its obligations under the second modification and had no factual basis for a defense.

On appeal, the Superior Court found that the trial court had technically erred in its approach: a motion to strike a confessed judgment may only be evaluated against the record that existed at the moment judgment was entered, not against later-submitted testimony and evidence offered in connection with the separate petition to open. But the appellate panel held that error was harmless, because the bank's underlying complaint, assessed strictly on its own terms under Pennsylvania Rule of Civil Procedure 2952, adequately alleged a default and the amount due to support the confessed judgment. The court accordingly affirmed denial of the motion to strike.

For commercial lenders and borrowers alike, the case is a reminder of how much leverage a confession-of-judgment clause still carries in Pennsylvania commercial finance, and how narrow the path to unwinding one actually is. A borrower challenging a confessed judgment on procedural grounds — the kind of facial-defect argument SA Living Fund raised — faces a demanding standard that looks only at the four corners of the record as it existed when judgment was entered, and courts will overlook even a trial court's analytical misstep if the complaint itself clears that bar. The case also illustrates that personal guaranties executed alongside a commercial mortgage loan travel with every subsequent modification: because each of the loan's modifications independently included its own confession-of-judgment clause, Grossman and Cohen remained on the hook as the loan's terms, timeline, and balance evolved over more than two years.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Borrowers and guarantors signing a commercial loan modification that includes a confession-of-judgment clause should treat each modification as a fresh exposure point, not a routine administrative update, since the lender can confess judgment under the terms of whichever modification is currently in effect.
- A motion to strike a confessed judgment lives or dies on the four corners of the complaint and the record as it stood when judgment was entered; evidence developed later, even if compelling, generally cannot be used to attack the judgment on a motion to strike, as distinct from a motion to open.
- A complaint for confession of judgment does not need extensive factual detail to survive a motion to strike in Pennsylvania — a bare-bones default allegation paired with an itemized computation of amounts due can be enough, leaving borrowers with a narrow window to challenge the judgment's validity rather than its merits.
- Where a trial court directs a lender to file a motion to reassess damages because of apparent calculation errors, that process is a separate avenue for correcting the judgment's amount and does not, on its own, undermine the validity of the underlying confessed judgment.
