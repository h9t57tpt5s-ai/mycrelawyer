# Massachusetts Court Rejects Unregistered Broker's Bid to Freeze $60 Million in Plymouth Industrial REIT Merger Proceeds

### Business Litigation Session ruling confirms unregistered intermediaries cannot enforce oral success-fee claims tied to a securities transaction, clearing the way for a $2.1 billion take-private deal

A Massachusetts judge has dismantled an eleventh-hour attempt to hold up the $2.1 billion take-private acquisition of Plymouth Industrial REIT, Inc., ruling that a real estate advisory firm's claim to a roughly $20 million success fee on the deal could not proceed because the firm was acting as an unregistered securities broker when it says it earned that fee. The ruling, issued January 20, 2026 by Justice Kenneth W. Salinger in the Business Litigation Session of Suffolk County Superior Court, denied plaintiff Redimere Advisors LLC's emergency motion for a preliminary injunction and dismissed all of its claims against Plymouth, resolving a dispute that had briefly threatened to complicate the merger's closing.

Plymouth Industrial REIT, a publicly traded industrial REIT, agreed to be acquired by an investor group led by Makarora Management LP together with funds affiliated with Ares Alternative Credit in an all-cash transaction valuing the company at $22.00 per share, or approximately $2.1 billion. Redimere Advisors, which describes itself as a real estate advisory and brokerage firm, claimed that it had orally agreed with Plymouth to earn a 2% success fee, roughly $20 million on a deal of this size, in exchange for introducing Plymouth to the Makarora and Ares buyer group and assisting in bringing the take-private transaction to a close. Plymouth countered that its only written engagement with Redimere, a letter agreement dated November 28, 2022, covered a narrower scope of equity-financing work and did not extend to the merger. When Plymouth declined to pay the claimed fee, Redimere sued and sought an emergency injunction that would have blocked the merger from closing, or alternatively diverted roughly $60 million of the merger proceeds into escrow with a third-party trustee pending resolution of its claims.

Justice Salinger's ruling disposed of the case on two independent grounds, either of which would likely have been sufficient on its own. First, the court found that the written 2022 engagement letter, by its terms, did not cover the take-private transaction with Makarora and Ares, meaning Plymouth had no contractual obligation to pay Redimere a fee on that deal regardless of what role Redimere may have played in facilitating it. Second, and more consequentially for the broader industry, the court held that any oral agreement to pay Redimere a transaction-based success fee was unenforceable because Redimere was acting as an unregistered broker in connection with a securities transaction, in violation of the registration requirements of the federal Securities Exchange Act. Because the merger involved the sale of Plymouth's publicly traded securities rather than a direct asset sale, arranging or facilitating that transaction for a transaction-based fee is activity that federal securities law reserves to registered broker-dealers, and an unregistered firm generally cannot use the courts to enforce a fee arrangement premised on having performed that function.

The decision carries meaningful implications well beyond this single dispute. Real estate advisory firms, deal originators, and finders frequently operate on the understanding that a handshake or email exchange promising a percentage-based fee for introducing a buyer is enforceable so long as the introduction in fact leads to a closed transaction. Where the underlying transaction is structured as a stock or securities deal, as REIT take-privates and public company mergers generally are, that assumption can be wrong as a matter of law regardless of the equities of who actually brought the parties together. The registration requirement exists precisely to ensure that intermediaries facilitating securities transactions are subject to regulatory oversight, and courts have shown little appetite for carving out exceptions for real estate-focused advisors who stray into M&A-style deal introduction work without registering as broker-dealers or structuring their engagement to fall within an available exemption.

For REITs and other public companies actively pursuing M&A or take-private transactions, the case is also a useful illustration of how courts weigh eleventh-hour injunction requests aimed at merger proceeds. Judges are generally reluctant to freeze deal proceeds or delay a closing based on a contested fee claim, particularly where the underlying contractual and regulatory defenses are strong enough to be resolved at the motion stage rather than requiring a full trial. Redimere's decision to seek both an injunction against the closing and, alternatively, an escrow of tens of millions of dollars in proceeds reflects a common but often unsuccessful litigation strategy: using the leverage of an approaching closing date to extract a favorable settlement, a strategy that depends on a court being willing to disrupt the transaction's timeline even where the underlying claim is legally shaky.

**Practical takeaways:** REITs and other public real estate companies negotiating with outside advisors, brokers, or deal originators should ensure written engagement letters clearly define the scope of covered transactions, since ambiguity about whether a later, larger deal falls within an earlier engagement's scope is exactly the kind of gap that generates litigation. Any intermediary expecting to earn a transaction-based fee on a securities deal, including a REIT take-private or public company merger, should confirm it is either a registered broker-dealer or that its role and compensation structure fit within an available exemption before assuming an oral or informal fee arrangement will be enforceable. Companies facing an eleventh-hour fee dispute as a deal approaches closing should recognize that courts are generally unwilling to freeze merger proceeds or delay a closing absent a strong showing on the merits, which limits the practical leverage such claims carry even when filed on an emergency basis.
