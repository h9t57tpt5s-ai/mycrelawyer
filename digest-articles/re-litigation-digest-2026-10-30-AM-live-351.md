# After Being Dismissed From a Window-Defect Suit, a Philadelphia Developer Sues the Parent Company Anyway — In Its Own Backyard

### PMC Property Group takes its "alter ego" theory against Apogee Enterprises to federal court in Minnesota, two months after a Pennsylvania judge found the parent too far removed from its subsidiaries' conduct to be sued there

**What happened.** PMC Property Group, Inc., the Philadelphia-based owner of three apartment towers plagued by spontaneously shattering glass, has opened a new front in its multi-year fight over the windows: a federal complaint filed October 27, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the District of Minnesota against Apogee Enterprises, Inc., the publicly traded parent of the window-systems and glass companies PMC blames for the defects. The filing follows a ruling roughly two months earlier in which a federal judge in Philadelphia dismissed Apogee from PMC's existing suit there for lack of personal jurisdiction — finding PMC had not plausibly alleged that Apogee controlled its subsidiaries' day-to-day operations. Rather than drop the parent company from the fight, PMC has now sued Apogee directly in the District of Minnesota, where Apogee is headquartered, asserting three claims: unjust enrichment, and two requests for declaratory judgment seeking to establish that the subsidiaries — Wausau Window and Wall Systems and Viracon — are alter egos of their corporate parent.

**The underlying dispute.** PMC's fight traces back to 2024, when the developer discovered that windows at Franklin Tower, Riverwalk Tower I, and Riverwalk Tower II — three Philadelphia apartment towers for which PMC says it paid roughly $50 million in window-system contracts — had begun breaking spontaneously. PMC alleges the glass, manufactured by Viracon and incorporated into window systems supplied by Wausau, contained nickel sulfide inclusions that caused panels to shatter without warning as they expanded and contracted with temperature swings; more than 60 windows had broken across the three buildings by the time PMC filed its original suit. PMC sued in the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas and, separately, in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, later amending its federal complaint to add Viracon as a defendant and to allege that Wausau, Viracon, and Apogee are, in effect, a single enterprise using the corporate form as a shield.

That theory ran into trouble in federal court. In late August 2026, U.S. District Judge Gerald Pappert granted in part and denied in part the defendants' motion to dismiss, allowing PMC's manufacturing-defect claims against Wausau and Viracon to proceed but dismissing Apogee from the case entirely. The court found that PMC had not plausibly alleged the parent company exercised the kind of day-to-day operational control over its subsidiaries that would support either personal jurisdiction over Apogee in Pennsylvania or liability under an alter-ego theory as pleaded. The ruling also disposed of PMC's unjust enrichment claim against Wausau on a separate ground: because PMC and Wausau had a contract covering the same subject matter, an equitable unjust-enrichment claim could not proceed against a contracting party, even as the same theory survived against Viracon, which had no direct contract with PMC, under Pennsylvania law permitting recovery for benefits indirectly conferred.

**The new filing.** PMC's response was not to abandon the parent-company theory but to relocate it. The Minnesota complaint, filed in the forum where Apogee itself is based, asserts that Apogee directed its subsidiaries' responses to the very defect complaints at the center of the Pennsylvania litigation. As evidence, PMC points to an October 2020 internal email in which a Wausau employee is alleged to have sought Apogee's approval for a warranty credit tied to Viracon's glass, with Apogee approving that credit — evidence PMC says shows the parent reaching into the operational and financial decisions of both subsidiaries, not merely owning them as passive investments. Framed as declaratory-judgment claims rather than damages claims, the Minnesota suit asks the court to establish, as a legal matter, that Wausau and Viracon are alter egos of Apogee, a predicate PMC would need if it hopes to ever collect a judgment from Apogee itself on the underlying defect claims still being litigated in Pennsylvania.

**Why it matters.** The maneuver illustrates a recurring problem for commercial property owners pursuing claims against complex corporate families: a subsidiary with the assets to satisfy a judgment is often thinly capitalized or indemnified upward to its parent, while the parent that controls strategy and cash is the one most insulated from direct suit. Judge Pappert's dismissal order did not say Apogee had no connection to the underlying conduct — it said PMC's pleading, as filed in that forum, did not clear the personal-jurisdiction bar. Filing anew in Apogee's home district removes the personal-jurisdiction defense that proved fatal in Pennsylvania, forcing Apogee to defend the alter-ego theory on its substance rather than on a threshold venue objection. For owners and developers facing a similar structure — a thinly capitalized supplier whose parent approved the terms that caused the harm — the sequence here offers a playbook: plead the parent's specific approval and control decisions with the same documentary specificity a court will demand, and be prepared to pursue the parent in its own backyard if the first forum's jurisdictional reach falls short.

**Practical takeaways.**

- A dismissal for lack of personal jurisdiction is not necessarily the end of a claim against a corporate parent — it may simply require refiling where the parent is unquestionably subject to suit, as PMC has done by moving its alter-ego theory to the District of Minnesota.
- Alter-ego and veil-piercing claims live or die on specific, documented instances of a parent reaching into a subsidiary's operational decisions; PMC's complaint leans on a single internal email showing Apogee approved a warranty credit, underscoring how much weight even one well-chosen document can carry at the pleading stage.
- An unjust-enrichment claim against a contracting party will typically be barred by the existence of the contract itself, while the same theory can survive against a non-contracting affiliate (here, glass manufacturer Viracon) that indirectly benefited from the relationship — a distinction worth building into how claims are structured against multi-entity suppliers from the outset.
- Owners negotiating large supply or installation contracts with a subsidiary of a larger corporate family should document, in real time, any instance of the parent company's direct involvement in warranty, credit, or remediation decisions, since that record becomes the evidentiary backbone of any later alter-ego claim.
