# Data Center Developer Sues Pennsylvania Township Over Rejected $35 Billion King of Prussia Campus

### MLP Ventures says Upper Merion Township reversed its own zoning position under resident pressure; the township says the developer tried to bully it into approval. Montgomery County's Court of Common Pleas will now decide.

Radnor-based developer MLP Ventures has sued Upper Merion Township in Montgomery County Court of Common Pleas, challenging the township Board of Supervisors' unanimous August 13 rejection of a proposed 4.6-million-square-foot data center campus across five King of Prussia-area sites. The lawsuit, filed September 16, 2026, asks the court to overturn the denial, grant preliminary approval for the project, and appoint an independent referee to oversee further review — an unusually aggressive remedy that reflects how far the relationship between developer and township has deteriorated over the course of the dispute. Company filings put the total build-out cost of the proposed campus at roughly $35.16 billion, a figure that underscores both the scale of the capital at stake and why the fight has drawn sustained regional attention.

The core legal dispute turns on a familiar but consequential land-use question: whether a data center qualifies as a "warehouse" use under Upper Merion's existing zoning code. According to MLP Ventures' complaint, the township's own zoning officer issued a determination letter concluding that the proposed use was permitted as-of-right, consistent with the township's stated position in recent years that data centers — which house the servers and networking equipment increasingly in demand to support artificial intelligence workloads — fall within the warehouse-use category already permitted under the code. MLP Ventures alleges that position changed only after a public backlash, including a petition that gathered more than 18,000 signatures, prompted the Planning Commission and then the Board of Supervisors to reverse course and treat the project as requiring discretionary approvals the board could — and did — deny.

The litigation has already generated one significant procedural skirmish. In August, a Montgomery County judge issued an order temporarily pausing township action on the data center applications while the zoning question was litigated. That order was vacated the following day, clearing the way for the Board of Supervisors to proceed with, and ultimately cast, its unanimous vote against the project just hours later. The sequence — an injunction granted and then quickly lifted on the eve of a dispositive municipal vote — illustrates how much can turn on timing in land-use litigation, and how a developer's procedural options can narrow sharply once a legislative body has acted.

For commercial real estate developers and site-selection teams pursuing data center projects nationally, the Upper Merion dispute is a case study in a risk that is becoming more common as AI-driven demand pushes data center proposals into suburban and exurban markets not historically zoned with server farms in mind. A "permitted use" determination from municipal staff — even one committed to writing — is not necessarily the last word once a project draws organized community opposition, and a legislative body's discretionary authority over conditional or special-exception approvals can become a vehicle for revisiting a staff-level interpretation under public pressure. Developers who have relied on an early determination letter as the basis for site acquisition, capital commitments, or public disclosures should treat MLP Ventures' suit as a live test of how much protection that letter actually affords once a governing body decides to reconsider.

The case also highlights the reputational and public-relations dimension that increasingly accompanies large-scale data center litigation. Township officials have publicly characterized MLP Ventures' litigation posture as an attempt to "bully" the township into approval, while the developer's counsel has rejected that characterization and framed the township's reversal as a bad-faith response to political pressure rather than a legitimate reassessment of the zoning question. However the Montgomery County court ultimately rules on the underlying zoning issue, the public back-and-forth is itself a reminder that data center proposals of this scale are increasingly contested in the court of public opinion as much as in the courtroom, with real consequences for how quickly, and on what terms, a project can move forward.

**Practical takeaways:** Developers relying on a municipal zoning officer's "permitted use" determination should document that determination thoroughly and understand that it may not bind a legislative body's later discretionary review, particularly once a project draws organized opposition. Site-selection and entitlement teams pursuing large-scale data center campuses should build community engagement into the project timeline early, rather than treating a favorable staff-level reading of the code as sufficient assurance. Counsel evaluating whether to seek injunctive relief against a pending municipal vote should weigh the risk that a favorable order may be short-lived, as it was here, and plan a litigation strategy that does not depend on the injunction holding through the vote. Finally, owners and lenders evaluating exposure to data center development nationally should watch how Pennsylvania courts resolve the "warehouse equivalence" zoning theory at the center of this case, since it is likely to recur in other jurisdictions facing similar proposals.
