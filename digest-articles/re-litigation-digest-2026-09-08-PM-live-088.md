# Affordable Housing Developer Accuses Florida Town of Extortion in Federal Suit Over Stalled Project

### Alder at Lake Park's complaint against the Town of Lake Park puts a familiar developer grievance — hostile code enforcement — into a far less common legal framing, alleging the town's own attorney tried to force a sale by weaponizing permitting and code violations.

A New York-based developer has taken an unusually aggressive legal stance against a municipality's land-use apparatus, filing a federal complaint in late August 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida accusing the Town of Lake Park, Florida of corruption, blackmail, and extortion in connection with a stalled affordable housing project. The plaintiff, Alder at Lake Park LLC — an entity tied to Daniel Goldstein of New York-based Bofsa Properties — alleges that the town's own attorney, Tom Baird, tried to coerce it into selling its assembled development site to a developer of his choosing, and that when Alder refused, the town retaliated with a wave of code enforcement citations designed to grind the project to a halt. For commercial developers navigating small-municipality permitting processes, particularly for below-market and mixed-income housing projects that depend on local approvals, the case is a notable escalation of a dispute pattern that ordinarily plays out through administrative appeals rather than federal corruption allegations.

The underlying project, known as The Kelsey at Park Avenue, is a mixed-use affordable housing development planned for a seven-parcel assemblage Alder controls at the intersection of Park Avenue and 10th Street in Lake Park, a small Palm Beach County municipality just north of West Palm Beach. According to the complaint, Baird met with Goldstein and Alder's representatives and stated that he personally opposed the project and would "never let Alder build what it wants." The complaint further alleges that Baird told Alder's representatives that "all problems would go away" if the company instead sold the assembled parcels to a developer he had already identified and lined up. When Alder declined to sell, the complaint alleges, Baird directed a town code enforcement officer to issue 34 separate code violations against the properties — a volume of citations Alder characterizes as retaliatory rather than the product of genuine, independently identified code deficiencies. The lawsuit seeks an injunction barring the town from imposing further code enforcement fines, an order compelling Lake Park to act on Alder's pending development applications rather than leave them in administrative limbo, and monetary damages for the harm the delay and citations have allegedly caused the project's economics. Baird has denied the allegations attributed to him and has characterized the lawsuit as retaliation against him and other town officials for performing their official duties.

The case sits at the intersection of two doctrines that commercial developers and their counsel encounter far more often separately than together. The first is the well-worn body of substantive due process and equal protection case law governing municipal land-use decisions, under which developers regularly challenge permit denials, downzonings, and selective code enforcement as arbitrary or discriminatory. The second, considerably rarer in the reported CRE litigation context, is the invocation of extortion and corruption theories against a sitting municipal attorney personally, framing code enforcement not merely as unfair or pretextual but as an instrument of a specific, individually motivated scheme to force a below-market sale to a favored buyer. Proving that kind of claim requires more than showing that citations were numerous or inconvenient; it requires tying specific enforcement actions to the alleged quid pro quo, which is why the complaint leans heavily on the direct quotes attributed to Baird rather than resting on the sheer volume of violations alone. Whether that proof holds up through discovery, and whether Baird enjoys some form of qualified or governmental immunity from personal liability for actions taken in his official capacity, will likely shape the case's trajectory well before any of the underlying zoning issues are reached on the merits.

For developers of affordable and mixed-income housing in particular, the case is a reminder that the political economy of small-municipality approvals can cut in unpredictable directions: the same local discretion that makes affordable-housing incentives and density bonuses possible in a given jurisdiction also concentrates enormous, difficult-to-check power in the hands of a small number of local officials and their counsel. A developer whose project depends on a friendly, or at least neutral, relationship with town staff and its attorney has comparatively few fast, affordable remedies when that relationship turns adversarial short of expensive federal litigation of exactly this kind. Practically, that argues for developers to build a contemporaneous written record of every interaction with local permitting and code enforcement officials from the earliest stages of a project, particularly any communications that touch on alternative buyers, side deals, or informal settlement overtures, since it is precisely that kind of documentation that transforms a garden-variety permitting dispute into a viable corruption or civil-rights claim. It also counsels developers to weigh, before committing significant capital to an assemblage in a small jurisdiction with limited institutional checks on a single official's discretion, whether the project's approval path realistically depends on the goodwill of one or two individuals rather than a more insulated, board-driven process, and to consider requesting a written record of code enforcement's basis for any citation issued during an active land-use dispute, both to build the evidentiary record and to deter enforcement decisions made for reasons unrelated to the code itself.
