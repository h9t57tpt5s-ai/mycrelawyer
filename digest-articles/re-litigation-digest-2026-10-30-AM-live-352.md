# New York Appeals Court Upholds Site Plan for Adaptive-Reuse Development Over Neighbors' Parking Challenge

### The Third Department's decision in Matter of Bruck v. Town of Rochester Planning Board gives developers converting historic industrial buildings to mixed commercial use a clear deference standard — and a usable template for defending a reduced parking count

**What happened.** New York's Appellate Division, Third Department, on October 29, 2026 affirmed a Planning Board's approval of a mixed-use adaptive-reuse project built around a historic granary and seed mill complex in the Hamlet of Accord, Ulster County, rejecting a challenge from neighboring property owners who argued the project's parking plan violated the local zoning code and deviated improperly from the plan the Town Board had originally approved. The ruling in Matter of Bruck v. Town of Rochester Planning Board closes out, at least at this stage, a dispute that pitted two individual petitioners who own property near the site against both the town's Planning Board and the project's developer, Accord, LLC.

**The project and the dispute.** Accord, LLC acquired the historic granary and seed mill property and pursued a rezoning of the parcel into the Town of Rochester's Economic Enterprise Overlay district, a designation intended, under the town code, to "promote flexibility in the development process while integrating a diversity of land uses within close proximity to each other." After an environmental review under the State Environmental Quality Review Act resulted in a negative declaration of significance, the Town Board adopted a local law granting the rezoning and approving Accord's conceptual development plan, which called for converting the existing structures into a restaurant, a bike shop, an artist gallery, a coworking space, worker housing, and an inn. The Town Board found the project required 63 parking spaces under the town's parking code, including 57 on-site spaces (25 of them allocated to the restaurant) and six off-site spaces.

As the project moved into site plan review before the Planning Board, Accord scaled back the proposal: the architect notified the board that the project no longer needed the six off-site parking spaces, that the artist gallery would be dropped, and that the coworking space would shrink by 421 square feet. The Planning Board granted conditional site plan approval in July 2024, finding the modified project in substantial conformance with the Town Board's conceptual plan and adopting the Town Board's prior finding on required on-site parking. Neighboring property owners Susan Bruck and a co-petitioner then sued under CPLR Article 78, arguing the Planning Board had improperly approved a site plan that materially deviated from the plan the Town Board authorized, and that the board had miscalculated the restaurant's parking requirement — contending that, applying the town code's "one parking space per 50 square feet" standard to the restaurant's full listed floor area, 78 spaces were required for the restaurant alone, far more than the 25 the board had approved.

**The ruling.** Supreme Court dismissed the petition, and the Third Department affirmed. The appellate panel held that a local planning board has broad discretion in reviewing site plan applications, and that judicial review is limited to whether the board's action was illegal, arbitrary and capricious, or an abuse of discretion. Applying that standard, the court found the Planning Board rationally determined that the project, as scaled back during site plan review, remained in substantial conformance with the conceptual development plan the Town Board had approved — the modifications eliminated uses and reduced square footage rather than adding scope, and the record supported the board's conclusion that nothing about the changes undercut the basis for the original rezoning. On the parking question, the court credited the developer's engineer, who had applied an alternative "one space per three seats" calculation because the town code did not specify whether restaurant parking should be measured by gross or net square footage, and who calculated the restaurant's actual floor area at 1,250 square feet rather than its larger listed area — yielding the 25-space figure the board had approved. The court found the Planning Board was entitled to adopt the Town Board's own parking determination and that doing so had a rational basis in the record, foreclosing the petitioners' argument that the ambiguity in the code should have been referred to the town's code enforcement officer or zoning board of appeals before any approval issued.

**Why it matters.** Adaptive-reuse projects that convert historic industrial buildings into mixed commercial space are often approved through a two-step process — a legislative rezoning based on a conceptual plan, followed by a more granular site plan review that inevitably refines or scales back the original concept. This decision confirms that New York courts will give planning boards real latitude to find later-stage modifications in "substantial conformance" with an earlier conceptual approval, so long as those modifications trim rather than expand the project's footprint and uses. It is also a useful data point on parking disputes specifically: where a zoning code's parking formula is ambiguous as applied to a particular use — here, whether "floor area" means gross or net square footage for a restaurant — a developer's engineer can supply a reasoned alternative methodology, and a reviewing court will defer to the board's acceptance of that methodology rather than require a formal referral to resolve the ambiguity first. For developers assembling adaptive-reuse projects that combine retail, hospitality, and flexible workspace uses under a similar overlay-district mechanism, the case is a reminder to document, in the record, both the engineering basis for any parking calculation and a clear account of how each project modification reduces rather than expands the scope the legislative body originally approved.

**Practical takeaways.**

- Scaling back a project's scope during site plan review — dropping a use, shrinking square footage, eliminating offsite parking — is far safer ground for a "substantial conformance" finding than any change that adds uses or intensity beyond what the legislative body approved at rezoning.
- When a zoning code's parking formula is ambiguous as applied to a specific use, a developer should put a reasoned, documented engineering methodology into the administrative record early, since a court reviewing under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard will look for a rational basis in that record rather than conduct its own independent calculation.
- Challengers who want a code ambiguity formally resolved by the code enforcement officer or zoning board of appeals should raise that request during the administrative proceeding itself — raising it for the first time on reply, or after the board has already acted, risks being deemed unpreserved.
- Adaptive-reuse projects pursued through a flexible overlay district, like the Economic Enterprise Overlay mechanism at issue here, benefit from a planning board's broad discretion, but that discretion is strongest when the final site plan can be shown to track, not expand, the conceptual plan the legislative body already blessed.
