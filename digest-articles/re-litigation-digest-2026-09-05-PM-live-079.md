# Sacramento Redevelopment's Buried Pesticide Contamination Survives Developer's Bid to Dismiss $9.5M Neighboring-Business Suit

### A Sacramento County Superior Court judge has cleared negligence, fraud, and punitive-damages claims to proceed against the developer and contractors behind Mirasol Village, in a case that tests how far liability for legacy soil contamination can travel to properties outside a redevelopment's own boundaries

When entrepreneur Betty Mitchell's indoor cannabis cultivation company, Liveade Wellness Inc., tested positive for chlordane — a synthetic pesticide banned in the United States since 1988 — she says she had never used, stored, or handled the chemical anywhere on her property. Her lawsuit against the developer and contractors behind the neighboring Mirasol Village redevelopment alleges the contamination migrated from that project's construction site instead, destroying a business she had invested nearly $1 million to build and giving rise to a $9.5 million claim that a Sacramento County Superior Court judge has now allowed to proceed on negligence, fraud, and punitive-damages theories.

Mirasol Village is the master-planned redevelopment of the former Twin Rivers public housing project in Sacramento's River District, led by national affordable-housing developer McCormack Baron Salazar, Inc. in partnership with general contractor A. Teichert & Son, Inc. and site contractor Midstate Construction Corporation. The site's contamination history predates the current litigation by years: a March 4, 2020 letter from the California Department of Toxic Substances Control identified organochlorine pesticides, including chlordane, as chemicals of concern at the property and called for additional environmental investigation before redevelopment proceeded. Mitchell's complaint, filed in April 2025, alleges that construction dust generated during Mirasol Village's build-out carried that legacy contamination beyond the project's boundaries and into her nearby facility, where it settled into her cultivation operation and rendered her product unsellable.

The claims that survived the defendants' effort to dismiss the case are significant less for their size than for their theory. A straightforward negligence claim — that a developer and its contractors failed to adequately contain dust and contaminated soil during construction — is a familiar one in environmental liability litigation. What makes this case notable is that Judge Richard K. Sueyoshi's rulings also let Mitchell's fraud and punitive-damages claims move forward, meaning the court found she had pled facts sufficient to support an inference that the defendants' conduct went beyond ordinary carelessness. Punitive damages claims against a redevelopment's construction team require a plaintiff to show something closer to conscious disregard of a known risk, which is a considerably higher bar than proving a contractor failed to meet an ordinary standard of care, and a court's willingness to let such a claim proceed past the pleading stage is itself a signal about how the underlying facts are being read.

The case sits at the intersection of two problems that are becoming more common as cities redevelop aging public housing, industrial, and agricultural sites: legacy contamination that predates current ownership by decades, and the reality that dust and soil disturbance from active construction do not respect parcel lines. Developers undertaking brownfield or formerly-contaminated redevelopments typically manage their own on-site environmental liability through a combination of Phase I/II environmental assessments, regulatory oversight agreements, and contractual risk allocation with their construction team. What is harder to manage — and what this case puts squarely at issue — is liability to a neighboring, unrelated business that had no role in the original contamination and no contractual relationship with the developer at all, but that alleges direct economic harm from the redevelopment's construction activity.

For commercial property owners, developers, and construction managers working near known or suspected legacy-contamination sites, the case is also a reminder that regulatory notice can cut against a defendant later in civil litigation. Because DTSC had already flagged organochlorine pesticides as a chemical of concern at the site years before construction began, Mitchell's complaint is positioned to argue the defendants had specific, documented notice of the contamination risk rather than encountering it as a surprise — a fact pattern that tends to make negligence claims stronger and can support the kind of knowing-disregard theory punitive damages claims require.

**Practical takeaways.** Developers and contractors working on any site with documented legacy soil or groundwater contamination — brownfields, former industrial parcels, older public housing sites, or agricultural land with historical pesticide use — should treat regulatory notices identifying chemicals of concern as creating a heightened duty to contain dust, runoff, and disturbed soil during construction, not simply a disclosure obligation to future occupants of the redeveloped site itself. Construction dust-control and site-containment plans should be evaluated with an eye toward off-site migration risk to neighboring businesses and residents, not solely compliance with air-quality permits, since a plan that satisfies a regulator can still leave a developer exposed to a neighboring property owner's common-law claims. Owners and general contractors on contaminated or formerly-contaminated sites should also confirm that commercial general liability and pollution-liability coverage extends to third-party, off-site claims from neighboring businesses, since this is precisely the gap where a punitive-damages theory — as opposed to a garden-variety negligence claim — can create exposure a standard policy was never underwritten to absorb.
