# Baltimore Residents Sue to Block MCB's $900 Million Harborplace Overhaul

### A citizen challenge to the City Charter and zoning changes underlying the Inner Harbor's largest redevelopment in decades tests how far a municipality can go to clear the way for a private developer's vision.

Seven Baltimore residents and business owners filed suit on September 21, 2026 against the City of Baltimore, the City Council, the Board of Estimates, and developer MCB HP Baltimore LLC, seeking to unwind the legislative package that cleared the way for MCB Real Estate's roughly $900 million plan to demolish Harborplace's waterfront pavilions and replace them with two residential towers, office and retail buildings, and reconfigured public space along the Inner Harbor. The suit, filed in the Circuit Court for Baltimore City, does not principally argue that the redevelopment itself is a bad idea; it argues that the City got there by an unlawful process, and it asks the court to void the approvals as a result. Within two days, MCB fired back publicly, framing the plaintiffs as a small group trying to block "long overdue" revitalization of a landmark that has struggled commercially for years.

For commercial developers and municipalities alike, the case is a pointed reminder that a high-profile, politically popular redevelopment project is only as durable as the legislative process that approved it — and that opponents increasingly look for defects in that process rather than relitigating the underlying policy judgment a legislature is generally entitled to make.

## What the City Approved, and What the Suit Targets

The dispute traces back to March 2024, when the Baltimore City Council passed, and Mayor Brandon Scott signed, three interlocking pieces of legislation clearing MCB's plan for the Inner Harbor site. One amended the City Charter to permit multifamily housing and structured parking on a stretch of the harbor that had previously been dedicated as public parkland — a designation with real legal weight, since public trust doctrine and dedicated-parkland restrictions can constrain what a city may permit private development to do on such land even when the city itself is the one authorizing it. A second measure rewrote applicable zoning, removing a 100-foot height limit that had constrained the site and authorizing the mixed-use program MCB now proposes: two residential towers of 32 and 25 stories totaling roughly 900 units, two 200,000-square-foot commercial and retail buildings, and an additional freestanding 8,500-square-foot retail building, replacing the low-rise pavilions that have anchored the Inner Harbor since the early 1980s.

The plaintiffs' complaint does not ask the court to weigh whether that program is good planning. Instead, it alleges that the City's approval process failed to comply with procedural and substantive requirements attached to the Charter amendment and the zoning changes — the kind of claim that, if successful, does not require a court to second-guess the City Council's judgment about land use at all. It simply requires enforcement of the rules the City itself was bound to follow in reaching that judgment. That framing matters enormously for how the case is likely to proceed: Maryland courts generally extend substantial deference to a municipality's substantive zoning and land-use decisions, but that deference has real limits when a plaintiff can show the decision-making process itself violated a charter or statutory requirement, particularly one — like a change to the classification of dedicated public parkland — that exists specifically to constrain what a city can too easily give away.

## A Familiar Playbook, Applied to a Marquee Waterfront Site

The Harborplace fight fits a pattern increasingly familiar in large urban redevelopment disputes nationally: opponents who cannot stop a project through ordinary public comment or the political process instead look to the legislative and procedural mechanics that enabled it, seeking a technical or structural defect that a court can act on without having to relitigate the underlying planning merits. That approach has produced mixed results elsewhere, and its success here will likely turn on granular questions of Maryland municipal and charter law — whether the 2024 legislation was adopted with the specific findings, hearings, or supermajorities a Charter amendment of this kind requires, and whether reclassifying dedicated parkland for private multifamily and commercial use exceeded what the City Council could accomplish through ordinary legislation at all.

MCB's public response — accusing opponents of prioritizing "nostalgia" over "progress for all" — signals the developer intends to litigate this as a referendum on revitalization rather than engage narrowly with the plaintiffs' procedural claims, at least in the court of public opinion. That posture is understandable given the political stakes of a project this visible, but it is also a reminder that a strong policy case for a project provides no legal defense if the underlying approval process turns out to have been legally defective. The case remains at an early stage, with no hearing date yet reported on dispositive motions.

## Practical Takeaways

- A politically popular, well-financed redevelopment remains vulnerable to a procedural challenge targeting the legislative mechanics of its own approval — developers should not treat community or council support as a substitute for a scrupulously documented approval record.
- Reclassifying dedicated public parkland or waterfront trust land for private development carries elevated legal risk distinct from ordinary rezoning, and merits its own dedicated legal review before a project's economics are locked in.
- Municipalities structuring a Charter amendment or zoning package to enable a specific, named project should build a hearing and findings record that can withstand a procedural challenge on its own terms, independent of the project's popularity.
- Developers financing or acquiring an interest in a project built on a recent legislative or charter change should diligence the durability of that enabling legislation, not just the zoning entitlement it produced, before treating land-use risk as resolved.
