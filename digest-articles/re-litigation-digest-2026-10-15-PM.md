# Pocono Warehouse Developer's Stormwater-Ordinance Challenge Dies on Ripeness in Pennsylvania Commonwealth Court

### A Monroe County landowner sued over tightened wetland buffer rules while its own waiver request sat unanswered — and the appellate court says that was the problem

A Pennsylvania appellate panel has affirmed the dismissal of a commercial developer's constitutional challenge to a township's stormwater ordinance, holding that the lawsuit was filed before the developer had exhausted the very administrative process that might have mooted its claims. The Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania's October 15, 2026 memorandum opinion in *Orchard BJK Company, LLC and Pocono Mountains Industries, Inc. v. Coolbaugh Township Board of Supervisors* is a cautionary tale for any commercial or industrial landowner tempted to go to court over a zoning or environmental ordinance while a pending application before the same local board could still resolve the dispute administratively.

Orchard BJK Company and Pocono Mountains Industries own a cluster of commercially and industrially zoned parcels in Coolbaugh Township, a Monroe County municipality in Pennsylvania's Pocono Mountains region. In April 2023, the Township's Board of Supervisors approved the companies' application to subdivide one tract into two parcels — a 35.5-acre Lot 1 and a 73.16-acre Lot 2 — and conditionally approved a land development plan for Lot 1 that called for an approximately 333,000-square-foot warehouse, built to the stormwater and subdivision ordinances then on the books.

Months later, the Board moved to tighten those rules. On August 2, 2023, it amended the Township's Stormwater Management and Earth Disturbance Ordinance to triple the minimum buffer between development and wetlands — from 50 to 150 feet for the inner buffer, and from 100 to 150 feet for the buffer between buildings and wetlands. Critically, the amendment left intact a waiver provision allowing the Board, on a project-by-project basis, to modify or excuse the buffer requirements entirely.

In April 2024, the developers filed two things at once with the Township: a preliminary land development plan for an approximately 186,000-square-foot warehouse on Lot 2, and a petition asking the Board to waive the new buffer standards and apply the pre-amendment rules instead. Then, while both the plan and the waiver petition were still pending before the Board, the developers sued in the Monroe County Court of Common Pleas. Their complaint alleged that the 2023 buffer amendments violated their constitutional right to enjoy their property and amounted to invalid special legislation, and asked the court to declare the amendments unconstitutional and void from the outset.

The Township fought back with preliminary objections, arguing — among other things — that the case wasn't ripe, because no real controversy would exist unless and until the Board actually denied the pending waiver request. The Common Pleas court agreed, construed the Township's various objections as raising ripeness, and dismissed the complaint in June 2025 without reaching the merits.

On appeal, the developers argued first that the trial court had improperly raised ripeness on its own, and second that their claims were ripe regardless. The Commonwealth Court rejected both arguments. On the first, the panel pointed to the Township's own preliminary objections, which expressly argued — paragraph by paragraph — that "[t]here is no actual case or controversy" until the Board ruled on the waiver request; the developers' claim that the trial court invented the ripeness issue sua sponte was, in the court's words, "plainly incorrect."

On the merits of ripeness, the court applied Pennsylvania's traditionally lenient standard for declaratory judgment actions, under which a claim need only present "the ripening seeds of a controversy." Even under that forgiving test, the panel held the suit was premature. The developers claimed the buffer amendments effectively blocked warehouse construction on Lot 2 and had depressed the value of their other holdings — but those harms depend entirely on what the Board eventually does with the still-pending waiver request. If the Board grants the waiver, the developers get the relief they wanted through the administrative process, and the constitutional claims may never need to be decided at all. A declaratory judgment, the court reiterated, "must not be employed to determine rights in anticipation of events which may never occur."

For commercial and industrial landowners anywhere in the country facing a newly tightened zoning, stormwater, or land-use ordinance, the practical lesson here cuts against the instinct to rush to court the moment an unfavorable rule is enacted. Where the ordinance itself preserves a waiver or variance mechanism, and an application under that mechanism is still pending before the same body being sued, a court is likely to find any parallel constitutional challenge premature — effectively forcing the landowner to let the administrative process play out, or to affirmatively abandon the waiver request, before a court will even consider the underlying legal theory. Developers should also take note that a local government's preliminary objections asserting "no actual case or controversy," phrased in the specific, prescribed terms of a ripeness defense, will preserve that defense even if a trial court's opinion frames it loosely as an insufficient-pleading or prior-pending-action issue. And because this was a non-precedential memorandum opinion, it carries no binding weight in future Pennsylvania cases, but it still signals how Commonwealth Court panels are likely to treat similarly timed challenges to local stormwater and buffer ordinances statewide.
