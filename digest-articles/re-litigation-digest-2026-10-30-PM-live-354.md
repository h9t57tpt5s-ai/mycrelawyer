# MAA and JBG Smith Pay $9.3 Million to Settle D.C.'s RealPage Rent-Pricing Antitrust Case

### The District of Columbia's Attorney General secured consent judgments against two of its largest landlords, adding to a growing list of settlements in the algorithmic rent-pricing litigation sweeping the multifamily industry.

The District of Columbia Office of the Attorney General announced on September 14, 2026 that it had reached consent judgments with JBG Smith (through its JBG Associates, L.L.C. entity) and Mid-America Apartment Communities, Inc. (MAA), resolving the District's claims that both landlords used RealPage's revenue-management software to coordinate rents in violation of antitrust law. JBG Smith agreed to pay $8.1 million and MAA agreed to pay $1.2 million, for a combined $9.3 million, in a case captioned *District of Columbia v. RealPage, Inc., et al.*, No. 2023-CAB-006762, pending in the Superior Court of the District of Columbia.

### The underlying antitrust theory

The District's suit is one node of a much larger legal reckoning facing RealPage and the landlords that used its pricing products. Since 2022 and 2023, the Department of Justice, dozens of state attorneys general, and a sprawling multidistrict class action in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Tennessee have all pursued claims that RealPage's revenue-management software functioned as a vehicle for horizontal price coordination: landlords fed RealPage non-public, competitively sensitive data — asking rents, lease terms, occupancy — which RealPage's algorithm then used to recommend pricing not just to that landlord, but informed by the pooled data of its competitors. Regulators have characterized this as a "hub-and-spoke" conspiracy, with RealPage as the hub and participating landlords as the spokes, each agreeing (the theory goes) to use the software and abide by its pricing guidance in exchange for the assurance that competitors were doing the same.

The District's complaint named RealPage along with a roster of landlords operating in the District, including JBG Smith and MAA. According to the consent judgments, JBG Smith used RealPage's revenue-management software to help set rents across multiple District properties, while MAA's use was tied to a single District property. Neither company admitted liability or wrongdoing as part of the settlements, which is standard in these resolutions, and both agreed to prospective restrictions on their future use of shared-data pricing software — commitments running eight years in MAA's case — along with the possibility of an independent compliance monitor, at the settling company's expense, if the OAG later has reason to believe the terms are not being followed.

### A rapidly growing settlement ledger

The District's deals with JBG Smith and MAA followed earlier District settlements with other large operators, including a $1.4 million resolution with Avenue5 Residential and Bell Partners reached in June 2026, and track a parallel wave of settlements Kentucky's Attorney General has reached in its own, separately filed RealPage suit, including with Greystar Management Services in July 2026. In the Tennessee MDL, dozens of landlord defendants — including Equity Residential's $56 million settlement and Blackstone-backed LivCor's $7 million settlement, both previously reported here — have resolved their exposure, with the presiding court approving roughly $360 million in aggregate settlements as of mid-October 2026. RealPage and a shrinking number of holdout landlord defendants continue to litigate, while several state attorneys general, including Kentucky's, have separately appealed a district court order limiting their ability to seek damages on residents' behalf alongside the private class settlements.

MAA disclosed the $1.2 million payment and the accompanying consent judgment in its Form 10-Q for the quarter ended September 30, 2026, noting the amount was accrued during the third quarter and that the company continues to defend a separate, still-pending suit brought by Kentucky's Attorney General against RealPage and a number of the state's largest landlords, including MAA itself.

### Practical takeaways

For multifamily owners and managers still facing RealPage-related exposure, the District of Columbia settlements confirm that enforcement authorities are treating even a single local property's use of shared-data pricing software as sufficient grounds for antitrust liability — scale of use affects the settlement amount, not whether a claim exists at all. Landlords should expect that any company sued in one of the many parallel actions (the DOJ case, the Tennessee MDL, or an individual state's suit) will likely face a consent-judgment path that combines a cash payment calibrated to the extent of their software use with forward-looking commitments on data-sharing and algorithmic pricing practices, often with monitoring provisions attached. Given the prospective, multi-year compliance commitments baked into these settlements — MAA's runs eight years — in-house teams should treat a RealPage-related consent judgment as an ongoing compliance obligation, not a one-time payment, and build monitoring of revenue-management software practices into their regular legal and operational review going forward.
