# Eighth Circuit Hands Multifamily Owners a Hard Lesson on Challenging Municipal Code-Enforcement Closures

### A federal appeals court's affirmance in *AB Realty, LLC v. Scott Lane* shows how little constitutional traction an apartment owner gets after a city shuts the building down — even when the inspector's warrant application contained errors.

When a city inspector shows up with an administrative search warrant and the power to close a building, commercial property owners often assume the Constitution gives them a real check on that authority. A November 6, 2026 decision from the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit is a reminder of how narrow that check actually is. In *AB Realty, LLC v. Scott Lane*, the court affirmed dismissal of a multifamily owner's Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendment claims arising from the City of Omaha's 2018 closure of the Yale Park Apartment Complex — even though the owner showed the inspector's warrant application contained demonstrably false statements.

**What happened.** Yale Park, an 84-unit apartment complex in Omaha, Nebraska, was owned by AB Realty, LLC. In 2018, tenant advocates began documenting code violations at the property and relaying their findings to the city, which began planning the building's closure. Omaha's Chief Housing Inspector, Scott Lane, applied for and obtained an administrative inspection warrant, citing suspected health and safety violations in roughly 84 units. According to the Eighth Circuit's own recitation of the record, the warrant application contained several factual errors, including a false claim that an AB Realty principal had denied the inspector's request to enter the building, a false claim that police had been called to the complex, and a false claim that a gas explosion had occurred there.

The city executed the warrant in September 2018. Police and housing inspectors searched the property, tenants were forced to relocate, and the city mailed AB Realty notices citing roughly 2,000 total code violations — carbon dioxide leaks, holes in the roof, non-functioning heat, exposed wiring, faulty plumbing, and moldy circuit breakers among them — and ordered that Yale Park could not reopen until they were cured. The city separately prosecuted Kay Anderson, an AB Realty principal, for misdemeanor code violations; a jury convicted him on a few counts while acquitting him of most. AB Realty completed repairs and reopened the building in 2021, then sued the city, its inspectors, and two tenant advocates under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, pressing Fourth Amendment, due process, and takings theories. The district court dismissed the claims, and AB Realty appealed.

**Why the claims failed.** The Eighth Circuit affirmed across the board, and its reasoning illustrates just how much deference administrative code-enforcement actions receive compared to ordinary criminal searches. On the Fourth Amendment claim, the court applied the *Franks v. Delaware* framework, which requires a plaintiff to show both that the warrant affidavit contained deliberately or recklessly false statements and that the affidavit's remaining, truthful content was insufficient to support the warrant. Even accepting that Inspector Lane's affidavit contained real falsehoods, the court held AB Realty had not shown the remainder of the affidavit failed to meet the lower bar that governs administrative inspection warrants — a standard built on "reasonable legislative or administrative standards," not the traditional probable-cause showing required for a criminal search.

The due process and takings claims fared no better, largely because of how they were pleaded. The court found AB Realty's complaint blended the interests of three separate plaintiffs — the LLC and two individuals — without identifying which constitutionally protected interest was at stake or whose process was actually denied, leaving the panel unable to evaluate what procedural safeguards were even required. On the takings claim, AB Realty asserted only that the city's actions constituted "an effective and inverse taking," without specifying whether it meant a per se taking or a regulatory one — a distinction the court found dispositive on its own, since the complaint supported neither theory as pleaded.

**Why it matters for commercial owners.** The ruling underscores a structural reality that multifamily and other commercial property owners facing aggressive code enforcement should internalize: administrative inspection warrants operate under a markedly lower threshold than warrants in the criminal context, so even a warrant application containing real inaccuracies may still survive a Fourth Amendment challenge if the untainted portions clear that lower bar. It also shows that takings and due process theories arising from a code-enforcement closure require real pleading discipline — a generic claim that a closure was an "inverse taking" or a due-process violation, without identifying the specific protected interest and the type of taking alleged, is unlikely to survive a motion to dismiss, regardless of how disruptive the closure itself was.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Administrative inspection warrants are evaluated under a lower, more deferential standard than ordinary criminal search warrants, so owners challenging one should expect a *Franks* claim to require showing the affidavit's remaining content — after removing any false statements — still fails even that lower bar.
- A takings claim arising from a code-enforcement closure should specify, from the outset, whether it alleges a per se taking or a regulatory taking; pleading "inverse condemnation" generically, without that distinction, invites dismissal.
- Where multiple plaintiffs with different legal interests (an entity and its individual principals) bring a joint civil-rights suit over the same closure, each plaintiff's distinct protected interest should be pleaded separately rather than asserted collectively.
- A parallel criminal prosecution of a property's principal over the same code violations can complicate — and delay — a civil challenge to the underlying closure, as it did here when the district court stayed the case pending the criminal proceeding's outcome.
