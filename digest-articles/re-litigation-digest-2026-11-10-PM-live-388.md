# Virginia Appeals Court: City Can't Apply New Tax-Penalty Ordinance Retroactively to Delinquent Commercial Land

**Richmond, Virginia — November 10, 2026.** The Court of Appeals of Virginia ruled today in *The City of Colonial Heights, Virginia v. Alpene Land, LLC, et al.* that while Virginia's default statutory interest and penalty rates allow a locality to collect on delinquent real estate taxes even when its own local ordinance is defective, a city cannot cure that defect by passing a new penalty ordinance mid-litigation and applying it retroactively to taxes that were already owed.

The City of Colonial Heights brought three judicial tax-sale actions under Virginia Code Section 58.1-3965 against commercial landowners Alpene Land, LLC and Barker Investment Corporation, seeking the sale of several parcels over more than $65,000 in real estate taxes delinquent across nine tax years, 2016 through 2024. After the owners sought to redeem the properties by paying the amount they argued was actually due, a dispute arose over whether the City's existing penalty-and-interest ordinance, City Code Section 258-13, was valid; the circuit court held the ordinance "enacts nothing" and that the City therefore had no authority to collect penalties or interest at all. While that ruling was pending final order, the City passed Ordinance No. 24-13, setting a flat 10% penalty and 10% annual interest rate and declaring the new rate retroactive to any real estate taxes due on or after May 15, 1992. The circuit court held the new ordinance could not be applied retroactively, reasoning under Virginia's Dillon Rule that the General Assembly had not delegated the City authority to impose penalties and interest on already-delinquent taxes after the fact.

The Court of Appeals reversed in part and affirmed in part. It held the circuit court was wrong to conclude the City could collect no interest or penalties at all: even with the local ordinance defective, Virginia Code Sections 58.1-3915 and 58.1-3918 set statewide default penalty and interest rates that apply to delinquent real estate taxes regardless of whether a locality's own ordinance validly sets its own rate. But the court agreed with the circuit court that the City's August 2024 ordinance could not be applied retroactively to taxes already delinquent before its passage, affirming that portion of the ruling.

**Why it matters for commercial owners:** The decision functions as backstop protection for localities — a defective local tax ordinance does not mean delinquent commercial property escapes interest and penalties altogether, since state default rates fill the gap. But it is also a clear limit on how far a city can reach backward once litigation exposes a drafting problem in its own ordinance: owners litigating a tax-sale or redemption dispute over a technical defect in a local penalty ordinance should expect the state's own default rate to apply rather than no penalty at all, while any newly enacted local ordinance passed in response to such a ruling cannot retroactively increase what is owed on already-delinquent years.
