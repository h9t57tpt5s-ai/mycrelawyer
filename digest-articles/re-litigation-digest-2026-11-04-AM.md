# Off-Duty Officer's Mistaken "Vacant Unit" Raid Revives Premises-Liability and Punitive-Damages Claims Against Apartment Owner

### Georgia Court of Appeals splits a trio of companion rulings in SMRE Roswell SPE, LLC v. Melgar, reinstating claims the trial court had thrown out while leaving a false-imprisonment claim alive for trial

The Georgia Court of Appeals has partially revived a multifamily owner's premises-liability and punitive-damages exposure arising from a security program that relied on its own property manager's bad information. In a trio of companion opinions issued November 3, 2026 — *SMRE Roswell SPE, LLC v. Melgar* and its cross-appeals, Case Nos. A26A1353, A26A1354 and A26A1355 — the court reversed summary judgment the trial court had granted to the owner of the Roswell Creek Apartments on two residents' premises-liability and punitive-damages claims, while affirming dismissal of their battery, trespass and contract-based negligence claims. The court also affirmed the trial court's separate refusal to dismiss a false-imprisonment claim against the ownership, meaning that claim proceeds to trial regardless of the outcome on the other counts.

The case grew out of a June 22, 2022 incident built on a staffing arrangement increasingly common at multifamily properties: hiring an off-duty municipal police officer as part-time complex security. According to the court's opinion, the Roswell Creek property manager arranged for a City of Roswell police detective to work up to 30 hours a month patrolling the complex, including periodically checking units the manager identified as vacant to make sure no unauthorized occupants had moved in. On the night in question, the officer — then on duty in his separate capacity as a city police officer — responded to reports of burglaries of unoccupied units at the complex. Cross-referencing the vacancy list the property manager had supplied him, he identified a unit as vacant and called for police backup. Officers approached with weapons drawn, entered the unit, and handcuffed and detained one resident; when a second resident arrived home shortly after, officers detained him as well. The unit was not actually vacant — it was a rented, occupied apartment, and at least one of the two detained residents did not speak English. The vacancy list the officer relied on was outdated.

The residents sued SMRE Roswell SPE, LLC, the complex's owner, asserting claims for assault and battery, trespass, premises liability, breach-of-contract-based negligence, false imprisonment, and punitive damages. The trial court granted summary judgment to the ownership on most claims but let the false-imprisonment claim proceed. On cross-appeal, the Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment on the battery, trespass and breach-of-contract-based negligence claims as to all plaintiffs, and affirmed summary judgment on the assault claim as to two of the three plaintiffs. But it reversed summary judgment on the premises-liability claim as to two plaintiffs, finding a genuine factual dispute over whether the ownership's own information — not merely the responding officers' conduct — was a cause of the wrongful entry and detention. Because the premises-liability claims survive as to those plaintiffs, the court also reversed summary judgment on their associated punitive-damages and attorney-fees claims, while affirming dismissal of those derivative claims for the third plaintiff whose underlying claims did not survive.

The decision matters to multifamily owners well beyond Georgia because off-duty-officer security arrangements are a widespread, low-cost way to put a uniformed, armed presence on a property, particularly at complexes that have had break-ins or squatting problems. The court's reasoning draws a line that owners should take seriously: an officer's status as sworn law enforcement does not insulate the property owner from premises-liability exposure when the officer is acting, in substance, on information and instructions the property manager supplied for a security function the owner created and controls. The ruling also confirms that Georgia courts will let a punitive-damages claim proceed to a jury once the underlying liability theory survives summary judgment, rather than requiring a separate showing of egregiousness at the dispositive-motion stage.

For owners and managers who run similar programs, the opinion is also a data-hygiene case. The incident traces to a single stale occupancy record — a list of "vacant" units handed to a part-time security contact without clear protocols for updating it as leases turned over. That gap, not any defect in the officer's law-enforcement conduct, is what kept the premises-liability and punitive-damages claims alive on appeal.

**Practical takeaways:** Multifamily owners that staff off-duty police officers or other armed third-party security should treat occupancy and vacancy records fed to those officers as a core liability-control document, with a defined update cadence and a sign-off process before any unit is flagged as vacant for security-sweep purposes; owners should clarify in writing whether an off-duty officer performing complex security functions is acting as the property's agent for liability purposes, since that status — not the officer's separate law-enforcement authority — is likely to drive a court's premises-liability analysis; and because Georgia's appellate courts will let punitive-damages and attorney-fee claims ride alongside a revived premises-liability theory, owners facing a forced-entry or wrongful-detention incident at a residential or mixed-use property should assume insurance and litigation exposure beyond simple negligence damages from the outset of any claim.
