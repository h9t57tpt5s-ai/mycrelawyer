# Cold-Storage REIT Sues Solar Contractor for Over $1 Billion After Warehouse Fire

### Lineage, Inc. says Altus Power re-energized rooftop solar panels against its explicit instructions, sparking a blaze that destroyed a 500,000-square-foot cold-storage facility in Los Angeles — a dispute with implications for every commercial landlord hosting third-party solar on its roof.

Lineage, Inc. (NASDAQ: LINE), the publicly traded cold-storage and logistics real estate company, disclosed in its third-quarter 10-Q filed November 9, 2026, that it has sued Altus Power, Inc., its subsidiary Los Palos Street Operating LLC, and contractor Pearce Services, LLC in Los Angeles County Superior Court over a June 17, 2026 fire that destroyed a roughly 500,000-square-foot cold-storage warehouse on South Los Palos Street in the Boyle Heights neighborhood of Los Angeles. News coverage of the suit, filed in September 2026, reports that Lineage is seeking more than $1 billion in damages, making it one of the largest property-casualty disputes to hit the commercial real estate and solar industries this year.

The underlying fire burned for roughly a week, prompted a state of emergency declaration from Governor Gavin Newsom and a local emergency declaration from Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass, and at one point threatened a compromised ammonia refrigerant line at the facility — a serious hazard given ammonia's use as a cooling agent in industrial cold storage. The Los Angeles Fire Department's official cause finding remains "undetermined," but investigators reportedly traced the blaze's origin to the vicinity of rooftop solar panels installed on the building.

According to reporting on the complaint, Lineage alleges that Altus Power and its installation contractor, Pearce Services, prematurely re-energized the rooftop solar array after maintenance work, despite Lineage's explicit request that they wait for safety checks to be completed first. The suit asserts claims including negligence, negligent supervision, and health and safety code violations, along with breach of contract. Lineage's own 10-Q frames the stakes in blunt terms: the company says it is unable to estimate total cleanup and restoration costs but that it is "reasonably possible" those costs will exceed its insurance claim limit, and it is separately fielding customer claims for inventory losses and cooperating with federal, state, and local investigators. The defendants have indicated they intend to contest the claims, and Los Palos Street Operating LLC has reportedly countered that Lineage, as the facility's tenant, bore responsibility for post-fire cleanup and debris removal under its lease.

The dispute sits at the intersection of two trends reshaping commercial real estate risk: the rapid buildout of rooftop and on-site solar on industrial and warehouse properties, often installed and operated by third-party energy companies rather than the property owner or tenant itself, and the outsized property and business-interruption exposure that a single casualty event can create at a large-footprint logistics facility. Cold-storage warehouses carry particular risk profiles — ammonia refrigerant systems, dense inventory concentrations, and specialized rebuild costs — that can turn an equipment failure into a nine-figure or larger loss in a matter of hours.

For commercial landlords, tenants, and REITs that have signed, or are considering, rooftop solar agreements with third-party energy developers, the case is an early test of how courts will allocate liability when a vendor's equipment or maintenance work is blamed for destroying the host building. The complaint's theory — that the vendor ignored an explicit instruction to hold off on re-energizing equipment pending a safety check — if proven, could sharpen the standard of care expected of solar installers and operators working on occupied commercial buildings, particularly those with heightened fire risks like cold-storage and other industrial uses. It also highlights a recurring friction point in solar-hosting arrangements: who bears responsibility, contractually and in tort, when the energy company's equipment becomes the alleged cause of a catastrophic loss to the host property.

The fight over this particular property is not limited to Lineage's suit against its vendors. Separately, hundreds of nearby residents have sued Lineage directly over the fire's effects on the surrounding community, underscoring that a single industrial casualty event can generate parallel litigation fronts — vendor-liability claims from the property operator, and nuisance or personal-injury claims from neighbors — that commercial owners and their insurers need to manage simultaneously.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Commercial landlords and tenants that host third-party rooftop solar should ensure their contracts with the solar developer and its maintenance contractors spell out, in writing, who controls the timing of re-energization and safety checks after service work — and preserve that correspondence, since it may become the central liability evidence in a later dispute.
- A single equipment failure at a large industrial or cold-storage facility can generate overlapping liability exposure: first-party property and business-interruption losses, third-party claims from neighbors and customers, and now, as this case shows, affirmative claims against the vendors whose work is blamed for the loss.
- Owners and operators of ammonia-refrigerated cold-storage facilities should confirm that fire-response and emergency-declaration protocols with local authorities account for the added hazard a compromised refrigerant line can pose during a prolonged fire event.
- Lease and vendor agreements governing rooftop energy installations should address post-casualty responsibility for cleanup and debris removal explicitly, rather than leaving it to be litigated after a loss, as the landlord-tenant dispute in this case illustrates.
