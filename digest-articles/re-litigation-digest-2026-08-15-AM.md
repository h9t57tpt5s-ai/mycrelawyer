# Non-Traded REIT Sponsors Face Fresh Scrutiny After New Jersey Judge Revives Proxy Disclosure Suit

### A federal court's ruling that Lightstone REIT shareholders may proceed with claims over a 2022 liquidity-delay vote turns on what a sponsor's proxy statement didn't say — and offers a cautionary template for any non-traded REIT weighing a charter amendment tied to its own economics.

Shareholders of three affiliated non-traded real estate investment trusts scored a significant win on August 7, 2026, when Judge Michael A. Shipp of the U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey denied a motion to dismiss their amended class complaint in *Ayer v. Lightstone Value Plus REIT I, Inc.* The ruling allows investors in Lightstone Value Plus REIT I, II, and III — all sponsored and externally advised by affiliates of David Lichtenstein's The Lightstone Group — to proceed with claims that 2022 proxy statements omitted a material conflict of interest ahead of a shareholder vote that eliminated deadlines requiring the funds to pursue a stock-exchange listing or liquidate. For an industry segment that raises billions from retail investors through structures explicitly premised on an eventual liquidity event, a federal court allowing a class of REIT shareholders to contest the very mechanism that delayed that event is a development sponsors, advisers, and independent directors across the space should be tracking closely.

The case traces back to charter amendments Lightstone pursued in fall 2022, which shareholders of the three REITs approved in December 2022 and January 2023. The amendments removed durational provisions that had required each fund to seek a stock exchange listing — or otherwise wind down — by its eighth or tenth anniversary, deadlines that would have fallen in 2024 and 2025. According to the complaint, Lightstone's solicitation campaign told shareholders a "yes" vote was "the most likely path to liquidity," when plaintiffs allege the amendments accomplished the opposite: removing the trigger that would have forced a listing or liquidation and leaving the REITs' duration open-ended.

What makes the ruling notable is not the liquidity delay itself — extending a non-traded REIT's life is a common, disclosed feature of many offerings — but what the proxy statements allegedly left out about the sponsor's own stake in the outcome. Plaintiffs allege Lichtenstein, through several special-purpose entities, held subordinated participation interests in the REITs potentially worth more than $59.8 million, and that those interests would have been rendered worthless had REIT II and REIT III liquidated on their original schedules in September 2024 and March 2025, respectively. The 2022 proxy materials disclosed that Lichtenstein held these interests, but plaintiffs allege they did not disclose that the interests had no value unless the REITs' duration was extended — a detail the court found could have significantly changed what a reasonable shareholder understood about what was actually being asked of them.

That distinction was decisive to the motion-to-dismiss ruling. Defendants had argued the shareholder vote itself ratified the charter amendments, a defense that typically forecloses a later claim that the same transaction breached fiduciary duties. Judge Shipp's opinion rejected that argument at the pleading stage, reasoning that a ratification defense depends on the underlying vote having been "fully informed" — and that the alleged omission of Lichtenstein's direct financial stake in extending the REITs' duration plausibly meant it was not. The ruling followed an earlier, narrower dismissal of the original complaint in August 2025, after which plaintiffs amended their pleading to sharpen the disclosure theory around the $59.8 million conflict — underscoring that the case's survival turned specifically on how the omission was pled, not on liquidity delay as a general matter.

The complaint also points to related financial detail meant to illustrate stakes for the class: shareholders allege that net asset value across the Lightstone REITs has languished since the amendments took effect, while Lichtenstein collected more than $7 million in management fees in 2023 alone. Plaintiffs are seeking rescission of the 2023 charter amendments, a declaration that defendants breached their fiduciary duties, compensatory damages, and recovery of litigation costs — remedies that, if ultimately granted, could force a reassessment of governance and disclosure practices across Lightstone's non-traded REIT platform and invite comparable claims against other sponsors with similarly structured sponsor-side interests.

For the broader non-traded REIT sector, the ruling arrives at a moment when sponsors are already navigating heightened scrutiny of fee structures, valuation methodologies, and redemption gates. A federal court's willingness to let a proxy-disclosure theory proceed past the pleading stage — specifically because a sponsor's economic interest in the outcome of a duration-extending vote went unstated — gives plaintiffs' firms a workable template for challenging similar charter amendments elsewhere in the space, and gives REIT boards a concrete disclosure gap to close before their next solicitation.

## Practical Takeaways

- **Disclose the sponsor's stake in the vote's outcome, not just that a stake exists.** Naming a sponsor's subordinated or promotional interest in a proxy statement is not sufficient if the statement omits how that interest's value depends on the very outcome being solicited — that gap is what kept this case alive.
- **A shareholder vote is not automatic ratification.** Boards and advisers should not assume that a favorable vote forecloses later breach-of-fiduciary-duty claims; a ratification defense fails if the vote was not fully informed, and materiality is assessed with the benefit of hindsight in litigation.
- **Charter amendments touching liquidity timelines deserve independent review.** Extensions of durational provisions, elimination of listing deadlines, or similar structural changes warrant an independent committee's scrutiny of sponsor-side incentives before the solicitation goes out, not after a complaint is filed.
- **Expect follow-on claims.** Plaintiffs' firms actively track non-traded REIT structures with comparable subordinated-interest features; sponsors with similar arrangements should audit past proxy disclosures now rather than wait for a demand letter.
