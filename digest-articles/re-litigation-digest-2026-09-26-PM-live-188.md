# Residents' Suit Over Joliet's $20 Billion Data Center Clears Its First Court Test

### A procedural notice-and-open-meetings challenge to an 800-acre data center annexation is emerging as a bellwether for how citizen groups are contesting the current wave of hyperscale development nationally.

A lawsuit challenging the city of Joliet, Illinois's approval of the roughly $20 billion, 795-acre Joliet Technology Center data center campus survived its first substantive court test on September 8, 2026, when Will County Circuit Judge Victoria Breslan heard argument on motions to dismiss filed by the city and the project's developers rather than disposing of the case outright. The case, brought by the nonprofit Joliet Residents for Responsible Growth against the City of Joliet and developers Powerhouse and Hillwood, is one of a growing number of suits nationally in which neighbors of proposed hyperscale data center campuses are using procedural and open-government claims — rather than substantive land-use objections alone — to slow or unwind local approvals.

The underlying project is large by any measure: a 24-building, 1.8-gigawatt data center campus that the Joliet City Council approved by an 8-1 vote on March 19, 2026, after annexing roughly 795 acres of agricultural land and rezoning it to light industrial use. The plaintiffs, who filed suit on May 18, 2026, do not primarily argue that a data center is an inappropriate use for the site in the abstract; instead, their complaint centers on the process by which the city got there. It alleges that a February 2026 public notice failed to disclose that the rezoning under consideration involved a data center at all, and that a March 2026 city council meeting violated the Illinois Open Meetings Act by denying residents a meaningful opportunity to cross-examine testimony offered in support of the project, along with claims raising potential conflicts of interest in the city's review process.

That framing matters legally. Illinois law, like most states', gives local governments considerable substantive discretion over zoning and annexation decisions, and a straightforward argument that a rezoning decision was simply wrong on the merits faces a deferential standard of review in court. Procedural and open-meetings claims are different: they do not ask a judge to second-guess a legislative body's policy judgment, only to enforce the notice-and-process rules the legislature itself imposed on how that judgment gets made. A city that wins the substantive zoning argument can still lose if it did not follow the process the Open Meetings Act and public-notice statutes require — and if it loses on that basis, the remedy can be to unwind the approval and send the matter back through a compliant process, which is precisely what the Joliet plaintiffs are seeking by asking the court to void the March 19 annexation vote.

For an industry now investing tens of billions of dollars annually in hyperscale data center campuses, procedural vulnerability of this kind is a genuine and growing legal-risk category. Developers and municipalities alike have strong incentives to move large approvals quickly, given the scale of capital and the competitive pressure to secure sites before rivals do — but that same urgency is what tends to produce the truncated public notice and compressed hearing schedules that give plaintiffs procedural openings. The Joliet case is not occurring in isolation: a comparable data center zoning fight in Edgerton, Kansas recently produced a ruling against the municipality on separate anti-SLAPP grounds, and a Texas dispute over the Circle T Data Center in Westlake, also involving Hillwood, has already produced a temporary restraining order over an alleged public-notice defect. Together, these cases suggest that procedural rigor in the public approval process is becoming as important a risk-management priority for data center developers as the underlying zoning entitlement itself.

No ruling on the motions to dismiss had been reported as of this writing, and the case remains pending before Judge Breslan. Given the project's scale — a resident-cited concern was its expected draw on a regional aquifer and its power demand, described by the plaintiffs as comparable to Hoover Dam-level output — a ruling either allowing the case to proceed to a merits challenge of the annexation vote, or dismissing it and clearing the project's path, will be closely watched well beyond Will County.

## Practical takeaways

Developers and municipal counsel structuring large-scale data center or industrial rezoning approvals should treat public notice language and open-meetings procedure as first-order legal risk, not administrative formality, particularly where a project's true scale or use was not made explicit in early notices. Because procedural claims sidestep the deference courts otherwise give zoning bodies on the merits, they can succeed even where the underlying land-use decision would easily survive substantive review, making them an increasingly attractive vehicle for opposition groups nationally. Sponsors evaluating a site for a hyperscale campus should factor in the realistic possibility of a multi-month procedural challenge even after a favorable council vote, and build that contingency into acquisition and financing timelines. Finally, the pattern across Joliet, Edgerton, and Westlake suggests lenders and equity partners financing data center developments should specifically diligence the adequacy of the public approval record, not just the existence of a favorable vote, before treating zoning risk as resolved.
