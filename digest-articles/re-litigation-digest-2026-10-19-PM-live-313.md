# Ohio Supreme Court Lets Voters Undo a Developer's Rezoning: Sommers Development Group Loses Its Bid to Keep a Referendum Off the Ballot

### A developer that won its zoning case at city hall now faces losing it at the ballot box, after the state's high court found no legal basis to intervene in a citizen referendum over a Mayfield Heights townhome project.

Developers who clear a rezoning and a variance through a city council often treat that approval as the end of the entitlement fight. A decision issued October 19, 2026 by the Supreme Court of Ohio is a reminder that in a chartered municipality with initiative and referendum rights, the city council's vote can be only the opening round. In *State ex rel. Sommers Development Group, L.L.C. v. Cuyahoga County Board of Elections*, the court unanimously denied a developer's request for writs of prohibition and mandamus that would have kept a citizen-driven referendum off the November 3, 2026 ballot, clearing the way for voters in Mayfield Heights to decide whether to reverse a zoning change the city council had already approved for a planned townhome community.

**The underlying dispute.** Sommers Development Group, LLC is the developer under contract with Rockwell Automation, Inc., which owns the property, to redevelop a parcel in Mayfield Heights, a Cleveland suburb, from an office-park use into a townhome community. To do so, Sommers needed the city to rezone the land from a "Headquarters or Executive Offices Park District" classification to a "Planned Unit Development District" classification, along with a density variance and preliminary site-plan approval. On May 27, 2026, the Mayfield Heights City Council granted all of it, adopting the rezoning ordinance and a resolution approving the variance.

That might have ended the matter, but Ohio municipal charters commonly reserve initiative and referendum powers to the electorate, and Mayfield Heights' charter is no exception. On July 24, 2026, a citizens' committee opposed to the project submitted a petition seeking an ordinance that would return the property to its prior zoning classification. After a hearing that produced no vote, the city council held a special meeting on September 1 and voted 4-3 not to adopt the committee's proposed ordinance itself — which, under the charter's initiative mechanism, sends the question to the voters rather than ending it. The committee then filed a supplemental petition to shore up its signature count, and the Cuyahoga County Board of Elections determined the petition carried 691 valid signatures, more than enough to qualify. On September 9, the council authorized certification of the initiative to the ballot for the November 3 general election.

Sommers protested to the board of elections the next day, arguing the petition was procedurally defective on four grounds: that it was really an untimely referendum petition dressed up as an initiative; that the underlying zoning action was an "administrative" act not subject to referendum at all; that the petition committee and council had not complied with the charter's own requirements for placing an initiative on the ballot; and that the council had certified the petition to the board after the statutory deadline. The board held a hearing on September 21 and denied the protest. Sommers took its case directly to the Ohio Supreme Court four days later, seeking an emergency order either barring the board from placing the measure on the ballot or directing it to grant the protest — and, if ballots had already been printed, barring the board from counting any votes cast on the question.

**What the court decided.** The board of elections and the intervening petition committee first argued Sommers had waited too long, invoking the doctrine of laches, which bars a claim when a party unreasonably delays asserting a right to the other side's prejudice. The court rejected that defense outright, holding that Sommers acted within a day of the council's certification resolution in filing its protest, and within four days of the protest's denial in filing suit — timelines the board and committee did not themselves characterize as unreasonable. The court distinguished older precedent suggesting a challenger must object as soon as a petition is submitted to a city council, holding instead that Sommers' sequence of filings satisfied the "extreme diligence" Ohio law demands in election cases.

Having cleared that procedural hurdle, Sommers still lost. The court held that Sommers failed to show the board of elections abused its discretion or clearly disregarded applicable law when it certified the initiative and rejected Sommers' protest, and denied both the writ of prohibition and the writ of mandamus. The practical result: the referendum stays on the November 3 ballot, and Mayfield Heights voters — not the courts — will decide whether the rezoning that cleared the way for Sommers' townhome project survives.

For commercial developers, the case is a pointed illustration of a risk that is easy to underweight during entitlement planning in charter municipalities with initiative and referendum rights: a council approval is not necessarily the final word, and courts will extend real procedural latitude to election boards processing a citizen challenge to that approval, even while giving a developer the benefit of the doubt on how quickly it had to object. Winning the laches argument did Sommers no good once the merits went against it. Developers facing a possible referendum fight should budget for the political and ballot-box risk separately from the zoning-approval process itself, scrutinize a municipality's charter provisions on initiative, referendum and certification deadlines before relying on a council vote as final, and recognize that Ohio courts have shown little appetite to second-guess a board of elections' procedural calls absent a clear abuse of discretion or a clear disregard of the law. Where a project depends on a contested rezoning, lining up public and political support ahead of any council vote may do more to protect the entitlement than any post-approval litigation strategy.
