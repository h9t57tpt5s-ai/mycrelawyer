# Phoenix Must Defend Data-Center Zoning Rules or Face $54M Takings Claim, Arizona Court Rules

*A Maricopa County judge keeps alive a Texas developer's Proposition 207 claim against Phoenix's data-center ordinance, forcing the city to prove at trial that the rule is a necessary public-health measure rather than an uncompensated taking.*

A Maricopa County Superior Court judge has ruled that the City of Phoenix must prove its 2025 data-center zoning ordinance is a necessary protection for public health, safety, or welfare — or pay a Texas-based developer as much as $54 million for the drop in value the ordinance caused to a parcel of industrial land. The order, reported October 28, 2026, narrows but does not end a lawsuit brought by West Buckeye Road L.P. (WBR), and it puts Phoenix's new special-permit regime for data centers squarely inside Arizona's unusual takings statute, Proposition 207, with a full evidentiary hearing already set for November 17.

WBR's dispute traces back to 2021, when the company bought industrial land in Phoenix and began spending millions of dollars preparing the site for a data-center development, at a time when the land's zoning permitted that use as of right. In 2025, amid a broader backlash against the power, water, and noise demands of hyperscale data centers, the Phoenix City Council adopted an ordinance requiring developers to obtain a special permit before building a data center anywhere in the city — effectively stripping WBR's parcel of the by-right development rights the company had relied on when it bought the land. WBR sued, advancing two theories: that the ordinance itself created a right to compensation, and, separately, that the ordinance triggered compensation under Proposition 207, the 2006 voter-approved Private Property Rights Protection Act.

The judge split the difference. The court dismissed WBR's claim that the ordinance's own text supported a damages suit, reasoning that nothing in the ordinance itself authorizes a developer to sue the city for compensation. But the Proposition 207 claim survived. That statute, codified at Arizona Revised Statutes section 12-1134, requires a local government to compensate a property owner when a land-use law reduces the property's fair market value — unless the government proves the law falls within one of the statute's own exemptions, including a measure genuinely necessary to prevent a public nuisance or protect public health and safety. The court's ruling means Phoenix cannot simply point to its police power in the abstract; at the November 17 hearing, the city will have to put on evidence that its data-center permitting rule is actually necessary to protect the public, not merely a land-use preference dressed up in public-safety language.

Phoenix Mayor Kate Gallego's office characterized the partial dismissal as a win, but the surviving claim is the one with the dollar figure attached, and it is far from an isolated dispute. Since Phoenix adopted the ordinance, reporting has tracked more than a dozen landowners and developers filing Proposition 207 notices of claim or lawsuits over the same rule, with the city granting waivers to some owners to resolve or avoid litigation and other claims — beyond WBR's $54 million demand — still pending. The WBR ruling is likely to function as a bellwether for the rest of that docket: if Phoenix cannot carry its burden at the November hearing on this parcel, every other similarly situated landowner has a template for forcing the same showing — or a payout.

The case lands at the center of a fight playing out in dozens of cities simultaneously, as municipalities from Michigan to Georgia have reached for moratoriums, rezonings, and special-permit regimes to slow data-center development, and developers have answered with lawsuits grounded in due process, equal protection, and state zoning-enabling statutes. Arizona's Proposition 207 gives Phoenix-area developers a tool most of those other jurisdictions' challengers lack: a statutory damages remedy that does not require proving a regulatory taking under the stricter federal constitutional standard, only that the law reduced the property's fair market value and does not fit a narrow statutory exemption.

**Practical takeaways:**

- In Arizona, a zoning change that restricts a previously by-right use — not just an outright development ban — can trigger a Proposition 207 damages claim if it measurably reduces fair market value, and the burden falls on the municipality to prove a statutory exemption applies.
- Municipalities drafting data-center (or similar) permitting ordinances in Prop 207 states should build a contemporaneous evidentiary record — studies, hearings, findings — showing the restriction is necessary to address a genuine public-health or safety harm, not simply a land-use preference, because that record is what the government will need to defend the ordinance later in court.
- Developers holding by-right industrial or commercial entitlements that a new ordinance strips away should document acquisition cost, pre-ordinance value, and sunk development costs promptly; those figures become the measure of damages in any Prop 207 or takings claim.
- Owners of similarly zoned parcels in the same jurisdiction should treat a bellwether ruling like this one as a signal of how much leverage they may have in settlement negotiations or waiver requests, rather than waiting for their own case to reach judgment.
