# Georgia Court of Appeals Backs County's Industrial Expansion Permit Over Developer's Objection

### Appellate panel affirms that a conditional use permit for an adjacent waste-processing operation survived a residential developer's zoning challenge, underscoring the deference courts give local land-use decisions

A Georgia residential developer's attempt to block a neighboring industrial operation from expanding next door has failed on appeal, in a ruling that illustrates just how much deference Georgia courts give to a local government's own zoning and conditional-use decisions. On November 2, 2026, the Court of Appeals of Georgia affirmed a trial court's rejection of a challenge brought by Norhaven Partners, LLC and an affiliated entity, Peeples Owner LLC, against Bartow County's decision to grant a conditional use permit allowing a neighboring industrial property owner to expand a waste-processing operation.

The dispute, *Norhaven Partners, LLC v. Bartow County*, centers on two adjacent parcels in Bartow County. In January 2023, the county approved conceptual site plans submitted by Norhaven to develop a 97-unit townhome community, known as the Overlook on Pettit, on residentially zoned land. By October 2023, Norhaven's development plans had been fully approved, including a land use disturbance permit. Immediately to the east of that planned residential community sits a separate parcel zoned heavy industrial and owned by Justin Rice and his affiliated company, Rice Commercial Investments, Inc. Rice had already been operating a mulch materials processing, wood shredding, and rock crushing business on that property. In July 2024, Rice applied for a conditional use permit to expand that operation to include additional recovered-materials processing. The Bartow County Board of Commissioners granted the permit.

Norhaven petitioned for judicial review of the commissioner's decision, arguing the industrial expansion would be detrimental to its adjacent townhome development. The trial court initially addressed several of Norhaven's arguments but remanded the matter back to the county commissioner for further factual findings and legal conclusions on the remaining issues. After the commissioner made those findings on remand and held a further hearing, the trial court again rejected Norhaven's arguments in full. Norhaven then appealed to the Georgia Court of Appeals, raising three claims of error: that the trial court improperly limited its review to the written transcript of the public hearing before the commissioner; that the court erred by remanding the case for further factual findings when the existing record was, in Norhaven's view, susceptible to only one reasonable interpretation; and that the court erred in denying Norhaven's petition after the commissioner allegedly failed to follow the trial court's instructions on remand.

Writing for the panel, Presiding Judge Sara L. Doyle rejected each argument and affirmed. The court's opinion reiterated the standard that governs judicial review of a quasi-judicial zoning decision by a local governing authority: a reviewing court must determine whether the agency's factual findings are supported by any evidence and examine whether its legal conclusions are sound, while legal conclusions receive de novo review. Critically, the court emphasized that on appeal from a trial court's review of such a quasi-judicial proceeding, the appellate court's task is not to assess whether the record supports the trial court's own order, but whether the record supports the final decision of the quasi-judicial agency itself, here, the Bartow County Board of Commissioners. Measured against that deferential framework, the panel found no basis to disturb the county's grant of the conditional use permit to the competing industrial use.

The decision matters to commercial and residential developers alike because it reinforces just how difficult it can be to use litigation to second-guess a county's conditional use or zoning determination once that determination survives the administrative process. Developers frequently assume that a favorable site plan approval for their own project effectively locks in the surrounding land-use environment, but this case shows that a neighboring property's separate zoning classification and its own conditional use rights remain very much alive, and a local government's decision to accommodate a neighboring industrial expansion will be reviewed only for evidentiary support and legal soundness, not re-litigated on the merits of whether it was the best policy outcome for adjacent landowners. The ruling also illustrates the risk of relying on procedural arguments, such as disputing the scope of the evidentiary record or the propriety of a remand, when the underlying substantive zoning decision itself is unlikely to be overturned.

For developers evaluating sites adjacent to existing or future industrial, commercial, or other intensive uses, the case is a reminder to conduct thorough land-use due diligence on neighboring parcels before committing to a project, since a developer has limited post-approval recourse if a neighbor later obtains permission to expand an already-permitted use. Practical takeaways: first, before acquiring or developing a site, investigate not only its own zoning and conditional use history, but also the zoning classification, permitted uses, and any pending conditional use applications on adjacent and nearby parcels, since an approved site plan for one property does not constrain a neighbor's separate zoning rights. Second, when challenging a local government's quasi-judicial land-use decision, recognize that Georgia courts apply a deferential "any evidence" standard to factual findings and will not treat the appeal as an opportunity to relitigate the wisdom of the decision; procedural objections about the scope of the record or a remand are unlikely to succeed unless they can show the decision itself lacks evidentiary or legal support. Third, developers concerned about detrimental neighboring uses should participate actively, and build a robust record of specific, evidence-based objections, during the public comment and administrative hearing process itself, since that record will largely determine the outcome of any later judicial review, win or lose.
