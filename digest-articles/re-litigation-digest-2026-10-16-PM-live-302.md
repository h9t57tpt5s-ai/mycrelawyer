# Alabama Supreme Court Affirms Strict Summary-Judgment Deadlines, Preserves Landlord's Unpaid-Rent and Indemnity Win Against Ballet Tenant

### A consolidated appeal over a commercial shopping-center lease shows how a missed filing deadline — not the merits — can decide a six-figure landlord-tenant dispute

The Supreme Court of Alabama on October 16, 2026, affirmed a trial court's decision to strike as untimely a commercial tenant's opposition to its landlord's motion for partial summary judgment, leaving in place a $65,827.50 unpaid-rent judgment and a ruling that the tenant owes its landlord defense and indemnity on personal-injury claims arising from the same leased premises. In a companion cross-appeal, the Court also held that the trial court could not simultaneously certify the partial judgment as final under Rule 54(b) and purport to dismiss the entire case outright, reversing that portion of the order and remanding for further proceedings.

The case, *Commercial Properties, Inc. v. Ourania Charalambidou, Dionysios Mitsios, and Montgomery Civic Ballet, Inc., d/b/a Montgomery Ballet Studios* (consolidated appeal numbers SC-2026-0190 and SC-2026-0191), arose from two commercial leases that Commercial Properties, Inc., as landlord, signed with Montgomery Civic Ballet, Inc. in May 2008 and January 2012. Under those leases, the Ballet rented two suites in a shopping center owned and managed by Commercial Properties. The leases contained a tenant indemnification provision, a default provision, and a provision making the tenant liable for the landlord's attorney fees and costs in enforcing the lease.

In December 2020, the Ballet vacated the premises, alleging the landlord had failed to remediate mold in the leased space. The following month, the Ballet and its affiliated individual plaintiffs filed a multicount complaint against the landlord, alleging personal injuries from mold exposure and damage to the Ballet's property, including its costumes. The landlord answered and counterclaimed for unpaid rent, and also demanded that the Ballet defend and indemnify it against the personal-injury claims under the lease's indemnification clause — a demand the Ballet never answered.

In February 2023, the landlord moved for partial summary judgment, seeking an interlocutory judgment that the Ballet owed $65,827.50 in unpaid rent, a ruling that the Ballet owed the landlord defense and indemnity on the personal-injury claims, and judgment in its favor on the Ballet's own claims against it. The motion sat unresolved for two years — a delay the landlord attributed to no fault of either party: the original trial judge assigned to the case, Johnny Hardwick, was assaulted while the case was pending and suffered injuries that kept him from his duties before he retired, and the case was reassigned to Circuit Court Judge Tiffany McCord.

When a hearing on the motion was finally set for April 21, 2025, the Ballet filed its opposition on the Friday before — which the landlord argued, and the trial court agreed, was one business day too late under the interplay of Alabama Rule of Civil Procedure 56(c)(2), which requires a summary-judgment response at least two business days before a hearing, and Rule 6(a)'s computation rules excluding intervening weekends. The trial court struck the response as untimely, leaving the summary-judgment motion unopposed, though it did not immediately rule on the motion's merits — an apparent oversight that triggered another round of procedural skirmishing before the court finally entered partial summary judgment for the landlord in December 2025, on the same record it had deemed unopposed nearly eight months earlier.

On appeal, the Ballet, its executive director, and his wife did not challenge the summary judgment on the merits in their opening brief — an argument the Court held was waived when first raised only in reply. Instead, they argued only that striking their late response had denied them a fair hearing. The Supreme Court, authored by Justice Will Sellers, rejected that argument, holding that the plaintiffs had notice of both the summary-judgment hearing and the motion to strike, and that any failure of their counsel to appear and be heard that day was not the trial court's doing.

On the landlord's cross-appeal, the Court addressed an irregularity in the trial court's February 2026 order: it certified the partial summary judgment as a final, appealable order under Rule 54(b), while at the same time purporting to dismiss the entire case — two characterizations the Supreme Court found could not both be true at once. The Court affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for the trial court to clarify the procedural posture of the claims that remained outside the scope of the partial summary judgment, including the personal-injury claims the individual plaintiffs brought against the landlord directly.

Practical takeaways for commercial landlords and tenants: deadlines in summary-judgment practice are measured in business days, not calendar days, and Alabama courts will enforce that distinction even against a response filed only hours late. A commercial tenant that stops paying rent or vacates over a maintenance dispute, rather than pursuing its claims while continuing to perform under the lease, risks losing the chance to contest the resulting judgment on the merits if it also stumbles procedurally. Indemnification and attorney-fee clauses in a commercial lease are not symbolic: here, a landlord recovered unpaid rent and shifted the cost of a tenant's personal-injury exposure claims back onto the tenant, almost entirely because the tenant's own filing missed a short window. Finally, parties relying on a Rule 54(b) certification to appeal a partial ruling should make sure the trial court's order does not simultaneously describe the entire case as dismissed, since that kind of internal inconsistency can send the matter back down rather than resolve it.
