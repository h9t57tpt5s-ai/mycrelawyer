# Des Moines Physician's Suit Pulls a Real Estate CEO Into the Widening Daniel Pettit Fraud Fallout

### A private $850,000 loan gone unpaid has become the vehicle for the first lawsuit naming Rob Orton, CEO of a roughly twenty-company real estate holding group, as a defendant alongside jailed Johnston developer Daniel Pettit.

Dr. Bernard Feldman, a 72-year-old Des Moines gastroenterologist and private real estate lender, filed suit in Iowa District Court for Polk County on April 22 against Daniel Pettit, Rob Orton, and their jointly held entity, Mozart Properties, alleging breach of contract over a $850,000 loan extended in 2021 that was never repaid, with roughly $32,000 in accrued interest outstanding at the time of filing. The complaint is notable less for its dollar figure than for who it names: Orton, president of Orton Homes and chief executive of American Group LLC, an umbrella holding company for roughly twenty real estate-related businesses including RE/MAX Precision in Clive, had not previously been named as a defendant in the wave of litigation surrounding Pettit's finances.

The lawsuit arrives amid a broader unraveling of Pettit's real estate empire that has drawn scrutiny from the FBI, the Johnston Police Department, and the Iowa Insurance Division. Pettit, the 43-year-old owner of Encompass Holdings and controlling partner in numerous limited liability companies, built a track record of substantial development deals in the Des Moines metro before running into significant financial distress after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. A subsequent probe found that Pettit allegedly owed roughly $70 million to banks, private investors, and former business partners, several of whom have already obtained judgments against him. Feldman himself previously won a separate judgment of nearly $9.3 million against Pettit after alleging fraud, fraudulent inducement, and misrepresentation in an earlier case. Pettit was later jailed in Polk County after spending more than a month evading a nationwide warrant, and additional investors, including a California couple, have since joined the line of creditors seeking to collect.

The April complaint alleges that the 2021 loan to Pettit, Orton, and Mozart Properties was never repaid according to its terms, and pursues a straightforward breach-of-contract theory against all three defendants jointly. Unlike Feldman's earlier fraud-based judgment against Pettit individually, this suit's decision to add Orton and the jointly held Mozart Properties entity signals an effort by creditors to identify every solvent party connected to Pettit's dealings, rather than pursuing recovery solely against a developer whose assets and liquidity are already deeply compromised and subject to competing claims.

For commercial real estate lenders and private investors who extend capital directly to developers outside of institutional bank financing, often on the strength of a personal relationship or a developer's track record rather than a fully diligenced credit underwriting process, the Pettit fallout illustrates both the appeal and the risk of that kind of private lending. Feldman's original loan, like many private notes extended to active local developers, appears to have been made on relatively informal terms relative to a bank facility, and the case underscores how difficult recovery becomes once a borrower's financial distress metastasizes across dozens of affiliated entities and multiple competing creditors. It also highlights a recovery strategy worth watching: when a primary borrower's own assets are insufficient or already encumbered by prior judgments, creditors increasingly look to co-obligors, guarantors, and business partners named on the same loan documents or jointly held entities, even when those parties have not previously faced public litigation over the underlying default.

The practical lesson for real estate lenders, investors, and the counsel who structure private notes extends beyond this single case. Private lenders financing local developers should insist on clear, individually enforceable obligations from every principal and affiliated entity involved in a transaction, rather than relying on a single borrower's signature, since joint-and-several liability structured at origination becomes the difference between a recoverable claim and a worthless judgment once distress sets in. Diligence on a borrower's other outstanding obligations and pending litigation, not just their historical track record, has become essential in markets where a single overextended developer can generate cascading defaults across an entire network of investors. And once fraud or insolvency concerns surface around a borrower, co-investors and lenders should move quickly to identify and pursue all potentially solvent co-obligors before competing creditors exhaust the available recovery pool, a race that is playing out in real time as additional plaintiffs continue to join the queue against Pettit and his associates.
