# Lender Moves to Force Sale of Philadelphia's Bourse Building as Developer Partnership Collapses

### KKR Real Estate Finance Trust says its $83.7M loan is in default after construction stalled and the project's joint venture partners turned on each other in Delaware Chancery Court

A commercial mortgage lender has asked a federal court to order a public auction of one of Philadelphia's most storied commercial buildings, the latest and most consequential turn in a redevelopment project that collapsed into open litigation between its own joint venture partners. KKR Real Estate Finance Trust Inc. filed suit on July 28, 2026 in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Pennsylvania against the ownership entities behind the Bourse building at 111 South Independence Mall East and the adjacent property at 400 Market Street, alleging default on an $83.7 million acquisition and construction loan and seeking a $24.2 million judgment plus interest, fees, and a judicial sale of the collateral. For commercial real estate lenders and joint venture sponsors alike, the case is a pointed illustration of how quickly a partnership breakdown can convert a performing construction loan into a forced liquidation.

The underlying project traces to June 2024, when an entity controlled by Philadelphia developer Dean Adler and his firm, Lubert-Adler Real Estate Funds, partnered with Keystone Development + Investment to acquire the 131-year-old Beaux-Arts Bourse building and the neighboring 400 Market Street from KKR Real Estate Finance Trust for roughly $41 million, financed in part by an $83.7 million loan from the same KKR entity. The venture's plan was ambitious: convert the historic Bourse into a 152-key hotel with restaurant and event space, while transforming 400 Market into a 176-unit apartment building, capitalizing on Old City's push toward adaptive reuse of aging commercial stock near Independence Mall.

That plan began unraveling in early 2026, when Adler moved to abandon the hotel concept and pursue a multifamily conversion of the Bourse instead, in partnership with PMC Property Group, Philadelphia's largest apartment owner, rather than Keystone. Keystone responded by suing in the Delaware Court of Chancery, accusing Adler of attempting to oust it as a managing partner and secured a temporary restraining order in March 2026 that kept Keystone in control of the venture while the ouster dispute was litigated. Adler counterclaimed, accusing Keystone of gross mismanagement and unauthorized spending that, in his telling, ran the once-celebrated project "into the ground," while Keystone maintained that Adler had failed to adequately capitalize the development and was maneuvering to install a new partner unilaterally. The Chancery fight over control has continued in parallel with the building's deteriorating finances.

While the ownership dispute played out in Delaware, work on the Bourse itself ground to a halt. According to KKR's federal complaint, the venture stopped making mortgage payments beginning in June 2026, and at least five contractors have filed more than $2 million in mechanic's liens against the property for unpaid work, a sign that the paralysis at the ownership level was already bleeding into the construction site and its subcontractors well before the lender moved. KKR's suit asserts that halting construction and missing payments constitutes a default under the loan documents, and asks the court for a $24.2 million money judgment representing the outstanding principal plus accrued interest and fees, along with an order directing a public sale of the Bourse and 400 Market Street to satisfy the debt.

The case sits at the intersection of two distinct but related legal problems that increasingly confront complex real estate joint ventures. The first is governance: when partners disagree sharply enough about a project's direction that one tries to force the other out, the resulting fight, typically litigated in Delaware given how often these ventures are structured through Delaware entities, can immobilize decision-making at exactly the moment a project needs it most. The second is credit risk: a lender's collateral does not wait patiently while its borrowers resolve an internal governance dispute. Missed payments, lapsed insurance, halted construction, and mounting mechanic's liens all continue to erode collateral value in real time, and a sophisticated lender facing that erosion has strong incentive to act quickly rather than wait for a resolution in a case to which it is not even a party.

For sponsors structuring joint ventures on complex, high-profile adaptive-reuse or construction projects, the Bourse dispute underscores that operating agreements should specify, well before a disagreement arises, who retains authority over major decisions like a change in project scope, how a deadlock over strategy gets resolved, and what obligations continue to run to lenders regardless of an internal governance fight. It is also a reminder that a governance dispute between sponsors is not a valid excuse for missed debt service from a lender's perspective, and that construction lenders monitoring a stalled project will often move on a parallel track the moment payments lapse, independent of and unconcerned with how any related ownership litigation ultimately resolves. Lenders on similarly structured deals, for their part, should treat a public partnership rupture between co-sponsors as an early warning sign meriting closer loan monitoring, since by the time missed payments and mechanic's liens appear, as they did here within months of the Chancery Court fight becoming public, a formal default declaration is frequently not far behind.
