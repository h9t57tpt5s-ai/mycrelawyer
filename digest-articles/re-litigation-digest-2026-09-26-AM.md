# Brightline's Real Estate Arms Land in Chapter 11 as Rail Parent Restructures $5.5 Billion Debt

### Seventeen affiliated entities — including two transit-adjacent development companies — filed jointly administered Chapter 11 petitions in New Jersey on September 24, keeping train operations running while the holding structure reorganizes.

Fortress Investment Group-backed Brightline Holdings LLC and sixteen affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11 protection on September 24, 2026, in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of New Jersey, opening one of the largest transportation-and-real-estate restructurings of the year. The petitions, jointly administered under a lead case captioned In re FIHPNP LLC (Case No. 26-20876) before Judge Mark E. Hall, list combined assets and liabilities of between $1 billion and $10 billion and are aimed at restructuring a debt stack that has grown to roughly $5.5 billion. Among the debtor entities are New Flagler Development LLC and Brightline Property Holdings LLC — companies formed to hold and develop the real estate around Brightline's stations — putting a set of commercial real estate assets squarely inside a Chapter 11 case for the first time.

For property owners, lenders, and developers who deal in transit-oriented development, the filing is a reminder that the real estate wrapped around an infrastructure asset can be pulled into that asset's financial distress even when the two businesses are nominally separate. Brightline's core operating company, Brightline Trains Florida LLC, was deliberately excluded from the filing and will continue running its Miami-to-Orlando passenger service without interruption. But the development entities that hold and build out station-area real estate — offices, retail, and mixed-use parcels marketed as amenities of the rail line itself — are now debtors in possession, subject to the bankruptcy court's oversight of their financing, leasing, and disposition decisions.

The restructuring follows more than a year of strain. Brightline has been skipping interest payments and negotiating with creditor groups for much of 2026 in an effort to right-size its capital structure, which includes unrated bonds, bank debt, and project-level financing tied to specific stations and real estate parcels. To fund the case, Brightline has arranged $258 million in debtor-in-possession financing from bond insurer Assured Guaranty Ltd. and other investors, who have also agreed to backstop $490 million in new debt once the company exits bankruptcy. That structure — DIP financing from the same constituency that will hold post-emergence paper — is a common feature of infrastructure-adjacent reorganizations, where the DIP lenders are frequently existing bondholders trying to protect and reposition their exposure rather than new money coming in from outside.

The filing does not arrive in isolation for Brightline's real estate footprint. A separate, unrelated foreclosure action is already pending against Brightline Investment Holdings LLC and its subsidiary DTS DT Retail LLC over the retail podium at Brightline's MiamiCentral Station in Miami, where U.S. Bank, acting for a senior lender group, sued in July 2026 to foreclose on a defaulted $65 million loan tranche and force appointment of a receiver. That case proceeds independently in Miami-Dade County Circuit Court and is not part of the New Jersey Chapter 11, underscoring how Brightline's real estate holdings are structured — and now failing — asset by asset rather than as a single consolidated portfolio.

For commercial real estate practitioners, the structural lesson is in the separateness itself. Brightline organized its station-area development entities, like New Flagler Development and Brightline Property Holdings, as distinct legal entities from both the rail operating company and from asset-specific holding vehicles like Brightline Investment Holdings. That separation is precisely what let the operating railroad stay out of the Chapter 11 filing and kept the MiamiCentral retail foreclosure confined to its own entity and its own court. Lenders financing transit-oriented or infrastructure-adjacent real estate should read the case closely for how New Jersey's bankruptcy court treats intercompany claims, shared services agreements, and any guaranties running between the debtor development entities and the non-debtor operating company — those provisions will determine how much of the sponsor's distress actually reaches the real estate, and how much stays walled off.

**Practical takeaways:** Owners and lenders financing station-area or infrastructure-adjacent real estate should confirm exactly which legal entity in a sponsor's structure holds their collateral, since a parent-level restructuring can pull in development subsidiaries even while an operating business is deliberately excluded. Secured creditors of the debtor entities should move quickly to understand their standing in the jointly administered case and how the $258 million DIP facility and proposed $490 million exit financing will affect their priority. Counterparties to separate, non-bankruptcy litigation against affiliated Brightline entities — such as the pending MiamiCentral retail foreclosure — should not assume that litigation is stayed or otherwise affected absent an entity actually being a debtor in the New Jersey case. Finally, the case is a useful template for reviewing how a sponsor's own transaction documents allocate guaranties and cross-default triggers between an operating business and its real estate development affiliates, before a similar restructuring forces the question.
