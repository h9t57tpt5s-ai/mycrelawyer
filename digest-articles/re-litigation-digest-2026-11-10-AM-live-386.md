# Supreme Court Ends Developer's Bid to Blame Pontiac, Michigan for a Collapsed Cannabis Redevelopment

**The U.S. Supreme Court declined to review a Sixth Circuit ruling that permitting delays by a Michigan city, even ones that killed a $45 million cannabis redevelopment project, were ordinary bureaucratic friction rather than a constitutional violation.**

On November 9, 2026, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in Rubicon Real Estate Holdings, LLC v. City of Pontiac, Michigan, closing out a dispute that began in January 2023 and traced back to a derelict, roughly 26.5-acre parcel on Glenwood Avenue that Rubicon had planned to redevelop into a medical marijuana cultivation and processing hub. The denial leaves intact a published June 18, 2026 opinion from a three-judge Sixth Circuit panel affirming summary judgment for the city, which in turn left standing a federal district court's earlier dismissal of Rubicon's entire case. For commercial developers pursuing cannabis-related real estate projects, and for any developer tempted to recast a frustrating municipal permitting fight as a federal civil-rights claim, the case is now a closed, cautionary data point rather than a live threat to Pontiac.

Rubicon's plan centered on converting the long-vacant site into licensed cannabis cultivation and processing facilities, a use Michigan law allows municipalities to permit but does not require them to fast-track. According to Rubicon's complaint, the Pontiac Planning Commission and City Council initially cooperated, approving a rezoning request for the property. But Rubicon alleged that over the following years, the city and its clerk, acting through delay rather than outright denial, dragged the permitting and licensing process out for nearly four years. That delay, Rubicon said, caused its construction lender to walk away from a committed $45 million loan needed to build out the facilities, since lenders financing a time-sensitive cannabis project are generally unwilling to keep capital committed indefinitely while local approvals stall. Rubicon also alleged a retaliation angle: that city officials targeted its principal after he won an earlier, unrelated ruling against the city in 2021, including by pressuring a contractor to terminate a roughly $9.3 million contract connected to his design firm.

Rubicon and an affiliated design firm sued the City of Pontiac and its clerk in January 2023, first in Oakland County Circuit Court and then in federal court in the Eastern District of Michigan, seeking roughly $60 million in damages and fees. The complaint asserted that the city's conduct violated the Equal Protection and Due Process Clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment, along with First Amendment retaliation and Fifth Amendment claims, framing years of permitting delay as intentional, targeted government action rather than ordinary municipal slowness. The federal district court rejected that framing, granting the city's motion for summary judgment and dismissing the case on the ground that the record did not establish a triable issue that the project's collapse resulted from a constitutional violation rather than discretionary, if slow-moving, bureaucratic process.

The Sixth Circuit's June 2026 opinion affirmed that result. The panel's reasoning tracks a pattern now familiar to commercial developers who have tried to convert permitting friction into a federal case: without evidence that a city singled out a specific applicant for different treatment than similarly situated developers, or that officials acted for a constitutionally impermissible reason, a slow or frustrating approval process is not itself a due process or equal protection violation, even when the financial consequences for the developer are severe. Rubicon's September 2026 petition asking the Supreme Court to take up the case did not persuade the justices to intervene, and the November 9 order list denying certiorari means the Sixth Circuit's holding is now the final word in the litigation.

The case is a reminder of how structurally difficult it is for a real estate developer to win a federal constitutional claim out of a permitting dispute with a municipality, no matter how costly the delay turns out to be. Courts consistently treat the ordinary friction of local land-use and licensing review, including review of a politically sensitive use like cannabis cultivation, as a matter for state law and local political accountability rather than federal civil-rights litigation, absent concrete evidence of discriminatory or retaliatory intent tied to a specific protected act. That has direct underwriting consequences: developers and their lenders financing cannabis, cultivation, or other use-restricted commercial redevelopment projects that depend on discretionary municipal sign-off should build permitting-timeline risk into their loan structures and term sheets rather than assume a court will later redistribute that risk back onto the municipality if approvals run long, and they should document specific instances of disparate treatment contemporaneously if they ever intend to make a civil-rights claim, since a generalized, multi-year narrative of slow bureaucracy is unlikely to clear the bar on its own. The nearly four-year gap between Rubicon's purchase and the collapse of its financing also illustrates why patient capital, or financing structured to tolerate permitting delay, is often a better fit for politically contested land uses than conventional construction debt with a fixed funding window.
