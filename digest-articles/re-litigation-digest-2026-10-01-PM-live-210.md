# Special Servicer Seizes Century-Old San Francisco Office Building After $15.7M Loan Default

### Rialto Capital's foreclosure takeover of 580 Market Street underscores how far Financial District office values have fallen — and how lenders are increasingly choosing to hold distressed collateral rather than sell into a weak market.

A century-old mixed-use office and retail building in the heart of San Francisco's Financial District has passed into the hands of its lender, in the latest sign that the city's office market distress has not yet bottomed out. Special servicer Rialto Capital took control of 580 Market Street through a non-judicial foreclosure sale, after the building's owner, an affiliate of La Jolla-based Paragon Company, failed to repay a $15.7 million loan that matured in June 2025.

The foreclosure followed a familiar pattern for maturity-default cases working through California's trustee-sale process. Rialto served Paragon with a notice of default in October 2025 after the loan went unpaid past maturity, setting the statutory clock running toward a public trustee's sale. By the time the trustee's deed was recorded this past July, the unpaid debt — inflated by accrued default interest and fees on top of the original principal — had grown to approximately $17.5 million. At the sale, Rialto submitted a credit bid of $5 million, well below the outstanding debt, and took title to the roughly 35,000-square-foot building, located a block from the Montgomery Street BART and Muni Metro station.

The gap between what Rialto bid and what it was owed illustrates just how far the property's value has fallen. Built in 1907 and long operated as a Class B office and ground-floor retail building, 580 Market last traded in 2015, when a Paragon affiliate paid roughly $24.2 million, or about $690 per square foot, for the property. An appraisal commissioned earlier this year valued the building at just $9.9 million — a decline of nearly 60% in little more than a decade, and a figure that sits well below even the reduced $17.5 million payoff amount Rialto claimed at the sale. Occupancy had fallen to roughly 53% as of the most recent reporting available before the foreclosure, with several of the building's largest remaining tenants facing lease expirations clustered in a narrow window, adding near-term leasing risk on top of the debt overhang.

For commercial property owners and lenders, the case is a useful data point on how special servicers are approaching distressed San Francisco office debt in the current cycle. Rather than flipping the collateral quickly at a loss, Rialto has signaled it is weighing whether to hold and lease up the building or market it for sale as-is, with any disposition not expected before the first quarter of 2028 — a multi-year hold period that reflects both the depth of the city's office vacancy problem and special servicers' general reluctance to crystallize losses in a market still searching for a floor. That timeline also matters for borrowers and junior lienholders elsewhere in the capital stack: a servicer willing to sit on real estate owned (REO) assets for years, rather than liquidate promptly, changes the calculus for anyone hoping a foreclosure sale will quickly reset basis and attract fresh capital back into a building.

The transaction is also a reminder that California's non-judicial foreclosure process, while faster than a judicial foreclosure in many states, still leaves meaningful value on the table when a credit bid undercuts even a deeply discounted appraisal. Borrowers facing a looming maturity default should not assume that a lender's published appraisal, or even its credit bid at sale, represents a market-clearing price; special servicers routinely bid below their own internal valuations to preserve flexibility, including the ability to pursue a deficiency claim against a guarantor where recourse exists, or to negotiate a post-foreclosure settlement with the former owner regarding tenant improvement allowances, pending litigation, or other liabilities that survive the transfer of title. Owners of other aging, partially leased office buildings in San Francisco's Financial District — a submarket where several nearby towers have also traded at steep discounts or gone through special servicing in the past two years — should treat the 580 Market outcome as a fresh comparable for underwriting their own refinancing or workout negotiations, since appraisers and lenders alike are likely to point to this sale as evidence of where pricing has actually settled, rather than where pre-pandemic valuations once stood.

**Practical takeaways:**

- A trustee's sale credit bid is not a market valuation. Borrowers and guarantors negotiating a pre-foreclosure workout should anticipate that a special servicer may bid well below both the outstanding debt and any third-party appraisal, and should clarify in writing whether the lender intends to pursue a deficiency claim after taking title.
- Lease expiration clustering compounds foreclosure risk. Owners refinancing a maturing loan on a partially leased building should expect lenders to scrutinize near-term rollover exposure as closely as the loan-to-value ratio itself.
- A lender's extended hold timeline for REO assets can signal how a submarket's distress will be priced going forward; nearby owners should monitor disposition timing and eventual sale prices as new comparables for their own portfolios.
- Non-judicial foreclosure moves quickly once a notice of default issues; borrowers with maturing CMBS or balance-sheet loans secured by distressed office assets should engage workout counsel well before the statutory notice period runs, not after.
