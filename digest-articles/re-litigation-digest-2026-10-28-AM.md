# Appeals Court Revives a Costly Lesson for Commercial Landlords: No Notice, No Recovery

### New York's First Department affirms dismissal of a landlord's unpaid-rent and guaranty claims after it reentered and relet space without giving the tenant notice the lease required

A commercial landlord that skips the notice procedure its own lease demands can lose the right to collect rent for the balance of the term — even when the tenant left the space vacant before the lease was up. That is the holding of a decision issued October 27, 2026 by the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court of the State of New York, First Department, in *Renaissance Midtown W. LLC v. Lividini & Co., LLC*, which unanimously affirmed the dismissal of a landlord's amended complaint seeking rent, guaranty liability, and veil-piercing relief against a former office tenant and its principal.

The case traces back to a commercial lease between Renaissance Midtown West LLC, the landlord, and Lividini & Company, LLC, the tenant. According to the allegations recounted in the opinion, the tenant vacated the premises while the lease was still in effect; its counsel gave notice of the vacatur in August 2020. The landlord did not respond at the time. More than four years later, in November 2024, Renaissance Midtown West commenced an action in New York County Supreme Court seeking unpaid rent for the remainder of the lease term, enforcement of a guaranty against the tenant's principal, and an order piercing the corporate veil to reach that principal personally. The trial court, Justice Lyle E. Frank, granted the tenant's motion to dismiss the amended complaint in an order entered July 24, 2025. The landlord appealed, and the First Department has now affirmed across the board.

The appellate panel's analysis turned on a notice provision tucked into paragraph 17(1) of the parties' lease — a "conditional limitation" clause letting the landlord terminate for defaults other than nonpayment, including if "the demised premises becomes vacant or deserted," but only after serving a five-day notice to cure specifying the default, followed, if the tenant fails to cure, by a three-day notice of cancellation. The court held that the tenant's vacatur triggered this notice requirement before the landlord could reenter and relet the space, citing the New York Court of Appeals' decision in *Holy Props. v. Cole Prods.* The landlord argued it was not trying to terminate the lease under that provision but simply to collect rent still owed for the balance of the term. The court was unpersuaded: the landlord's own allegations — that the tenant returned the keys without objection and that the landlord then executed a new lease with a replacement tenant — were "at odds with the continuation of the landlord-tenant relationship." Separately, the panel noted that unlike the tenant in *622 Third Ave. Co. LLC v. Hyatt Leader Ltd.*, a case the landlord relied on, Renaissance Midtown West never told the departing tenant that its acceptance of the keys was "without prejudice" to a damages claim; after the tenant's August 2020 notice, there was no further communication between the parties until the lawsuit was filed in 2024.

Two other rulings in the decision are worth separate attention. First, the court rejected the guaranty claim because the lease's form guaranty — attached to the lease itself — was blank and unexecuted; under New York's statute of frauds for guaranties, General Obligations Law § 5-701(a)(2), an unsigned form does not bind anyone, no matter how strong the landlord's equitable argument. Second, the court rejected the attempt to pierce the corporate veil and reach the tenant's principal personally, holding that veil-piercing "is not a separately pleaded claim" but a fact pattern that must show the principal "exercised complete domination and control" over the entity "to perpetrate a wrong or injustice," per the Court of Appeals' 2018 decision in *Cortlandt St. Recovery Corp. v. Bonderman*. A bare allegation that the principal failed to keep a separate bank account, the panel held, does not get a plaintiff there.

For owners, asset managers, and in-house real estate counsel, the decision is a reminder that New York's conditional-limitation notice rules are enforced literally, regardless of a landlord's subjective intent. A landlord that accepts a vacating tenant's keys without expressly reserving its rights, and that fails to serve the lease's own cure-and-cancellation notices before reentering and reletting, risks forfeiting even a straightforward damages claim for the unexpired term — and risks it for years, since the defect surfaces only when the landlord finally sues. The ruling also underscores two drafting basics that are easy to let slip in a fast-moving lease negotiation: a guaranty attached as an exhibit is worthless unless it is actually signed, and veil-piercing against a tenant's principal demands far more than loose bookkeeping allegations.

**Practical takeaways:**
- Before reentering and reletting space a commercial tenant has vacated early, serve every notice the lease's default and cancellation provisions require — even if the landlord's actual goal is only to preserve a rent claim, not to terminate the lease.
- If accepting a tenant's surrender of keys, state in writing that acceptance is without prejudice to pursuing unpaid rent or other damages; silence can later be read as abandoning the landlord-tenant relationship altogether.
- Confirm that any guaranty referenced in or attached to a lease is actually executed before relying on it — an unsigned specimen form satisfies neither the parties' expectations nor New York's statute of frauds.
- A veil-piercing claim against a tenant's principal needs concrete allegations of domination and misuse of the corporate form to cause injury; generic claims about commingled funds or loose recordkeeping will not survive a motion to dismiss.
