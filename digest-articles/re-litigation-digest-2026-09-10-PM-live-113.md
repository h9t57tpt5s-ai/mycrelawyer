# Federal Court Rejects Landlords' Bid to Recast COVID Eviction Bans as a Physical Taking

### A Washington federal judge's dismissal of *Cedarland Homes LLC v. Ferguson* reaffirms that pandemic-era eviction moratoria are regulation, not condemnation — but the fight over compensation is headed to the Ninth Circuit.

More than five years after Washington State and a string of its cities imposed some of the nation's longest-running COVID-19 eviction moratoria, a federal judge in Seattle has closed the door — for now — on a landlord class action seeking compensation for the losses those moratoria caused. On August 5, 2026, U.S. District Judge Barbara J. Rothstein dismissed *Cedarland Homes LLC v. Ferguson*, rejecting the theory that eviction bans amounted to a physical taking of private property under the Fifth Amendment. The ruling matters well beyond Washington: it is one of the most fully litigated tests yet of whether the U.S. Supreme Court's 2021 decision in *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid* — which found that a regulation forcing landowners to admit outside parties onto their property can be a per se physical taking — extends to landlord-tenant regulation generally.

Cedarland Homes and fourteen other named plaintiffs, represented by Davis Wright Tremaine and lead counsel John DiLorenzo, filed the proposed class action on December 18, 2025, in Thurston County Superior Court before it was removed to the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Washington. The suit named Governor Bob Ferguson, the State of Washington, and eight cities and counties that layered their own local moratoria on top of the state's eviction ban, which ran from March 2020 to October 2022. The plaintiffs argued that the moratoria forced them to house nonpaying tenants — turning tenants into what the complaint called government-compelled occupants — while owners remained on the hook for property taxes, mortgage payments, insurance, and maintenance. Washington did create a landlord mitigation program to offset some of those losses, but the program capped reimbursement at $15,000 per unit, an amount the plaintiffs said fell far short of their actual damages.

Judge Rothstein's opinion turned on how far *Cedar Point Nursery* actually reaches. That case involved a California regulation granting union organizers a right of physical access to agricultural land — a straightforward, if temporary, physical invasion authorized by regulation. Rothstein held that an eviction moratorium is a different animal: it does not grant a third party a fresh right to enter or occupy property that wasn't already there under an existing lease, but instead restricts a landlord's remedies against a tenant who is already lawfully in possession. Citing Ninth Circuit precedent as well as prior Washington state court decisions rejecting physical-takings challenges to pandemic-era landlord-tenant rules, Rothstein concluded that moratoria of this kind are properly analyzed as economic regulation subject to the more forgiving *Penn Central* balancing test, not as a per se taking that requires compensation regardless of the government's interest or the burden imposed.

For commercial and residential property owners alike, the ruling is a reminder of how high a bar per se takings claims face when they are dressed up in the language of "compelled occupation." Plaintiffs' lawyers nationwide have tried to stretch *Cedar Point Nursery* to reach everything from rent control to short-term rental restrictions to eviction bans, betting that a Supreme Court sympathetic to property rights would extend the physical-takings framework into new territory. Rothstein's opinion — grounded in the distinction between granting new access rights and merely regulating an existing landlord-tenant relationship — represents one of the clearer judicial pushbacks against that strategy, and one that other courts weighing similar claims are likely to cite.

The litigation is not over. The Rental Housing Association of Washington, which backed the suit, has filed a notice of appeal to the Ninth Circuit and has signaled it will seek certiorari from the U.S. Supreme Court if the appeal fails, meaning the ultimate scope of *Cedar Point Nursery* in the landlord-tenant context remains an open question for at least another cycle of appellate review.

**Practical takeaways:**

- Physical-takings claims premised on landlord-tenant regulation continue to face a steep uphill climb after Rothstein's ruling; owners with pandemic-era eviction-moratorium losses should expect *Penn Central*-style regulatory-takings analysis, not automatic compensation, absent a further reversal on appeal.
- Property owners relying on state mitigation or rent-relief funds should document actual losses carefully now — the adequacy of a capped reimbursement program (here, $15,000 per unit) can become central to any future as-applied takings or due process claim even where a facial physical-takings theory fails.
- The case is headed to the Ninth Circuit, and ultimately possibly the Supreme Court; CRE owners, REITs with residential exposure, and landlord associations in other jurisdictions with emergency eviction or rent-freeze measures should monitor the appeal closely before relying on *Cedar Point Nursery* as a template for their own takings claims.
