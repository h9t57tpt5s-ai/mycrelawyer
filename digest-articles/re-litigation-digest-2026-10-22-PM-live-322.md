# California Appeals Court Wipes Out $12.2M Groundwater Contamination Judgment Against Industrial Property Owners

### A consultant's own report blamed the upgradient owner for the plume — but the Second District says that's not enough to win summary judgment on duty, nuisance, or trespass

A California appeals court has reversed a $12,245,108.42 judgment against the current and former owners of a Los Angeles-area industrial site, holding that a downgradient neighbor's reliance on an environmental consultant's contamination report was not enough to establish liability as a matter of law. The decision, *Joseph F. Farivar Investment Group, Inc. v. HPC Vineburn, LLC* (Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District, Division One, Case No. B343432), unwinds a trial court judgment that had declared the owners of 1919 Vineburn Avenue in Los Angeles responsible for a plume of 1,4-dioxane and related solvents that migrated onto the adjacent, lower-lying property at 4335 Valley Boulevard.

The dispute traces back decades. Before 1989, the Nardon Manufacturing Company operated a metal-fabrication facility at the Vineburn property, discharging 1,4-dioxane and other industrial solvents into onsite clarifiers and, according to the complaint, into the municipal sewer system. Meruelo Maddux Properties-1919 Vineburn Street, LLC owned the property from roughly 2005 to 2013, after which HPC Vineburn, LLC took over. The Los Angeles Regional Water Quality Control Board has overseen cleanup of a related site — the "Former Square D Site" — located on the downgradient Farivar property since 2000. In 2018, the Water Board directed Vineburn to investigate further, and a 2020 subsurface report by Vineburn's own consultant, Hazard Management Consulting, concluded that contamination at the Nardon facility had "contributed" to the dioxane and solvent plume beneath the Farivar property, identifying a contamination "hot-spot" near a former wastewater clarifier.

Plaintiffs Joseph F. Farivar Investment Group, Inc., which owns the Valley Boulevard property, and Bordan Shoe Company, Inc., which leases it, sued for continuing nuisance, continuing trespass, negligence (including negligence per se), and declaratory relief. Leaning heavily on the 2020 consultant report and on experts who put the cost of remediating the Farivar property at roughly $11.78 million and the loss-of-use damages at just under $193,000, the plaintiffs moved for — and won — summary adjudication on all four causes of action. After dismissing their remaining claims, they obtained a judgment for more than $12.2 million, along with a declaration that the defendants were responsible for the contamination and its remediation.

The Court of Appeal reversed across the board. On negligence, the panel held that the plaintiffs failed to establish, as a matter of law, that the defendants breached any common-law or statutory duty of care — the consultant's factual findings about where contamination originated did not, by themselves, resolve the separate legal question of duty. On continuing nuisance and continuing trespass, the court found the plaintiffs had used an "improper damages measure" for what are, by their nature, continuing torts, meaning the record did not support summary adjudication on those theories either. And on the declaratory relief claim, the court held the trial court erred in partially adjudicating the issue by summary procedure. The result: the entire $12.2 million judgment is gone, and the case returns to the Los Angeles County Superior Court (Hon. Rolf M. Treu) for further proceedings.

For owners, tenants, and lenders touching legacy-contamination sites, the ruling is a reminder that an adverse consultant report — even one commissioned by the defendant itself in response to a regulatory directive — does not automatically translate into civil liability. Environmental compliance obligations owed to a water board, and tort liability owed to a private neighbor, run on separate tracks with separate elements, and a plaintiff who treats them as interchangeable risks exactly what happened here: a judgment that looks airtight at the trial level but doesn't survive the duty and damages analysis on appeal.

The case also underscores how contamination liability can attach across a chain of ownership. HPC Vineburn did not create the contamination — it inherited the site, and the site's problems, from Meruelo Maddux, which in turn inherited them from the original manufacturer. Successor owners of industrial and former-industrial parcels remain exposed to continuing-tort theories brought by neighbors long after the polluting operations have ceased, even where the current owner has, as the opinion notes, set aside funds and proposed its own cleanup plan.

Practical takeaways for commercial owners, lenders, and counsel: first, don't assume a consultant's causation finding settles the legal question of duty — negligence still requires proof of a breached standard of care, and summary judgment is a demanding vehicle for getting there. Second, continuing nuisance and continuing trespass claims demand the right damages framework; getting the measure wrong can doom an otherwise well-supported claim even where the underlying facts are largely undisputed. Third, buyers of industrial and formerly industrial property should scrutinize upgradient and downgradient parcels in diligence, since successor liability for legacy contamination can persist for decades and migrate with groundwater regardless of who is currently on title. Finally, tenants in industrial and flex space with any exposure to a landlord's environmental history should track litigation like this closely — the outcome of the landlord's contamination dispute can directly affect occupancy costs, remediation access, and lease continuity.
