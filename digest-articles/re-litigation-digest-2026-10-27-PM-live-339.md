# North Carolina Judge Rules Data Center Developer's Vested Rights Trump County's Moratorium

### A Chatham County summary judgment handed to ECO TIP West is an early signal for the roughly 30 North Carolina local governments that have passed similar data-center moratoria

A North Carolina trial judge has ruled that a data center developer's project can proceed despite a county-wide moratorium, in a decision that offers one of the first clear answers to a legal question now pending in dozens of jurisdictions across the state: does a developer who secured zoning approval before a moratorium took effect have a vested right to keep building anyway? In a summary judgment order reported September 22, 2026, Chatham County Superior Court Judge John Morris sided decisively with the developer, ECO TIP West, LLC, finding no genuine issue of material fact and ruling that the company was entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

ECO TIP West sued Chatham County in its own Superior Court on April 23, 2026, after county commissioners adopted a 12-month moratorium on new data-center development on February 11, 2026. The moratorium, which by its terms runs until February 11, 2027, or until the county adopts new zoning regulations specific to data centers, whichever comes first, was part of a broader statewide reaction to a surge of large-scale data-center proposals; North Carolina news organizations have tracked roughly 30 local governments statewide that have passed comparable moratoria. ECO TIP West's complaint, however, argued that its planned 750-megawatt data center at the Triangle Innovation Point West parcel near Moncure — part of the sprawling Moncure Megasite in Chatham County — was different: the company says it received a zoning permit for the project from the county on October 29, 2025, more than three months before the moratorium existed, giving it a statutory "by-right use" the county could not retroactively strip away.

The suit invoked North Carolina's Chapter 160D, the state's consolidated local planning and development regulation statute, along with the vested-rights provisions of N.C. Gen. Stat. § 160D-107(c) and the North Carolina Constitution. ECO TIP West sought a declaratory judgment confirming its exemption from the moratorium, along with compensatory damages and attorney's fees for the delay the moratorium had already caused. According to reporting on Judge Morris's order, the court agreed on the merits, ruling that the project could proceed and that Chatham County must pay the developer damages for the delay, though public reporting has not yet disclosed the specific dollar figure awarded or the full text of the court's underlying reasoning. The company has said it had already invested more than $11 million developing the project before the county intervened.

### Why a single Superior Court ruling matters to a much larger fight

Data-center moratoria have become one of the fastest-growing categories of commercial land-use litigation in the country over the past two years, as local governments nationwide — from Ohio and Michigan to Georgia and Kentucky — have reached for temporary building pauses to buy time against resident opposition over noise, water consumption, and grid strain from gigawatt-scale computing campuses. The recurring legal question in nearly every one of these disputes is the same one Chatham County lost: whether a developer that obtained permits, zoning approval, or other governmental sign-off before a moratorium's effective date holds a vested right the moratorium cannot touch.

Judge Morris's ruling does not resolve that question nationally, or even for every project in North Carolina — vested-rights analysis is intensely fact-specific, turning on exactly what permit or approval a developer held and exactly when it held it, under each state's own statutory framework. But as one of the first North Carolina trial court rulings to address the issue head-on, with a clear win for a developer that could document a permit predating the moratorium by several months, it is likely to embolden other developers with similarly timed approvals to challenge their own county's or municipality's data-center pause, while giving counties reason to scrutinize more carefully the permitting status of any project before adopting a moratorium meant to cover it.

It is also notable that the court did not merely exempt ECO TIP West's project from the moratorium; it ordered the county to pay damages for the delay the moratorium caused, a remedy that raises the financial stakes of adopting a moratorium that a court later finds improperly swept in an already-vested project. For the roughly thirty other North Carolina local governments with their own data-center moratoria on the books, and for similarly situated jurisdictions nationally, the decision is a concrete reminder that a facially neutral pause is not necessarily a safe harbor if a specific project had already cleared the regulatory bar the moratorium was meant to raise.

### Practical takeaways

- A data-center developer that has obtained a zoning permit or comparable approval before a moratorium's effective date should be prepared to document that timing precisely, since it is likely to be the single most important fact in any vested-rights challenge to the moratorium.
- Local governments drafting a data-center moratorium should inventory pending and recently approved applications before adopting the measure, since a court may later find that a specific, already-permitted project was improperly swept into a pause meant for future applicants — and may order damages for the resulting delay, not just an exemption going forward.
- Both developers and local governments nationally should track the growing body of data-center moratorium rulings coming out of North Carolina, Ohio, Michigan, Georgia, and Kentucky as a single, fast-developing area of land-use law, since courts in one state's vested-rights dispute are likely to be cited as persuasive authority in another's.
