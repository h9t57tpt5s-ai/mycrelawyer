Q4 2026 is just underway

This report turns over to Q4 2026 on October 1 with the quarter's own docket still essentially empty. CREdocket synthesizes this report by the date of the underlying legal event — a filing, ruling, or development — not the date a matter is added to the tracker, so a case added today but arising from a late-September filing or ruling still counts toward Q3, not Q4. This page will build back out section by section, the same way the prior quarter's report grew from a single theme in its early days to a multi-theme synthesis by its end, as real matters get dated into October, November, and December.

The quarter's first dated development is a ruling out of the District of Colorado: on October 1, Judge Regina M. Rodriguez allowed Colorado Apartment Association v. Ryan to proceed past a renewed motion to dismiss, keeping alive the real estate industry's federal-preemption challenge to Denver's and Colorado's building-emissions mandates — with the U.S. Department of Justice having weighed in on the industry's side days earlier. It is an early signal of the zoning and regulatory-compliance litigation likely to recur through Q4 as more cities adopt building-performance standards.

Several of the themes that defined Q3 — a refinancing wall that reached institutional-grade collateral by quarter's end, REIT governance disputes drawing scrutiny from courts, the SEC, and jilted merger partners, and algorithmic rent-pricing litigation expanding alongside a new wave of state and local bans — involve cases that are still active and likely to generate Q4 developments of their own. Check back as the quarter develops, or browse every matter CREdocket has tracked to date on the full Litigation Tracker.

A second early Q4 matter, set for jury trial on October 12, is a reminder that municipal land-use and economic-development disputes can simmer for years before reaching a courtroom: in Tutelian v. City of Fresno, a downtown Fresno developer is suing the city and a city councilmember over a decade-old parking commitment he says the city abandoned in favor of its own competing garage project, with more than $7 million in claimed losses at stake if his anchor tenant leaves as a result.

A third early Q4 development is procedural rather than substantive, but practically significant for anyone negotiating a real estate settlement by email: on October 1, the Massachusetts Appeals Court held in Zammuto v. Damianidis that a settlement reached almost entirely through email exchanges between counsel was binding, ordering $100,000 paid even after the paying party tried to walk away before every signature was in hand. Litigators structuring multi-party settlements around a dependent transaction should take note.

A fourth early Q4 matter raises the financial stakes on municipal delay itself: on October 2, developer N17 Development sued the City of Menlo Park in N17 Development v. City of Menlo Park, alleging the city strung out review of its 665-unit Willow Park towers through shifting objections rather than a timely approval or denial. Because Menlo Park had already received a warning letter from the California Attorney General before suit was filed, a loss could trigger statutory penalties of up to $33 million — a test of how far procedural delay alone can expose a city under the state's housing-accountability law.
